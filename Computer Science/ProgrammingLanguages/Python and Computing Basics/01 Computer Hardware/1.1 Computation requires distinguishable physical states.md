# 1.1 Computation requires distinguishable physical states

A **computer** is a physical system arranged to represent information and to transform those representations according to rules. This definition has three parts. The system is physical: its operation is realised by matter and energy, not by mathematical symbols floating independently of a machine. It represents information: physical conditions are made to stand for values under an agreed interpretation. It transforms representations: controlled physical changes implement operations. A laptop, a server, and a pocket calculator differ radically in scale and purpose, but each satisfies this description.

An **information state** is a distinguishable condition used to represent one possibility rather than another. A mark on paper, a voltage range in a wire, the orientation of a magnetic region, and the presence or absence of light can all serve as information states. The physical condition has no intrinsic computational meaning. A high voltage does not contain the abstract number one. Engineers establish a convention under which one stable range is interpreted as one and another stable range as zero. The convention joins a physical carrier to an abstract symbol.

The word **abstract** means considered apart from a particular physical realisation. The number five is abstract: it is neither the five dots on a die nor the character `5` printed on a page. Those are representations of five. A **representation** is a physical or symbolic arrangement interpreted as standing for something else. One value may have several representations, and one pattern may acquire several meanings under different interpretations. The bit pattern `01000001`, for example, can represent the integer 65 under an unsigned binary convention or the capital letter A under the ASCII text-encoding convention introduced later. The bits alone do not choose.

Physical systems are continuously variable and exposed to disturbance. Voltage does not arrive as a metaphysically exact 0 volts or 5 volts; it fluctuates because of temperature, electromagnetic interference, component variation, and the finite behaviour of matter. Digital machines manage this analogue reality by assigning ranges of physical conditions to a small number of logical states. If voltages from 0 to 0.8 volts count as logical zero and voltages from 2 to 5 volts count as logical one, small disturbances inside either range do not change the interpreted state. The unused interval between the ranges contributes a **noise margin**: a tolerance within which a signal may vary without being mistaken for the other logical value.

This is the first reason binary representation is useful. A **binary** system has two alternatives. Two well-separated ranges are easier to construct, restore, copy, and distinguish reliably than a large number of narrowly separated ranges. Binary is not the only conceivable basis for computation, nor is it selected because nature contains only two conditions. It is an engineering agreement that composes particularly well from reliable switches.

### Worked example 1.1 — Representation is a contract

Suppose a sensor reports a voltage and a receiver uses this contract:

| Voltage | Interpretation |
|---:|:---|
| 0.0–0.8 V | logical 0 |
| 0.8–2.0 V | invalid or indeterminate |
| 2.0–5.0 V | logical 1 |

A reading of 0.15 V represents 0 under the contract. A reading of 4.74 V represents 1. A reading of 1.30 V has no licensed interpretation. Calling the last reading “probably 1” would silently replace the contract with a different rule. The correct response is to classify it as outside the promised ranges and investigate the circuit or sampling conditions.

Three separations follow.

1. The voltage is the physical condition.
2. `0` or `1` is the logical symbol assigned by the interpretation rule.
3. The meaning later attached to that symbol—part of a number, character, instruction, image, or address—belongs to another contract.

No later software abstraction cancels these separations. It hides them behind a stable interface.

> **Common misconception — “Computers understand zeros and ones.”**  
> A computer does not understand written digits. Circuits occupy physical states; engineers interpret ranges of those states as binary symbols. “Zero” and “one” are concise names in the model used to design and reason about the machine.
