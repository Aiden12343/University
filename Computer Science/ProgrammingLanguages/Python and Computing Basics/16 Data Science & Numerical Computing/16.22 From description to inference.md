# 16.22 From description to inference

**Statistical inference** uses sample data and a probability model to reason about an unknown population feature or future outcome. An **estimand** is the precise quantity the analysis aims to estimate. An **estimator** is a rule mapping possible samples to estimates. An **estimate** is the value produced for the observed sample.

For example:

- estimand: mean latency over all valid requests produced by a defined service population next week;
- estimator: sample mean of requests selected by a stated random-sampling design;
- estimate: 92.4 milliseconds from the realised sample.

Without an estimand, accuracy cannot be defined. A mean over observed successful requests may estimate something quite different from a mean over all attempted requests.

A **sampling distribution** is the distribution of a statistic across hypothetical repetitions of the sampling procedure. The **standard error** is the standard deviation of that sampling distribution. It measures estimator variability under assumptions; it is not the sample standard deviation of observations.

A **confidence interval** is an interval produced by a procedure with a stated long-run coverage property under a model. A 95% confidence procedure yields intervals containing the fixed parameter in 95% of repeated samples under its assumptions. After one interval is calculated in a conventional frequentist analysis, the parameter is not treated as randomly moving between intervals. Saying “there is a 95% probability that this fixed parameter lies in this realised interval” changes the interpretation unless a Bayesian model supplies a parameter distribution.

### Worked example 16.13 — Bootstrap uncertainty

The **bootstrap** approximates a sampling distribution by repeatedly resampling observed units with replacement. It is computationally convenient but inherits the representativeness and dependence structure of the data.

~~~python
import numpy as np

values = np.array([83.0, 107.0, 91.0, 95.0, 88.0])
rng = np.random.default_rng(41)

replicates = np.empty(10_000)
for index in range(replicates.size):
    resample = rng.choice(values, size=values.size, replace=True)
    replicates[index] = resample.mean()

lower, upper = np.quantile(replicates, [0.025, 0.975])
print(values.mean(), lower, upper)
~~~

Each resample contains five selections from the five observed values, allowing repetition. The percentile interval uses quantiles of bootstrap means. The code demonstrates mechanics, not universal validity. Five observations provide scant information; ordinary resampling assumes units are exchangeable; clustered or serial data require a bootstrap that preserves dependence; and percentile intervals have coverage limitations. A computational procedure cannot manufacture information absent from the study design.

### 16.22.1 Estimator properties and repeated-sampling reasoning

Let (	heta) denote an estimand and (hat{\theta}) an estimator computed from a random sample. Its **bias** is

\[
\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\theta})
=\operatorname{E}[\hat{\theta}]-\theta.
\]

Its variance describes dispersion of (hat{\theta}) across repeated samples. Under squared-error loss, the **mean squared error** decomposes as

\[
\operatorname{MSE}(\hat{\theta})
=\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta})
+\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2.
\]

An unbiased estimator is not automatically preferable: a small controlled bias may accompany a much larger variance reduction and hence lower mean squared error. Conversely, low variance around the wrong target gives a consistently wrong answer. The appropriate criterion follows from the estimand and loss.

**Consistency** means, roughly, that an estimator approaches the target as information grows under a specified asymptotic regime. It does not guarantee good finite-sample behaviour. **Efficiency** compares variability among estimators under a model. **Robustness** concerns sensitivity to deviations, contamination, or perturbations. These are distinct virtues; no single estimator optimises them universally.

Sampling design enters the estimator. If units have unequal inclusion probabilities, an unweighted mean of observed units generally targets the realised sample rather than the population mean. A design weight proportional to inverse inclusion probability can restore a population interpretation under conditions, while non-response and calibration introduce further assumptions.

~~~python
import numpy as np

values = np.array([10.0, 20.0, 80.0])
inclusion_probabilities = np.array([0.8, 0.8, 0.2])
weights = 1.0 / inclusion_probabilities

unweighted = values.mean()
weighted = np.average(values, weights=weights)
print(unweighted, weighted)
~~~

The weighted result gives the rarely sampled third type more influence. It is not inherently “fairer”: it estimates a target under the claimed inclusion mechanism. Survey variance must also reflect stratification, clustering, finite-population correction, and weight estimation where relevant.

> **Common misconception 16.61 — “An estimate is a property of the observed numbers alone.”**  
> Its interpretation depends on the estimand, unit, sampling and observation processes, estimator, weights, exclusions, and probability assumptions. The same values can support different targets under different designs.

### 16.22.2 Standard errors, dependence, and effective information

For independent identically distributed observations with finite variance (sigma^2), the sample mean has variance (sigma^2/n). Repeated observations from the same person, location, household, batch, or time sequence are commonly dependent. Treating them as (n) independent units exaggerates information.

In a simple equal-size cluster model with cluster size (m) and within-cluster correlation (
ho), a **design effect** is approximately

\[
1+(m-1)\rho.
\]

The effective sample size relative to simple independent sampling is roughly the nominal count divided by this factor. Ten thousand measurements from ten sensors do not necessarily contain the inferential information of ten thousand independent sensors.

Standard-error estimators can be model-based, heteroskedasticity-robust, cluster-robust, design-based, block-bootstrap, or otherwise structured. Each answers under assumptions. “Robust standard error” is incomplete without naming what dependence or misspecification it is robust against and under what asymptotics.

The ordinary nonparametric bootstrap samples observational units independently from the empirical distribution. If the data consist of patient-level clusters, resample patients with their records; if paired measurements define units, resample pairs; for dependent time series, a block method may preserve local dependence. Resampling individual rows destroys these structures.

A **parametric bootstrap** simulates from a fitted probability model rather than directly from observed units. It can represent structures that a tiny empirical sample cannot resample richly, but transfers all relevant model assumptions into the uncertainty estimate. A **permutation test** instead rearranges labels under an exchangeability null; it is not synonymous with bootstrap resampling.

Monte Carlo repetitions add numerical approximation error to a bootstrap or permutation result. If estimating a tail probability near 0.05 from only 100 random permutations, the numerical granularity and variability are too large for fine distinctions. Report repetition count, random-stream design, and Monte Carlo error where it affects conclusions.

> **Common misconception 16.62 — “Resampling automatically handles whatever dependence the data contain.”**  
> A resampling scheme encodes its own exchangeability units. Row-wise bootstrap, cluster bootstrap, block bootstrap, paired bootstrap, and permutation answer under different structures; choosing the wrong unit can destroy the dependence that determines uncertainty.

### 16.22.3 Interval procedures and their targets

A confidence interval concerns a parameter or estimand. A **prediction interval** concerns a future observation and ordinarily includes both parameter uncertainty and irreducible outcome variation. A **tolerance interval** aims to cover a stated fraction of a population. A Bayesian **credible interval** contains a stated posterior probability under a prior and likelihood. Similar endpoints do not make their interpretations interchangeable.

The familiar normal-approximation interval

\[
\hat{\theta}\ \pm\ z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\hat{\theta})
\]

relies on approximate sampling normality, a standard-error estimator, and regularity. It can perform poorly for bounded parameters near a boundary, rare counts, skewed estimators, small samples, weak identification, or data-dependent model selection. An exact-looking decimal endpoint does not certify coverage.

Coverage is conditional on the full procedure. If analysts try many models and report the narrowest interval, standard nominal coverage need not survive. If a stopping rule depends on accumulating results, the procedure must account for it. If the sample misses part of the target population, a precise interval can tightly quantify the wrong population.

~~~python
from math import sqrt

from scipy.stats import norm

successes = 42
trials = 100
estimate = successes / trials
z = norm.ppf(0.975)

# Wilson score interval for a binomial proportion.
denominator = 1.0 + z * z / trials
centre = (estimate + z * z / (2 * trials)) / denominator
half_width = (
    z
    * sqrt(estimate * (1 - estimate) / trials + z * z / (4 * trials * trials))
    / denominator
)
print(centre - half_width, centre + half_width)
~~~

The Wilson interval avoids some severe defects of the naive proportion ± normal standard error interval. It still assumes a binomial sampling model and independent trials with a common probability. Clustered or changing probabilities require another procedure.

Interval width communicates resolution, not truth. A narrow interval can result from an enormous biased sample; a wide interval can honestly reflect limited information. Report the estimate, interval type, confidence or credibility level, unit, model, and sample/design details. Avoid interpreting overlap or non-overlap of two separate intervals as a universal significance test for their difference.

> **Common misconception 16.63 — “A 95% interval means the method is 95% likely to be correct.”**  
> Coverage is a specific repeated-procedure property under assumptions, not a general probability of correctness. It does not include unmodelled selection, measurement, coding, or causal errors.
