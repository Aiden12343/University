# 16.23 Hypotheses, tests, and effect sizes

A **statistical hypothesis** is a claim about a probability model or parameter. A **null hypothesis** identifies a reference claim used to derive a test statistic’s distribution. A **p-value** is, under that null model, the probability of obtaining a test statistic at least as incompatible with the null as the observed statistic, according to the predeclared direction and procedure.

A p-value is not:

- the probability that the null hypothesis is true;
- the probability that the result arose “by chance”;
- the probability that replication will fail;
- a measure of practical importance.

A **significance level** is a preselected rule for limiting a class of false rejections under repeated use. Choosing it after seeing the p-value defeats its error-control interpretation. Repeated testing, optional stopping, outcome switching, and selective reporting alter error rates.

An **effect size** describes the magnitude of a difference or association in substantively meaningful or standardised units. A tiny effect can be estimated very precisely in a large sample and yield a small p-value; a large important effect can remain uncertain in a small sample. Report magnitude and uncertainty, not a binary significant/non-significant verdict.

### 16.23.1 A test is a calibrated decision rule under a model

A hypothesis test begins with a sample space, null-model family, statistic, direction, and rejection rule. The **test statistic** compresses the data into a value whose distribution under the null can be derived or approximated. “At least as extreme” is meaningless until extremeness and one- or two-sided direction are specified.

A **Type I error** is rejection of the null when the relevant null model is true. A **Type II error** is failure to reject when a specified alternative is true. The **power** at an alternative is the probability of rejection under that alternative. A significance level (alpha) controls Type I error only for the complete predeclared procedure and assumptions; it does not promise that (alpha) of published rejections are false.

Power depends on effect magnitude, variability, sample size, design, missingness, test, and significance threshold. There is no study that is simply “80% powered” without an alternative and design. Post hoc power computed by inserting the observed effect into the original formula is largely a transformation of the p-value and supplies little new information; interval estimates and design sensitivity are more useful.

### Worked example 16.14 — A paired comparison preserves the unit

Suppose the same devices are measured before and after a firmware update. The scientific units are devices, and the analysis should use within-device differences rather than treat the two columns as independent samples.

~~~python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

before = np.array([102.0, 98.0, 110.0, 95.0, 105.0, 100.0])
after = np.array([97.0, 96.0, 104.0, 94.0, 101.0, 98.0])
differences = after - before

result = stats.ttest_rel(after, before)
mean_change = differences.mean()
standard_error = differences.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(differences.size)

print(f"mean paired change: {mean_change:.3f} ms")
print(f"SE: {standard_error:.3f} ms")
print(f"t statistic: {result.statistic:.3f}")
print(f"two-sided p-value: {result.pvalue:.4f}")
~~~

The paired t procedure assumes the device-level differences form an appropriate independent sample from a population with a mean and sufficiently regular distribution for its finite-sample or approximate calibration. It does not require before and after readings separately to be identically distributed, but outlying differences matter strongly in six units. The mean change and its uncertainty carry units and should lead the report.

Failure to reject is not acceptance of exact equality. It means the statistic did not cross the chosen threshold under this design and model. The data may be compatible with a wide range of important effects.

> **Common misconception 16.64 — “A p-value is the probability that random chance produced the result.”**  
> It is a tail probability for a statistic under a specified null procedure, conditional on assumptions and analysis choices. It neither assigns probability to the null nor partitions causation into “chance” and “real”.

### 16.23.2 Multiplicity, selection, and false discoveries

If (m) independent true-null tests each use level 0.05, the probability of at least one false rejection is

\[
1-(1-0.05)^m,
\]

which is about 0.401 for ten tests. Dependence changes the calculation but not the selection problem. **Family-wise error rate** is the probability of at least one false rejection in a declared family. Bonferroni controls it by testing each at (alpha/m) without requiring independence, though it can be conservative. Stepwise procedures such as Holm’s can improve power while controlling the same family-wise criterion.

The **false discovery rate** is the expected proportion of false rejections among rejections under a defined procedure, with a convention when none are rejected. Benjamini–Hochberg orders p-values and controls FDR under stated dependence conditions. FDR control does not mean that every reported discovery has the same probability of being false, nor does it repair biased studies.

The family is defined by the scientific decision process, not one code cell. Trying outcomes, subgroups, transformations, exclusion rules, models, and stopping times expands the effective search. Reporting only the path that “worked” causes selective-inference bias even if each individual call was correct. **Preregistration** records hypotheses and analysis decisions before observing outcomes; it distinguishes confirmatory from exploratory work but does not make a poor design sound.

~~~python
import numpy as np


def benjamini_hochberg(p_values: np.ndarray, q: float) -> np.ndarray:
    p = np.asarray(p_values, dtype=float)
    if p.ndim != 1 or np.any((p < 0) | (p > 1)):
        raise ValueError("p-values must be a one-dimensional array in [0, 1]")
    order = np.argsort(p)
    ranked = p[order]
    thresholds = q * np.arange(1, p.size + 1) / p.size
    passing = np.flatnonzero(ranked <= thresholds)
    rejected = np.zeros(p.size, dtype=bool)
    if passing.size:
        cutoff_rank = passing[-1]
        rejected[order[: cutoff_rank + 1]] = True
    return rejected


p_values = np.array([0.001, 0.011, 0.030, 0.20, 0.80])
print(benjamini_hochberg(p_values, q=0.05))
~~~

This compact implementation illustrates the step-up rule but is not a substitute for a vetted statistical package supporting adjusted p-values, missing values, dependence variants, and documented conventions. The ordered algorithm rejects all hypotheses through the largest passing rank, not merely each p-value that individually lies below its own rank threshold.

> **Common misconception 16.65 — “Correcting the p-values shown in one table solves multiple testing.”**  
> Multiplicity arises from the full set of data-dependent opportunities to select a claim, including unreported outcomes and models. A correction needs a declared family or discovery process and cannot recover hidden analyses.

### 16.23.3 Practical thresholds, equivalence, and interval evidence

A **minimum practically important difference** is a domain threshold below which an effect would not change interpretation or action. It should be specified from consequences and measurement resolution, not reverse-engineered from observed data. Confidence intervals can then distinguish several states: evidence compatible only with negligible effects, compatible with both negligible and important effects, or excluding negligible effects under the procedure.

An **equivalence test** asks whether the effect is sufficiently contained inside a predeclared equivalence region, often using two one-sided tests. It reverses the usual burden: the null includes effects at or beyond the equivalence bounds. A **non-inferiority test** asks whether a new intervention is no worse than a comparator by more than a specified margin. Its direction, analysis population, adherence assumptions, and margin require domain justification.

Statistical standardisation can obscure practical meaning. Cohen-like standardised mean differences divide by a standard deviation, so their magnitude changes when population heterogeneity changes even if the physical-unit effect does not. Risk ratios, odds ratios, risk differences, number needed to treat, elasticities, and correlations answer different questions and have different baselines. Report an effect in natural units whenever possible, with standardisation as an additional comparison aid.

A non-significant difference between group A and zero and a significant difference between group B and zero do not prove A and B differ. The relevant analysis directly estimates (A-B). Similarly, “no interaction p-value” does not establish homogeneous effects, especially with low subgroup power.

Model assumptions should be diagnosed in relation to the inferential target. Mechanical normality tests on raw outcomes can reject harmless deviations in large samples or miss consequential ones in small samples. Residual structure, dependence, leverage, measurement process, and study design matter more than a ritual checklist. When assumptions are doubtful, use alternative estimators, robust uncertainty methods, transformations, randomisation inference, or sensitivity analyses—and state the target each one changes or preserves.

> **Common misconception 16.66 — “Not statistically significant means equivalent, unimportant, or no effect.”**  
> Failure to reject can reflect low information or an imprecise estimate. Equivalence and non-inferiority require their own predeclared margins, procedures, and adequate precision.
