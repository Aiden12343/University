# 16.24 Correlation, prediction, and causation

**Covariance** measures joint variation around means:

\[
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\operatorname{E}[(X-\operatorname{E}X)(Y-\operatorname{E}Y)].
\]

The Pearson **correlation coefficient** divides covariance by the product of standard deviations, producing a dimensionless value between \(-1\) and \(1\) when variances are positive. It describes linear association. Zero correlation does not imply independence except under additional distributional assumptions; a strong nonlinear relationship can have zero linear correlation.

**Causation** means that intervening on one quantity would change another under a defined causal model. Observed association can arise from direct causation, reverse causation, a common cause, selection, measurement artefact, or chance. A **confounder** is a variable related to both an exposure and outcome in a manner that distorts an unadjusted causal comparison.

Randomised assignment can, under proper execution and analysis, balance pre-treatment causes in expectation and identify specified causal effects. Observational adjustment requires causal assumptions that data alone cannot verify. Machine-learning predictive accuracy does not by itself establish causation: a model can predict rain from umbrellas without umbrellas causing rain.

### 16.24.1 Association depends on scale, population, and aggregation

Covariance has product units and changes with rescaling. Pearson correlation standardises by marginal standard deviations, but remains sensitive to outliers, range restriction, mixtures of groups, and nonlinear form. Spearman rank correlation measures monotone association through ranks; Kendall’s coefficient uses concordant and discordant pairs. They do not become causal by being “nonparametric”.

~~~python
import numpy as np

x = np.array([-2.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 2.0])
y = x * x

correlation = np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]
assert abs(correlation) < 1e-12
~~~

Here (Y=X^2) is completely determined by (X), yet symmetric curvature gives zero Pearson correlation. Always inspect the joint distribution and state the form of association sought. A correlation matrix also silently uses a missing-data policy; pairwise deletion can make different entries refer to different subsets.

**Simpson’s paradox** describes a reversal or substantial change between aggregated and stratified associations. It is not a numerical curiosity: aggregation changes the comparison by mixing groups with different baselines and compositions.

~~~python
import pandas as pd

data = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "group": ["easy", "easy", "hard", "hard"],
        "method": ["A", "B", "A", "B"],
        "successes": [81, 234, 192, 55],
        "attempts": [87, 270, 263, 80],
    }
)
data["rate"] = data["successes"] / data["attempts"]
within = data.pivot(index="group", columns="method", values="rate")
overall = data.groupby("method")[["successes", "attempts"]].sum()
overall["rate"] = overall["successes"] / overall["attempts"]
print(within)
print(overall["rate"])
~~~

The method comparison within each difficulty stratum can differ from the aggregate because method use is distributed differently across strata. Which comparison is relevant depends on the causal or descriptive question. Blind adjustment for every available variable is not the solution: some variables are consequences, mediators, or selection colliders.

Correlation estimates also suffer **ecological fallacy** when group-level association is attributed to individuals, and **atomistic fallacy** when individual patterns are attributed to groups. Changing the unit of analysis changes the joint distribution. A time-trending pair of unrelated series can have high correlation through common trend; differencing or modelling time may change the estimand rather than simply “remove spurious correlation”.

> **Common misconception 16.67 — “Correlation is a stable property of two named variables.”**  
> It belongs to variables as measured in a specified population, range, time, scale, and missing-data regime. Aggregation, outliers, subgroup composition, transformations, and nonlinear structure can alter or erase it.

### 16.24.2 Causal questions require counterfactual structure

For a binary treatment (A), the **potential outcomes** (Y(1)) and (Y(0)) denote the outcome a unit would have under treatment and control under a causal model. The individual causal effect (Y(1)-Y(0)) cannot ordinarily be observed because one unit receives one condition. Population estimands such as the **average treatment effect** are averages of this unobserved contrast. Identification asks whether observed data plus assumptions determine the estimand; estimation begins only after identification.

A **directed acyclic graph** (DAG) represents assumed causal directions without directed cycles. An arrow (X\to Y) asserts that (X) is a direct cause of (Y) relative to variables included in the graph. DAGs help reason about open and blocked paths:

- a common cause (C\to A) and (C\to Y) creates a back-door path that may require adjustment;
- a mediator (A\to M\to Y) carries part of the total effect, so adjusting for it changes the target;
- a collider (A\to S\leftarrow Y) blocks a path when unconditioned, but selecting or adjusting for (S) can create association;
- a proxy may measure a confounder imperfectly and leave residual confounding.

The graph is not learned conclusively from a correlation matrix. It encodes temporal, substantive, and design knowledge open to criticism. Different causally plausible graphs can imply different adjustment sets while fitting the same observational distribution.

Randomisation makes treatment assignment independent of pre-treatment potential outcomes in expectation, supporting identification of an intention-to-treat effect under consistency, interference, attrition, and measurement conditions. It does not ensure perfect balance in one finite sample; does not repair non-compliance or differential missingness automatically; and does not identify effects in unrepresented populations without transport assumptions.

Observational identification commonly invokes:

- **exchangeability** conditional on measured covariates: no remaining common causes of treatment and outcome after adjustment;
- **positivity**: each relevant covariate pattern has a non-zero probability of each treatment condition being compared;
- **consistency**: a unit’s observed outcome under its received treatment corresponds to the defined potential outcome;
- a version of **no interference**: one unit’s outcome is not affected by other units’ assignments, unless such effects are explicitly modelled.

These are not pandas validation rules. Positivity can be probed empirically but is also population-relative; unmeasured confounding cannot be ruled out from the analysis table alone. Sensitivity analysis quantifies how strong a violation would need to be to alter a conclusion.

Prediction conditions on associations useful at deployment. Causal estimation asks about intervention. A highly predictive postcode may proxy structural inequality while being unsuitable as an intervention target. A variable can improve prediction and worsen causal adjustment if it is a collider. Conversely, a randomised treatment effect can be identifiable even when individual outcomes are poorly predictable.

Temporal precedence is necessary for cause but insufficient. Regressing an outcome on a lagged exposure does not automatically remove confounding or feedback. Granger-style predictive precedence is a forecasting notion, not general interventional causation. Causal language—effect, impact, attributable, leads to—should be reserved for a design and assumptions supporting it.

> **Common misconception 16.68 — “Enough controls, data, or predictive accuracy turn an association into a causal effect.”**  
> Causal identification depends on intervention, timing, selection, measurement, and structural assumptions. More variables can introduce collider or mediator bias, and more rows only estimate the same unidentified association more precisely.
