# 17.10 Baselines and error decomposition

A **baseline** is a simple reference procedure. For regression it might predict the training mean or median. For classification it might predict class prevalence or the most frequent class. A complex model that cannot improve a relevant baseline has not justified its complexity.

Prediction error can be discussed through **bias** and **variance**. In a squared-error theoretical decomposition, bias describes systematic difference between average fitted prediction and the target function, while variance describes sensitivity of fitted prediction to the sampled training data. Highly constrained models can underfit; highly flexible models can follow sample-specific noise. This is not a one-dimensional law that deeper models always have low bias and high variance. Data, regularisation, optimisation, and representation interact.

**Overfitting** means fitting sample-specific variation in a way that harms relevant new-case performance. **Underfitting** means the model or fitting procedure fails to capture usable structure even in development data. Training error alone cannot diagnose generalisation.

### 17.10.1 Baselines encode the minimum useful comparison

A baseline should match information available at prediction time. Predicting the global training mean is weak; predicting the most recent known value, a seasonal value from last week, a rule already used operationally, or a simple domain equation can be stronger. A new model must justify cost and risk relative to the incumbent, not merely relative to zero.

Baselines diagnose pipeline failures. If a sophisticated model performs worse than a constant, possible causes include target inversion, metric direction, split mismatch, preprocessing errors, or severe shift. If a random identifier feature performs well, duplication or leakage is likely.

For classification, prevalence forecasts provide a probability baseline; majority-class labels provide an accuracy baseline; random rankings provide a ranking baseline. These are different. A model can beat majority accuracy while have worse log loss than prevalence if its probabilities are overconfident.

> **Common misconception 17.25 — “The baseline is intentionally bad so the real model looks useful.”**  
> A credible baseline is the strongest simple or incumbent procedure justified by available information. Weak baselines conceal whether complexity adds value.

### 17.10.2 Bias–variance decomposition and learning curves

Assume \(Y=f(x)+\varepsilon\), with \(E[\varepsilon\mid x]=0\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). Across hypothetical training samples \(D\), squared prediction error at fixed \(x\) decomposes as

\[
E_D E_{Y\mid x}\!\left[(Y-\hat f_D(x))^2\right]
=
\sigma^2
+\left(E_D[\hat f_D(x)]-f(x)\right)^2
+E_D\!\left[(\hat f_D(x)-E_D\hat f_D(x))^2\right].
\]

The terms are irreducible conditional noise, squared bias, and estimator variance under this model. Real applications add measurement error, shift, optimisation variability, and misspecified targets. “Bias” here is not demographic or measurement bias, though those can affect the decomposition’s objects.

A **learning curve** plots training and validation performance against training-set size. High and similar errors can indicate limited representation or underfitting; a persistent large gap can indicate variance, leakage, or distribution differences. Curves are diagnostic patterns, not mechanical proofs. Hyperparameters must be reselected at each size if the question concerns the complete learning procedure.

Modern overparameterised systems can show non-monotonic **double-descent** patterns as capacity changes. This does not repeal bias–variance reasoning; it shows that simple monotonic textbook caricatures omit interpolation, optimisation, and implicit regularisation.

> **Common misconception 17.26 — “More complex models always have lower bias and higher variance.”**  
> Complexity is multidimensional, and optimisation, regularisation, data representation, sample size, and interpolation regimes alter both. The phrase is a conditional diagnostic, not a universal ordering.
