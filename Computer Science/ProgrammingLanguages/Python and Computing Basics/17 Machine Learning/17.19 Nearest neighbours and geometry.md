# 17.19 Nearest neighbours and geometry

A nearest-neighbour method predicts from training cases close to a query under a chosen distance. Euclidean distance between vectors \(x\) and \(z\) is

\[
d(x,z)=\sqrt{\sum_j(x_j-z_j)^2}.
\]

Feature scale therefore determines geometry. A feature measured in thousands can dominate one measured between zero and one. Standardisation is not cosmetic.

As dimensions increase, distances can become less discriminating under many distributions, and the volume near a fixed-radius neighbourhood changes sharply. This family of phenomena is called the **curse of dimensionality**. It is not a claim that every high-dimensional problem is impossible; representation structure and sparsity can make specific problems tractable.

Nearest-neighbour prediction stores substantial training information and can be expensive at query time. Index structures accelerate some metrics and dimensions but degrade in others. Evaluation must include latency and memory, not only accuracy.

### 17.19.1 A metric defines neighbourhood

A **metric** \(d\) is non-negative, symmetric, zero only for identical points, and satisfies the triangle inequality. Euclidean, Manhattan, cosine-derived, Hamming, edit, and domain-specific distances encode different invariances. Some useful dissimilarities violate metric axioms, limiting index structures and geometric reasoning.

For Minkowski distance,

\[
d_p(x,z)=\left(\sum_j |x_j-z_j|^p\right)^{1/p},
\]

\(p=1\) gives Manhattan and \(p=2\) Euclidean distance. Standardisation weights features inversely by their fitted spread; it does not establish that one standard deviation has equal substantive meaning across fields. Mahalanobis distance incorporates covariance but can be unstable or singular in high dimensions.

Categorical mismatch, missingness, time, and text require deliberate distance. One-hot Euclidean distance gives every category pair the same geometry within a field but lets fields with many simultaneously active indicators contribute more. Mixed-type distances or separate kernels can be preferable.

> **Common misconception 17.44 — “Nearest neighbours is assumption-free because it does not fit coefficients.”**  
> Distance, scaling, \(k\), weighting, feature representation, and training population are strong assumptions. The training records themselves are fitted state.

### 17.19.2 Search, smoothing, and dimensional concentration

With \(k=1\), classification can follow local noise; larger \(k\) smooths across broader neighbourhoods and can erase small groups. Uniform voting and distance weighting express different locality. Ties and equal distances need deterministic policies.

Brute-force query cost is roughly proportional to stored cases times features. KD trees and ball trees prune search under suitable metric and low-dimensional geometry; in high dimensions pruning can approach brute force. Approximate nearest-neighbour methods trade recall for speed and introduce their own indexes and nondeterminism.

Distance concentration means nearest and farthest distances can become similar under some high-dimensional distributions. Sparse meaningful structure, learned embeddings, and appropriate metrics can counteract it, but dimensionality reduction learned on all data leaks. Evaluate the complete representation-and-search system.

Nearest-neighbour systems retain examples and can disclose sensitive training records through outputs or membership attacks. Deletion requests may require rebuilding indexes. Latency depends on data growth, so operational evaluation must include realistic corpus size.

> **Common misconception 17.45 — “k-nearest neighbours has no training cost.”**  
> It may perform little numerical optimisation, but validation, scaling, representation, indexing, storage, privacy controls, and update construction are training work.

### 17.19.3 Margins, support vectors, and kernels

A linear score \(f(x)=w^\mathsf{T}x+b\) defines a separating hyperplane \(f(x)=0\). For labels encoded as \(y_i\in\{-1,+1\}\), the signed functional margin is \(y_if(x_i)\). After accounting for the scale of \(w\), a large geometric margin places training cases farther from the boundary.

A soft-margin linear support-vector machine can minimise

\[
\frac12\|w\|_2^2
+C\sum_i\max(0,1-y_i f(x_i)).
\]

The **hinge loss** is zero once a case lies beyond margin one, linear for violations, and does not directly produce probabilities. \(C\) controls the cost of violations relative to weight norm under the implementation’s scaling. Features require scaling because the norm defines geometry.

Only cases on or within the margin, or violating it, contribute to the optimum under the dual representation; these are **support vectors**. Their number affects prediction cost in kernel machines. A support vector is not necessarily an outlier or prototypical case.

A **kernel** \(K(x,z)\) computes an inner product in an implicit feature space when it satisfies the required positive-semidefinite conditions. The radial basis function kernel

\[
K(x,z)=\exp(-\gamma\|x-z\|_2^2)
\]

produces local nonlinear boundaries. Large \(\gamma\) makes influence narrow and can fit fine detail; small \(\gamma\) makes influence broad. \(C\), \(\gamma\), scale, and sampling interact and must be selected together inside validation.

~~~python
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y = make_moons(
    n_samples=800,
    noise=0.22,
    random_state=6,
)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X,
    y,
    stratify=y,
    random_state=6,
)
classifier = make_pipeline(
    StandardScaler(),
    SVC(
        kernel="rbf",
        C=3.0,
        gamma="scale",
    ),
)
classifier.fit(X_train, y_train)
prediction = classifier.predict(X_test)
print(accuracy_score(y_test, prediction))
~~~

Setting <code>probability=True</code> in an SVC invokes an additional probability-fitting procedure with computational and validation implications; it does not turn the hinge objective into likelihood. Alternatively, calibrate decision scores through an explicit cross-fitted calibration procedure.

Kernel matrices require \(O(n^2)\) storage in a direct formulation and can make large-sample training costly. Linear solvers, approximate feature maps, neighbour methods, trees, or neural representations may scale differently. The “kernel trick” avoids explicit high-dimensional coordinates, not dependence on the number of training cases.
