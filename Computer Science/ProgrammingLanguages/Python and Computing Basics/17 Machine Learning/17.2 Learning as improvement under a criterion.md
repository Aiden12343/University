# 17.2 Learning as improvement under a criterion

**Machine learning** studies computational systems whose performance on a specified task improves through experience represented by data. Each noun requires qualification:

- the **task** states what output is required from what input;
- the **performance measure** states how outputs are judged;
- the **experience** states which observations or interactions are available;
- the **model class** states which input–output relationships the procedure can represent;
- the **learning algorithm** states how a member of that class is selected.

A system has not “learned” merely because it contains parameters obtained from data. It has learned only relative to a criterion. A memorising table can achieve perfect accuracy on repeated training records and fail completely on new cases. The central problem is **generalisation**: useful performance on relevant cases not used to choose the fitted model.

An **input** is the information supplied to a model for one case. A **feature** is one represented component derived from that input. A **target**, **label**, or **response** is the outcome a supervised procedure is trained to predict. A **prediction** is a model’s output for an input. Features are not necessarily natural properties. Token counts, pixels, temperature readings, and encoded categories result from measurement and representation decisions.

**Supervised learning** uses examples pairing inputs with desired targets. **Unsupervised learning** seeks structure in inputs without supplied target labels, such as groupings or lower-dimensional representations. **Reinforcement learning** studies an agent choosing actions in an environment and receiving consequences over time. These families overlap in systems but imply different evidence and evaluation.

### Worked example 17.1 — Specify the task before the model

“Predict customer churn” is incomplete. A usable specification might state:

- unit: one active subscription at midnight UTC each Monday;
- input window: events available up to that time;
- target: whether the subscription ends within the next 28 days;
- exclusions: administrative test accounts;
- output: estimated probability between 0 and 1;
- use: prioritise a fixed-capacity service review;
- evaluation population: subscriptions created after a specified deployment date;
- harms: unnecessary contact and failure to contact at-risk customers.

Changing the target horizon from 28 days to 7 days creates a different learning problem. Including an event recorded after the prediction time supplies future information and invalidates evaluation. No estimator choice repairs an incoherent task.

### 17.2.1 Task, environment, and action are separate objects

A prediction task is not complete until it defines the circumstances in which a prediction is made. Let (X_t) denote information genuinely available at decision time (t), (Y_{t+h}) the target evaluated after horizon (h), and (a) an action chosen from the prediction. The system may estimate a score (s(X_t)), a probability, a numerical outcome, a ranking, a set, or a generated sequence. The action rule is another mapping and may depend on capacity, costs, law, and human review.

For example, estimating the probability of equipment failure in 30 days differs from deciding which ten machines receive preventive maintenance. A calibrated probability model may be sound while the allocation rule is inequitable or wasteful. Conversely, a coarse ranking may support the constrained decision even when individual probabilities are imperfect. Model quality and decision quality must therefore be evaluated separately and jointly.

The **deployment environment** includes the population, measurement instruments, upstream software, available interventions, feedback loops, and consequences. An offline benchmark samples only part of that environment. If predictions influence which outcomes become observable—for example, inspected machines reveal defects while uninspected machines do not—the deployed model changes its own future training data.

Targets require operational definitions. “Churn”, “fraud”, “toxicity”, “creditworthiness”, and “success” are not primitive natural labels. They are constructed from observable rules, time windows, adjudication, and sometimes institutional decisions. **Label noise** is disagreement between recorded target and intended target under a model; **label ambiguity** is the possibility that no single target is objectively determined; **selective labels** arise when outcomes are observed only after particular historical decisions.

> **Common misconception 17.3 — “A target column is ground truth.”**  
> A target is a recorded operationalisation produced by measurement and institutional processes. It can be delayed, censored, disputed, selectively observed, or systematically wrong for particular groups.

### 17.2.2 Learning paradigms define different evidence

Supervised learning includes **regression**, where an output is commonly numerical, and **classification**, where it belongs to a finite category set. The distinction follows the target and loss rather than the storage dtype: an integer disease code is categorical, while an integer count can be a regression target. **Multi-output** tasks predict several targets; **multilabel** classification permits several labels per case; **ordinal** prediction preserves category order without assuming equal intervals.

Unsupervised learning supplies no target variable that declares the desired structure. Clustering, density estimation, representation learning, anomaly scoring, and dimensionality reduction optimise different objectives. Their validation requires reconstruction, held-out likelihood, stability, known synthetic structure, or external usefulness appropriate to the objective.

**Semi-supervised learning** combines labelled and unlabelled cases. **Self-supervised learning** creates supervisory signals from the input structure itself, such as predicting hidden tokens or alternative views. **Transfer learning** reuses representations or parameters learned for another task or population. **Online learning** updates as observations arrive. **Active learning** chooses which cases to label. Each changes which data are adaptive and which evaluation boundaries are required.

Reinforcement learning differs because actions affect later observations and rewards. The learner faces delayed consequences, exploration, and a sequential environment. A reward is a designed numerical signal, not a complete account of human value. This chapter concentrates on supervised learning and the gradient machinery that supports modern representation learning; reinforcement learning is located in the conceptual map but not treated as a complete specialism.

### 17.2.3 Generalisation is always relative to a distribution and use

Let a training procedure (A) map a training sample (D) to a fitted predictor (f_D=A(D)). Its training loss measures performance on cases used by (A). **Generalisation error** concerns loss on relevant new cases generated under a stated regime. The word “new” is insufficient: a duplicated customer record can be new as a row while containing familiar entity information; next year may be new in time but drawn after a policy change.

A lookup table demonstrates the gap:

~~~python
from collections.abc import Hashable, Iterable
from typing import TypeVar

Key = TypeVar("Key", bound=Hashable)
Value = TypeVar("Value")


class Memoriser:
    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self._values: dict[Key, Value] = {}

    def fit(self, keys: Iterable[Key], values: Iterable[Value]) -> "Memoriser":
        self._values = dict(zip(keys, values, strict=True))
        return self

    def predict(self, keys: Iterable[Key]) -> list[Value]:
        return [self._values[key] for key in keys]


model = Memoriser().fit(["A", "B", "C"], [1, 0, 1])
assert model.predict(["A", "C"]) == [1, 1]
~~~

The fitted object reproduces known identifiers exactly but raises <code>KeyError</code> on an unseen identifier. Perfect training performance says nothing about the relation between features and outcomes. Some modern overparameterised models can both memorise and generalise because architecture, optimisation, data structure, and implicit regularisation interact; “capacity greater than sample size” alone is not a complete diagnosis.

**In-distribution generalisation** assumes evaluation resembles the training-generating regime under the relevant conditional structure. **Out-of-distribution generalisation** asks for performance under specified changes. It is not a single ability: a model may tolerate lighting changes but fail on a new camera, or transfer between hospitals but fail after a coding revision. Robustness claims must name the shift.

> **Common misconception 17.4 — “Generalisation means performing well on any unseen input.”**  
> It means performance on a defined class of relevant future or held-out cases. No finite training set licenses unrestricted behaviour over every possible input or environment.

The empirical loss is only a proxy for the decision objective. If mistakes have unequal consequences, cases have unequal importance, or abstention is possible, the loss and evaluation must represent them. Optimising a surrogate can be computationally convenient, but its relation to the true decision must be proved or tested.

> **Common misconception 17.5 — “A learning algorithm discovers the task from the data.”**  
> Data constrain a procedure only after people choose the unit, representation, target, loss, hypothesis class, sampling regime, and intended action. Different choices can make the same records support different learned systems.

### 17.2.4 Prediction, causal effects, and decisions answer different questions

Predictive learning estimates an association such as \(P(Y\mid X=x)\) or a target summary of it. A causal question asks what would happen under an intervention. If \(A\) is a treatment, the expression

\[
P(Y\mid A=a,X=x)
\]

describes outcomes among observed cases receiving \(a\), whereas an interventional quantity written

\[
P(Y\mid \operatorname{do}(A=a),X=x)
\]

describes a hypothetical action that sets \(A\) while breaking its ordinary causes. Equality requires identification assumptions or an experimental design. A confounder influencing both \(A\) and \(Y\) can make a highly predictive association a poor intervention estimate.

A **potential outcome** \(Y(a)\) denotes the outcome that would occur under action \(a\). For one unit, only the outcome under the realised action is ordinarily observed; the other is counterfactual. Causal inference therefore cannot be reduced to adding the action as another predictive feature. Random assignment can identify average effects by making action independent of potential outcomes under compliance and measurement assumptions. Observational identification instead relies on substantive claims such as no unmeasured confounding, overlap, and consistency.

Decision theory adds consequences. Given information \(x\), action set \(\mathcal A\), uncertain outcome \(Y\), and utility \(U(a,Y)\), an idealised decision rule chooses

\[
a^*(x)=
\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal A}
E[U(a,Y)\mid X=x].
\]

A probability model supplies only part of this expression. Utility includes benefits, harms, delay, resource limits, legal duties, and risk attitudes. The action can itself change \(Y\), in which case interventional rather than purely observational outcome models are needed.

This separation prevents three category errors:

- a variable can improve prediction while being unusable for intervention;
- an accurate risk score need not imply that acting on the highest risk produces greatest benefit;
- an intervention with positive average effect can harm a subgroup or fail under a capacity constraint.

Uplift modelling, treatment-effect estimation, contextual bandits, and reinforcement learning address action-dependent outcomes under additional assumptions. They do not make those assumptions disappear. Exploration can identify effects but deliberately changes who receives actions and raises ethical and operational constraints.

**Selective labels** make the distinction concrete. A repayment outcome is observed for approved loans, not rejected ones. A predictor trained on approvals estimates performance conditional on the historical approval policy. Deploying it to decide for rejected applicants extrapolates into an unlabelled region. Neither cross-validation nor model complexity repairs the missing counterfactual evidence.
