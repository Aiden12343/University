# 17.31 Fairness, proxies, and allocation

A model participates in a socio-technical system: people define labels, collect data, choose actions, and bear consequences. **Fairness** is not one scalar property. Proposed criteria include equal error rates, equal positive predictive values, calibration within groups, individual similarity, and causal notions. Some cannot be satisfied simultaneously when group prevalences differ except under special conditions.

Removing a protected attribute does not remove its information; other features can act as **proxies**. Including the attribute can be necessary to audit disparities or implement a lawful correction. The appropriate treatment depends on jurisdiction, use, and normative purpose; technical optimisation cannot resolve those choices.

Labels can encode historical decisions rather than desired outcomes. Predicting prior hiring decisions reproduces the decision process, not an objective concept of merit. Measurement error can differ by group. Aggregate metrics can conceal severe failure in a small population.

A responsible analysis documents:

- intended and prohibited uses;
- affected populations;
- label construction;
- representation and missingness;
- performance with uncertainty by relevant slices;
- threshold and resource constraints;
- appeal, override, and recourse;
- privacy and security;
- monitoring and retirement.

### 17.31.1 Formal criteria and their denominators

Let \(A\) denote a group attribute, \(Y\) the target, \(\hat Y\) a binary decision, and \(S\) a score.

**Demographic parity** requires

\[
P(\hat Y=1\mid A=a)
\]

to be equal across groups. It constrains allocation rates without conditioning on the observed target.

**Equal opportunity** requires equal true-positive rates:

\[
P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=1,A=a).
\]

**Equalised odds** additionally requires equal false-positive rates. **Predictive parity** compares positive predictive value:

\[
P(Y=1\mid\hat Y=1,A=a).
\]

**Group calibration** requires that, for people receiving score \(s\), outcome frequency is \(s\) within each group under a defined binning or continuous condition:

\[
P(Y=1\mid S=s,A=a)=s.
\]

These criteria condition on different denominators and express different normative concerns. When base rates differ and prediction is imperfect, calibration, equalised error rates, and predictive parity generally cannot all hold simultaneously except in special cases. An organisation cannot evade the choice by asking software to “optimise fairness.”

Observed \(Y\) may itself be selectively measured or normatively unsuitable. Equal error rates against arrest, diagnosed disease, or prior approval decisions can equalise fidelity to biased measurement rather than justice. Causal fairness definitions ask how decisions would differ under interventions or counterfactual protected status, but they require a defensible causal model and consistency assumptions.

> **Common misconception 17.68 — “Fairness means giving every demographic group the same positive-decision rate.”**  
> Equal allocation is one criterion. Whether it is appropriate depends on the decision, target validity, harms, legal context, base rates, and the institution’s obligations.

### 17.31.2 Auditing with uncertainty and intersections

Group metrics require uncertainty intervals, especially for small groups and rare outcomes. Reporting a rate of zero from three cases is not evidence of zero population risk. Bootstrap or model-based intervals must respect clusters and time structure. Privacy constraints can conflict with fine-grained auditing, requiring governance rather than silent omission.

Single-attribute averages can conceal **intersectional** failure. A system may have similar aggregate performance by sex and by race while failing a subgroup defined by both. Exhaustively searching many intersections creates multiplicity and unstable estimates. Hierarchical models, predeclared priority groups, minimum support rules, and qualitative investigation can complement one another.

Thresholds translate scores into allocations. Group-specific thresholds can equalise a chosen rate while treating otherwise similar scores differently; a shared threshold can produce unequal rates when score distributions or calibration differ. The acceptable policy is a legal and ethical decision informed by technical consequences.

Mitigation can act at several layers:

- improve sampling, measurement, and label construction;
- reweight or transform training data;
- constrain the training objective;
- adjust thresholds or allocate review resources;
- redesign the institutional process or remove the predictive use.

Every intervention can shift another metric. Evaluate utility and harms for all relevant groups on data not used to tune the mitigation. Provide contestability, human review with measured quality, and meaningful recourse. A nominal human-in-the-loop can rubber-stamp automation or introduce its own disparities.

> **Common misconception 17.69 — “Removing protected attributes prevents discrimination.”**  
> Proxies can reconstruct them, labels can encode prior inequity, and impact can remain unequal. Protected attributes may be essential for auditing, subject to lawful and privacy-preserving governance.
