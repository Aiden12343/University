# 17.4 Loss, risk, and empirical risk

A **loss function** \(L(\hat{y},y)\) assigns a numerical penalty to prediction \(\hat{y}\) when the target is \(y\). Loss translates the task into an optimisation criterion. For numerical regression, squared error is

\[
L(\hat{y},y)=(\hat{y}-y)^2.
\]

Absolute error is

\[
L(\hat{y},y)=|\hat{y}-y|.
\]

Squaring penalises large errors disproportionately and is differentiable at zero; absolute error is less sensitive to extreme magnitude but has a corner at zero. Neither captures every real consequence.

The **risk** of a model is expected loss over the relevant data-generating distribution:

\[
R(\theta)=\operatorname{E}[L(f_\theta(X),Y)].
\]

That distribution is unknown. **Empirical risk** averages loss over observed training examples:

\[
\hat{R}_n(\theta)
=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(f_\theta(x_i),y_i).
\]

**Empirical risk minimisation** chooses parameters with low observed average loss, possibly subject to constraints or regularisation. Low empirical risk does not guarantee low population risk because the sample is finite, selected, and potentially different from deployment.

### Worked example 17.2 — Two losses select different centres

For a constant predictor \(c\), minimising average squared error selects the sample mean. Minimising average absolute error selects a sample median. With targets \(2,3,3,4,80\), squared loss chooses 18.4, while absolute loss permits 3. The learning algorithm’s “best” answer depends on the declared loss.

Suppose underprediction costs twice as much as overprediction. Neither symmetric loss encodes that fact. A weighted asymmetric loss may be needed. The mathematical objective is a compressed value judgement; making it explicit permits criticism.

### 17.4.1 Loss, utility, and action

A training loss is a mathematical proxy; **utility** represents the value or harm of consequences to affected parties under a decision model. Minimising prediction loss need not maximise utility. A hospital may care about delayed treatment, unnecessary intervention, staffing capacity, and unequal error burden, none of which is fully represented by ordinary log loss.

For a binary action with calibrated event probability \(p\), false-positive cost \(C_{FP}\), and false-negative cost \(C_{FN}\), acting positive has expected cost \((1-p)C_{FP}\), while acting negative has expected cost \(pC_{FN}\). Acting positive is preferred when

\[
p > \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP}+C_{FN}}.
\]

This threshold follows only under the simplified cost table, calibrated probabilities, equal treatment effects, and no capacity interaction. If reviewing one case prevents reviewing another, decisions are coupled and a per-case threshold is incomplete.

Losses also determine which conditional functional is optimal. Squared error elicits a conditional mean; absolute error elicits a conditional median; asymmetric quantile loss elicits a conditional quantile. Selecting a loss therefore selects the kind of prediction.

~~~python
import numpy as np


def pinball_loss(
    prediction: np.ndarray,
    target: np.ndarray,
    quantile: float,
) -> np.ndarray:
    if not 0.0 < quantile < 1.0:
        raise ValueError("quantile must lie strictly between zero and one")
    error = target - prediction
    return np.maximum(quantile * error, (quantile - 1.0) * error)


prediction = np.array([10.0, 10.0])
target = np.array([8.0, 14.0])
print(pinball_loss(prediction, target, quantile=0.9))
~~~

At quantile 0.9, underprediction receives greater weight than equal-sized overprediction. The loss does not prove that 0.9 is ethically or operationally appropriate.

> **Common misconception 17.8 — “The standard loss is a neutral technical choice.”**  
> A loss specifies which errors matter, how cases are weighted, and which distributional functional is targeted. It embeds consequences even when those consequences remain unstated.

### 17.4.2 Probabilistic losses and truthful forecasts

A **scoring rule** assigns loss to a probabilistic forecast after an outcome is observed. A rule is **proper** when reporting the forecaster’s true probability distribution minimises expected loss; it is strictly proper when that optimum is unique under conditions.

For binary outcome \(y\in\{0,1\}\) and predicted probability \(p\), log loss is

\[
-y\log p-(1-y)\log(1-p),
\]

and Brier loss is \((p-y)^2\). Log loss penalises confident errors without a finite bound as \(p\) approaches the wrong endpoint. Implementations clip probabilities for numerical computation, which changes extreme finite values but avoids taking \(\log 0\).

~~~python
from math import log


def binary_log_loss(probability: float, outcome: int) -> float:
    if outcome not in (0, 1):
        raise ValueError("outcome must be zero or one")
    epsilon = 1e-15
    p = min(max(probability, epsilon), 1.0 - epsilon)
    return -(outcome * log(p) + (1 - outcome) * log(1.0 - p))


assert binary_log_loss(0.9, 1) < binary_log_loss(0.6, 1)
assert binary_log_loss(0.99, 0) > binary_log_loss(0.6, 0)
~~~

Accuracy after thresholding is not a proper probability score: forecasts 0.51 and 0.99 receive the same correct label at threshold 0.5. A model can improve accuracy while degrading calibration and probabilistic usefulness.

> **Common misconception 17.9 — “The metric used to rank models and the loss used to train them must be identical.”**  
> Optimisation may require a differentiable surrogate, while evaluation may use several decision and probability criteria. The relation between surrogate improvement and real objective must nevertheless be justified.

### 17.4.3 Weighted empirical risk and population meaning

Ordinary empirical risk weights each stored row equally. A weighted objective is

\[
\widehat R_w(\theta)
=
\frac{\sum_i w_i L(f_\theta(x_i),y_i)}
     {\sum_i w_i},
\qquad w_i\ge 0.
\]

Weights can represent unequal sampling probabilities, class-cost emphasis, importance weighting under covariate shift, repeated-frequency compression, or policy priorities. These meanings are not interchangeable. Multiplying weights by a constant leaves the normalised empirical average unchanged but can alter a library’s regularisation balance or optimiser step if its objective is normalised differently.

Repeated rows are not always equivalent to integer sample weights: stochastic batching, regularisation scaling, tree subsampling, early stopping, and metadata routing can make them differ. Inspect the estimator’s documented use of <code>sample_weight</code> and ensure the validation scorer uses the intended weights as well.

The empirical objective is random because the sample is random or contingent. Optimisation error—the gap between achieved and best training objective—is distinct from estimation error due to finite data, approximation error due to model class, and irreducible outcome variation. Driving training loss to machine precision addresses only one component.

> **Common misconception 17.10 — “The model with the lowest training loss is the best-fitted answer available from the data.”**  
> It may exploit finite-sample noise, incorrect weights, leakage, numerical artefacts, or an unsuitable surrogate. Training optimisation is nested inside a statistical and decision problem.
