# 19.3 Confidentiality, integrity, availability, and authenticity

Three classical objectives are:

- **confidentiality:** information is not disclosed to unauthorised actors;
- **integrity:** information or system state is not altered undetectably or without authorisation;
- **availability:** authorised use remains possible within required conditions.

Further objectives include:

- **authenticity:** evidence supports the claimed source or identity;
- **accountability:** actions can be attributed under an audit model;
- **non-repudiation:** a party cannot plausibly deny a committed action under a legal/cryptographic framework;
- **privacy:** people retain appropriate control and protection concerning information and observation;
- **safety:** system behaviour avoids unacceptable physical or human harm.

These objectives can conflict. Extensive logging supports accountability but threatens confidentiality and privacy. Strict account lockout impedes password guessing but enables denial of service. Security design is controlled trade-off, not accumulation of every control.

### 19.3.1 State, transit, use, and lifecycle

Protection claims must identify where information exists. **Data at rest** resides in storage; **data in transit** crosses a communication channel; **data in use** is interpreted or transformed by a running component. Storage encryption can protect discarded media while an authorised application exposes plaintext. Transport encryption can protect a connection while an endpoint logs the request. An encrypted backup remains confidential only while its key and restoration path remain protected.

Integrity likewise has layers. A cryptographic tag can detect changed bytes, a database constraint can preserve a state invariant, a code review can constrain authorised changes, and an audit trail can support later reconstruction. None alone proves that the original data were true. Authentic input from a compromised sensor is authentically wrong.

Availability needs a service definition. A health endpoint returning 200 while every purchase fails is not useful availability. Specify eligible users, operations, correctness conditions, latency, freshness, geographic or safety constraints, and measurement windows. **Resilience** is the ability to resist, absorb, recover from, and adapt to disruption; it includes recovery quality rather than only uptime.

### 19.3.2 Privacy, safety, and accountability

Privacy is not equivalent to secrecy. It concerns appropriate collection, use, linkage, inference, retention, access, correction, and deletion of information about people. A public fact can still be used in an intrusive aggregate. **Data minimisation** limits collection and retention to what a stated purpose needs. **Purpose limitation** resists later reuse incompatible with that purpose. Pseudonymous identifiers reduce some direct exposure but can often be linked through behaviour or auxiliary data.

Safety analysis asks how software action can harm people or the physical world. Availability controls for a medical system differ from those for a discussion forum; failing closed can protect confidentiality while preventing urgent care. Safety and security therefore require explicit priority rules and degraded modes, not one universal maxim.

Accountability requires reliable identity and event semantics, protected records, fair investigation, and governance. Non-repudiation is frequently overstated. A valid digital signature shows that a private key produced a signature over bytes; it does not prove which human controlled the endpoint, whether malware acted, whether consent was informed, or how a court will treat the evidence.

> **Common misconception 19.6 — “Encryption at rest makes stored data confidential.”**  
> It protects against specified storage-access threats. Applications, keys, snapshots, exports, indexes, logs, and authorised identities can still disclose plaintext.

> **Common misconception 19.7 — “A valid signature proves a person intended the transaction.”**  
> It proves possession or use of key material under algorithmic assumptions. Human intent, endpoint integrity, delegation, coercion, and legal effect require additional evidence.
