# 20.30 Cost as a systems constraint

Cloud billing measures allocated or consumed resources under provider-specific dimensions: compute time, memory, storage class, requests, data transfer, database capacity, logs, metrics, addresses, and managed-service premiums. A design can be technically reliable and financially unsustainable.

**FinOps** names practices connecting engineering, finance, and product decisions around variable technology cost. Useful cost models allocate spending to services or workloads through tags, accounts, projects, and measured drivers. Shared infrastructure requires an explicit allocation rule.

Optimisation begins with unit economics:

\[
\text{cost per successful operation}
=
\frac{\text{total relevant cost}}
{\text{successful useful operations}}.
\]

Reducing headline compute while increasing retries, data transfer, or operator labour may increase total cost. Reserved commitments lower unit price in exchange for utilisation risk. Spot or interruptible capacity lowers price but requires interruption-tolerant work.

Budgets and anomaly alerts need ownership and response. Hard automatic shutdown can create severe availability or data risk; use graduated controls and distinguish experimental from critical environments.

### 20.30.1 Cost model and allocation

Cost has fixed, stepwise, and variable components. A database instance may be fixed for the month, storage grows per byte-time, requests charge per operation, and support or licences jump at a tier. Marginal cost—the cost of one additional useful operation—can differ from average cost. Architecture decisions should state which measure they optimise.

Allocation maps shared cost to an owner or product through accounts, projects, namespaces, tags, measured usage, or an agreed rule. **Showback** reports attributed cost; **chargeback** transfers it into a budget. Allocation is never purely technical: a shared control plane may be divided by requests, reserved capacity, revenue, or equal share, each creating incentives.

Tags are incomplete when resources are untaggable, tags are missing, or shared network and support charges appear separately. Reconcile allocation totals to the provider bill and retain an “unallocated” category rather than distributing unknown cost invisibly. Ownership metadata needs enforcement at creation and expiry for experiments.

### 20.30.2 Unit economics and optimisation

Choose a useful denominator: successful orders, processed documents, active tenant-months, or stored protected gigabyte. Requests are poor units if one user outcome fans out unpredictably. Track cost per unit by service version and workload mix, and include failure waste—retries, abandoned work, idle surge, duplicate telemetry, and incident labour.

Optimisation order is generally:

1. remove unused resources and pathological waste;
2. correct architecture and data transfer;
3. right-size under SLO and failure requirements;
4. improve scheduling and elasticity;
5. purchase commitments for the stable residual demand;
6. reassess unit value and demand.

Buying a commitment before reducing waste locks it in. Spot capacity is appropriate for checkpointable, replicated, or delay-tolerant work; interruption of quorum members or stateful writes can cost more than the discount. Egress can dominate a design that moves data across zones, regions, or providers; include both monetary cost and latency/failure edges.

Telemetry has unit cost. High-cardinality metrics, verbose logs, full traces, long retention, and repeated indexing can rival application storage. Set differentiated retention and sampling from investigative, audit, security, and legal needs. Cost reduction must not delete the evidence required to detect billing fraud or recover an incident.

### 20.30.3 Financial guardrails and sustainability

Forecast with uncertainty bands and known step changes rather than one precise monthly number. Alerts should compare actual and forecast, unit cost, and causal dimensions. A spending spike caused by successful customer growth differs from a retry storm, leaked key, or orphaned experiment.

Budgets need response authority. Development environments can stop automatically after warning and state capture; critical services should shed nonessential work or require escalation rather than lose durable state. Provider quota can act as a hard financial guardrail but may become an unplanned availability limit.

Energy and carbon impact are additional system constraints. Utilisation, hardware efficiency, region energy mix, data movement, retention, and embodied hardware all matter; a single provider dashboard number is an estimate under a methodology. Optimise without compromising required reliability or shifting impact outside the measurement boundary.

> **Common misconception 20.63 — “Serverless means paying only for useful work.”**  
> Charges can include invocations, duration, provisioned concurrency, logs, network, storage, orchestration, retries, and idle dependent services; failed work still consumes resources.

> **Common misconception 20.64 — “High utilisation is financially optimal.”**  
> Near-saturation queueing, failure risk, scaling delay, and lost demand can make total cost per successful outcome worse despite fewer idle resources.
