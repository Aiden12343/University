# 20.31 Change management

Every change has:

- intent;
- owner;
- reviewed diff;
- test evidence;
- dependency and migration impact;
- rollout plan;
- observability;
- stop conditions;
- rollback or mitigation;
- post-deployment verification.

Small frequent changes reduce the search space when failure occurs. A change window can coordinate expertise but does not make a large untested batch safe. Emergency changes should follow an abbreviated controlled path and receive retrospective review.

A **canary analysis** compares new and baseline populations over error, latency, saturation, and domain metrics. Random traffic assignment reduces selection differences, but low event counts create uncertainty. Automated rollback thresholds must avoid reacting to ordinary noise while acting before severe harm.

Change freezes reduce concurrent variability during critical periods but can defer patches and accumulate a large release. Their risks must be explicit.

### 20.31.1 Change risk and evidence

Change risk is not proportional only to lines modified. A one-line permission wildcard or retention deletion can exceed a large internal refactor. Assess blast radius, reversibility, observability, novelty, state transition, dependency coupling, operator familiarity, exposure duration, and consequence.

Standard changes are recurring, well-understood, pre-authorised operations executed through a tested path. Normal changes receive ordinary review. Emergency changes use an accelerated path because delay itself is harmful, while preserving identity, least privilege, evidence, stop conditions, and retrospective review. Calling every urgent request an emergency is a governance failure.

Separate proposer, reviewer, and high-risk approver where consequence warrants, but avoid review theatre. Reviewers need a semantic diff: artifact digest, dependency and SBOM change, configuration, database plan, IAM expansion, infrastructure replacements, and expected metrics. Thousands of generated lines without summarised consequences do not create informed approval.

### 20.31.2 Delivery-performance measures

As of the 2026 DORA definition, five software-delivery performance measures are used at the application or service level:

- **change lead time:** commit to production deployment duration;
- **deployment frequency:** deployments per period or interval between them;
- **failed deployment recovery time:** time to recover from a deployment requiring immediate intervention;
- **change fail rate:** proportion of deployments requiring immediate intervention;
- **deployment rework rate:** proportion of deployments that are unplanned responses to production incidents.

Definitions and collection boundaries must be stable. A deployment of identical bytes, a feature-flag release, and an infrastructure change may be counted differently; state policy. Failed-deployment recovery is narrower than recovery from every incident. Aggregating unlike services or ranking teams invites gaming and discards context.

Goodhart's law warns that when a measure becomes a target, it can cease to be a good measure. A mandate for daily deployments can produce empty changes; a low change-fail target can discourage detection or encourage oversized denominator events. Use measures as a balanced diagnostic over time, pair them with user outcomes and quality, and investigate constraints rather than rewarding the number itself.

### 20.31.3 Change records and verification

A machine-generated change record should link source review, artifact digest, attestations, configuration snapshot, IaC plan digest, schema operations, approver, deployment events, canary evidence, and final outcome. The record is an evidence graph, not a manually copied ticket paragraph.

Post-deployment verification asks whether intended user behaviour changed as predicted and unintended indicators remained within bounds. Absence of alerts is weak evidence if instrumentation is broken or thresholds lag. Run selected synthetic and domain checks, compare artifact and configuration identity, and observe for delayed effects appropriate to the change.

> **Common misconception 20.65 — “More approvals reduce change risk.”**  
> Uninformed or duplicated approvals add latency without evidence. Risk falls when authority, expertise, immutable subject, test evidence, and accountability align.

> **Common misconception 20.66 — “Deployment frequency is a universal productivity target.”**  
> It is one contextual delivery outcome. Optimising it alone can create meaningless releases, hidden batching, or unsafe change; compare a service with itself under stable definitions.
