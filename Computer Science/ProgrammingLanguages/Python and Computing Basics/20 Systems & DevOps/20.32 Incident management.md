# 20.32 Incident management

An operational **incident** is an unplanned disruption or degradation requiring coordinated response. Roles can include incident commander, operations lead, communications lead, and scribe. Clear authority prevents every participant issuing conflicting changes.

Initial response:

1. declare scope and severity from user impact;
2. establish a shared channel and timestamped log;
3. assign roles;
4. stabilise through safe reversible mitigation;
5. preserve evidence;
6. communicate known facts and uncertainty;
7. verify recovery through user-relevant indicators;
8. monitor recurrence.

Diagnosis is hypothesis testing. A graph beginning near a deployment suggests a hypothesis, not proof. Rollback can test and mitigate if safe. Multiple simultaneous changes and delayed metrics complicate attribution.

Communications should state impact, affected users, start time, current action, and next update. Avoid invented resolution times. Internal technical detail and public disclosure have different audiences but must remain factually consistent.

Post-incident analysis asks:

- what happened;
- why safeguards permitted it;
- what amplified or contained impact;
- why detection and recovery took their observed time;
- which assumptions failed;
- what specific changes will reduce recurrence or consequence.

Human action occurs within system incentives, interfaces, documentation, access, fatigue, and organisational constraints. Blaming an operator stops inquiry at the least useful boundary.

### 20.32.1 Severity and command

Severity should derive from current or credible imminent impact: users affected, essential function, data integrity, safety, financial exposure, legal obligation, and recovery complexity. Technical novelty is not severity. A familiar widespread outage can be critical; an exotic crash on an unused laboratory endpoint can be low impact.

The incident commander coordinates objectives, roles, decisions, and cadence rather than personally debugging every component. The operations lead executes mitigation; subject specialists investigate; communications translates verified facts for audiences; a scribe maintains the timeline and decision log. Small incidents can combine roles, but command and action ownership must remain clear.

Declare early when coordination benefit exceeds ceremony cost. A false-positive declaration can be closed with evidence; delayed declaration loses timeline, ownership, and communication. Severity can move as evidence changes. Record why, because later impact knowledge should not be projected backwards onto earlier decisions.

from dataclasses import dataclass
from datetime import datetime


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class IncidentDecision:
    occurred_at: datetime
    decision: str
    evidence: tuple[str, ...]
    alternatives_rejected: tuple[str, ...]

~~~

The record separates contemporaneous evidence from hindsight. `review_at` turns a mitigation into a hypothesis with a next observation point. Sensitive security and personal information may require a restricted evidence store with a redacted operational timeline.

### 20.32.2 Stabilisation and diagnosis

First reduce harm through a safe reversible action when available: stop rollout, shed a harmful operation, revoke a compromised identity, isolate a region, or increase a proven bottleneck's capacity. Preserve enough evidence to test hypotheses. “Do not change anything until root cause is known” can prolong harm; uncontrolled simultaneous changes destroy inference.

Troubleshooting is Bayesian in spirit even when no numbers are assigned. Begin with symptoms and change history, generate hypotheses that predict observations, run the lowest-risk discriminating test, update confidence, and record results. Confirmation bias favours the first plausible deployment; explicitly seek evidence that would refute it.

Correlate by time carefully. Clocks can differ, ingestion can delay, and a metric point represents an interval. Record event-time and receive-time where possible, synchronise clocks, and use causal identifiers. A graph change five minutes after deployment can be consequence, coincidence, or monitoring lag.

Mitigation success is judged by user-relevant indicators and state integrity, not the disappearance of one alert. Observe recovery long enough to cover retry queues, cache refill, replication catch-up, and recurrent scheduled load. Declare stable before resolved if deeper repair remains.

### 20.32.3 Communication, fatigue, and handover

Each update should state current impact, known scope, actions underway, uncertainty, and next update time. Do not speculate publicly or promise a restoration time unsupported by a bounded plan. Internal communication can include hypotheses but must label them. Different audiences need different detail without contradictory facts.

Long incidents degrade judgement. Rotate responders, maintain explicit handover, protect breaks, and avoid making one expert a permanent bottleneck. The handover covers current impact, system state, actions and results, active hypotheses, dangerous actions to avoid, next decisions, and communication commitments.

On-call design includes sustainable staffing, compensation, training, access, escalation, and psychological safety. Pages should be actionable and sufficiently urgent to justify interruption. Track pages per shift, false or unactionable rate, time to acknowledgement, and sleep disruption; suppressing alerts without fixing detection quality merely moves risk.

### 20.32.4 Learning and corrective action

A post-incident review constructs a timeline, impact, contributing conditions, successful defences, detection and response performance, and counterfactual questions. “Root cause” can imply one removable origin; complex incidents often require several ordinary conditions to align. Ask why the system permitted the action and why consequence propagated.

Corrective actions should change a system: enforce an invariant, reduce authority, add a test, alter default, improve detection, bound a queue, restore recovery independence, or redesign a workflow. Each has owner, priority, due condition, and verification. “Remind engineers to be careful” is not a durable control.

Track whether actions are completed and effective. Closing a ticket after merging code does not prove deployment or risk reduction. Re-run the scenario, inspect the control, or measure the changed signal. Also preserve what worked; removing a successful manual safeguard in the name of automation can reduce resilience.

> **Common misconception 20.67 — “The incident commander should be the strongest technical debugger.”**  
> Command requires maintaining shared state, priorities, authority, roles, and communication. Deep investigation can be delegated to specialists who are not coordinating the whole response.

> **Common misconception 20.68 — “Root cause is the first broken component.”**  
> Failure becomes an incident through interacting design, detection, capacity, authority, recovery, and organisational conditions. Replacing one component may leave the pathway intact.

> **Common misconception 20.69 — “Resolution ends incident work.”**  
> Evidence preservation, delayed-impact monitoring, durable repair, communication, action verification, and readiness restoration remain.
