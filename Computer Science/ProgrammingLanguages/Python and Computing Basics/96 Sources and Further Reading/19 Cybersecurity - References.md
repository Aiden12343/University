# 19.36 References and further study

The references below are not a substitute for a system-specific threat model. They provide the primary standards, foundational arguments, normative protocol definitions, and maintained technical guidance from which the chapter's claims are derived. Standards and online specifications must be read at a recorded version: a security argument that cites an unversioned web page cannot later establish which rules, defaults, or algorithms its author relied upon. Where a source distinguishes normative requirements from explanatory material, an implementation claim should cite the precise normative clause. Where several sources overlap, the more specialised source governs its own domain, while legislation, contracts, and local policy may impose additional obligations.

### 19.36.1 Foundations, policy models, and risk

1. Saltzer, Jerome H., and Michael D. Schroeder. “The Protection of Information in Computer Systems.” *Proceedings of the IEEE* 63, no. 9 (1975): 1278–1308. [doi:10.1109/PROC.1975.9939](https://doi.org/10.1109/PROC.1975.9939). This paper is the source of several enduring design principles used throughout the chapter: least privilege, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation, economy of mechanism, open design, separation of privilege, least common mechanism, and psychological acceptability. The principles are design questions rather than automatic guarantees. In particular, “open design” rejects dependence on secrecy of a mechanism; it does not require disclosing operational keys or personal data.

2. Lampson, Butler W. “A Note on the Confinement Problem.” *Communications of the ACM* 16, no. 10 (1973): 613–615. [doi:10.1145/362375.362389](https://doi.org/10.1145/362375.362389). Lampson precisely states why executing a program while preventing it from communicating retained information is harder than ordinary access control. The paper motivates this chapter's caution about plugins, sandboxes, shared resources, timing channels, and data in use. Its importance lies less in a particular historical operating system than in the distinction between denying direct access and confining every indirect information channel.

3. Bell, D. Elliott, and Leonard J. LaPadula. *Secure Computer Systems: Mathematical Foundations and Model*. MITRE Technical Report MTR-2547, Volume I, 1973. This report formalises a state-machine approach to confidentiality and the security properties conventionally summarised as restrictions on reading upward and writing downward in a classification lattice. It should be studied as a deliberately narrow model: it says much about confidentiality-preserving information flow, but does not by itself establish integrity, availability, usability, correct classification, or resistance to covert channels.

4. Biba, Kenneth J. *Integrity Considerations for Secure Computer Systems*. MITRE Technical Report MTR-3153, 1977. Biba develops integrity policies whose ordering intuitions complement confidentiality-oriented models. The report helps separate “who may learn this?” from “whose data may influence this decision?”—questions that are routinely conflated in application design. Its abstract labels do not select a business policy automatically; an engineer must still identify integrity levels, trusted subjects, permitted transformations, and the operational consequences of refusing information flows.

5. Clark, David D., and David R. Wilson. “A Comparison of Commercial and Military Computer Security Policies.” In *1987 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy*, 184–194. [doi:10.1109/SP.1987.10001](https://doi.org/10.1109/SP.1987.10001). Clark and Wilson shift attention towards well-formed transactions, constrained data items, certified transformation procedures, separation of duties, and auditable commercial integrity. The model directly informs the chapter's treatment of controlled state transitions. It explains why permission to write a database row is often too coarse: the security property may require that change to occur only through a validated transaction with independent authorisation.

6. Brewer, David F. C., and Michael J. Nash. “The Chinese Wall Security Policy.” In *1989 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy*, 206–214. [doi:10.1109/SECPRI.1989.36295](https://doi.org/10.1109/SECPRI.1989.36295). This work models conflicts of interest whose access constraints depend on a principal's preceding accesses. It is a useful counterexample to the idea that authorisation can always be reduced to static roles. The policy's historical terminology is now often replaced by “Brewer–Nash model”; the underlying contribution is dynamic, history-sensitive information-flow control across competing datasets.

7. Denning, Dorothy E. “A Lattice Model of Secure Information Flow.” *Communications of the ACM* 19, no. 5 (1976): 236–243. [doi:10.1145/360051.360056](https://doi.org/10.1145/360051.360056). Denning gives a general lattice formulation for constraining information flow and relates program operations to security classes. It supplies a formal foundation for reasoning about derived information, not merely direct object permissions. Readers should connect it to taint tracking and declassification while remembering that practical labelling, implicit flows, termination behaviour, and side channels complicate enforcement.

8. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0*. NIST CSWP 29, 2024. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.CSWP.29). CSF 2.0 organises cybersecurity outcomes under Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover. It is intentionally technology- and sector-neutral. Use it to communicate and profile desired outcomes, not as a claim that selecting category labels implements controls. A defensible profile maps each relevant outcome to owners, system boundaries, concrete safeguards, measurements, evidence, dependencies, exceptions, and residual risk.

9. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Risk Management Framework for Information Systems and Organizations*. NIST SP 800-37 Rev. 2, 2018. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-37r2). The RMF integrates preparation, categorisation, control selection and tailoring, implementation, assessment, authorisation, and continuous monitoring. Its lifecycle corrects the common error of treating security as a pre-release checklist. The document is especially useful for understanding how evidence about one component contributes to an accountable risk decision at a larger organisational level.

10. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Guide for Conducting Risk Assessments*. NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1, 2012. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-30r1). This guide distinguishes threat sources, threat events, vulnerabilities and predisposing conditions, likelihood, impact, and uncertainty. It supports the qualitative risk method in §19.2 and discourages multiplication of arbitrary ordinal scores as if they were measured probabilities. Readers should use its terminology to state evidence and uncertainty explicitly, while tailoring organisational tiers and assessment detail to the decision being made.

11. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations*. NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, including current errata and updates. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-53r5). This extensive control catalogue covers technical, operational, personnel, physical, supply-chain, privacy, and governance concerns. It is a source for selecting and tailoring controls, not a universal implementation recipe. A control identifier alone is weak evidence: the assessor must identify scope, parameters, inheritance, implementation, assessment procedure, limitations, and relationships to concrete threats and system requirements.

12. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Zero Trust Architecture*. NIST SP 800-207, 2020. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-207). The publication treats zero trust as an architectural approach centred on resources, explicit authentication and authorisation, dynamic policy, telemetry, and minimised implicit trust. It does not assert that every request can be made risk-free or that network controls are obsolete. Read it alongside §19.4 to distinguish a useful decision architecture from product branding and from the impossible objective of trusting no component whatsoever.

13. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *NIST Privacy Framework: A Tool for Improving Privacy through Enterprise Risk Management*, Version 1.0, 2020. [Official publication](https://www.nist.gov/privacy-framework/privacy-framework). The framework separates privacy risk from a narrow confidentiality model and considers how systems process data about people even when no unauthorised disclosure occurs. Its functions and profiles help connect data processing to individual and organisational consequences. It is particularly relevant to logging, reconnaissance, retention, identity proofing, modelled inferences, and incident communications.

14. OWASP Foundation. “Threat Modeling Cheat Sheet.” *OWASP Cheat Sheet Series*. [Maintained source](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Threat_Modeling_Cheat_Sheet.html). This practitioner reference structures threat modelling around decomposition, threat identification, mitigation, and validation, and surveys several elicitation approaches. It complements rather than replaces a formal security model. Record the exact revision used, and prefer a small model tied to real data flows and authority boundaries over a large diagram whose assumptions, owners, and verification status are unknown.

15. MITRE. *ATT&CK Knowledge Base*, Enterprise, Mobile, and Industrial Control Systems matrices; consult the versioned release current for the assessment. [Official knowledge base](https://attack.mitre.org/). ATT&CK catalogues observed adversary tactics and techniques and supplies a shared vocabulary for emulation, detection, and coverage analysis. A technique mapping is not evidence that an organisation detects every implementation of that technique. Defenders should record the ATT&CK version, data sources, platform assumptions, analytic logic, validation procedure, and adversary behaviours outside the catalogue.

### 19.36.2 Weaknesses, platforms, and application boundaries

16. MITRE. *Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE)*, version 4.20, and *2025 CWE Top 25 Most Dangerous Software Weaknesses*. [Official corpus](https://cwe.mitre.org/). CWE classifies recurring weakness forms such as improper neutralisation, bounds errors, missing authorisation, and resource mismanagement. The Top 25 is an evidence-informed prioritisation aid, not a complete threat model or a frequency table for every population. Use CWE identifiers to describe root weakness classes; do not substitute them for the affected code path, exploit conditions, consequence, or remediation evidence.

17. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and partner agencies. “The Case for Memory Safe Roadmaps” and related Secure by Design guidance, 2023–2025. [CISA memory-safety guidance](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/case-memory-safe-roadmaps). These publications connect persistent memory-safety vulnerabilities to language and product strategy rather than placing the entire burden on downstream patching. They support §19.7's distinction between Python-level memory safety and native extensions. A roadmap should inventory unsafe components, prioritise exposed parsers and privileged code, establish measurable migration stages, and state where mitigations temporarily contain rather than eliminate risk.

18. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Application Container Security Guide*. NIST SP 800-190, 2017. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-190). This guide analyses image, registry, orchestrator, container, and host risks, with countermeasures across the lifecycle. Its enduring lesson is that a container is not a virtual machine and is not an unconditional trust boundary. Apply its guidance together with current platform documentation, because kernel features, runtime defaults, admission controls, signing mechanisms, and orchestration interfaces have continued to evolve.

19. Python Software Foundation. “Security Considerations.” *Python 3.14 Standard Library Documentation*. [Versioned documentation](https://docs.python.org/3/library/security_warnings.html). This compact index identifies modules and behaviours with security-sensitive defaults or caveats, including command execution, archive extraction, randomness, deserialisation, XML processing, and network clients. It is a starting map rather than a complete audit. Follow each link into the exact interpreter-version documentation and determine whether data, code, local environment, or remote peer is untrusted in the system at hand.

20. Python Software Foundation. “`os` — Miscellaneous Operating System Interfaces.” *Python 3.14 Standard Library Documentation*. [Versioned documentation](https://docs.python.org/3/library/os.html). The module exposes process credentials, environment variables, descriptors, directory-relative operations, inheritance, links, flags, and platform-dependent filesystem semantics. It is essential reading for §§19.5, 19.10, and 19.11. Security arguments must be qualified by platform and Python version: the presence, atomicity, link-following behaviour, audit events, and descriptor support of operations are not uniform.

21. Python Software Foundation. “`tempfile` — Generate Temporary Files and Directories.” *Python 3.14 Standard Library Documentation*. [Versioned documentation](https://docs.python.org/3/library/tempfile.html). This module provides race-resistant high-level primitives for temporary resources and documents their deletion and visibility semantics. It demonstrates why predicting a pathname and opening it later is unsafe. Selection still requires care: named versus unnamed files, Windows handle rules, cleanup after abrupt termination, filesystem placement, permissions, capacity limits, and whether another process needs a durable name all affect the correct construction.

22. Python Software Foundation. “`subprocess` — Subprocess Management.” *Python 3.14 Standard Library Documentation*. [Versioned documentation](https://docs.python.org/3/library/subprocess.html). This is the normative Python interface reference for process creation, argument passing, pipes, environments, timeouts, and exit status. Its security notes explain why shell invocation changes the interpretation boundary. A list of arguments prevents shell metacharacter parsing when no shell is used, but does not validate an invoked program's own option grammar, identify the intended executable, constrain child resources, or remove inherited authority.

23. Python Software Foundation. “`tarfile` — Read and Write Tar Archive Files.” *Python 3.14 Standard Library Documentation*. [Versioned documentation](https://docs.python.org/3/library/tarfile.html). The extraction-filter interface and version-specific defaults are central to safe archive handling. Filters reduce common traversal and link risks but cannot supply application quotas, semantic allowlists, collision policy, decompression budgets, antivirus decisions, or trustworthy filenames. Record the exact Python release because historical defaults differ, and treat extraction as a staged transaction into a fresh restricted directory rather than as a single convenience call.

24. Python Software Foundation. “`pickle` — Python Object Serialization.” *Python 3.14 Standard Library Documentation*. [Versioned documentation](https://docs.python.org/3/library/pickle.html). The documentation explicitly characterises pickle data as unsafe when untrusted because reconstruction may execute arbitrary callables. Authentication of a pickle establishes only that a key holder produced the bytes; it does not make future code changes, compromised producers, or excessive resource consumption safe. Prefer constrained data formats and explicit schemas across trust boundaries, and reserve pickle for contexts whose producers already possess equivalent code-execution authority.

25. Python Software Foundation. “XML Vulnerabilities” and the security notes for Python XML modules. *Python 3.14 Standard Library Documentation*. [Versioned documentation](https://docs.python.org/3/library/xml.html#xml-vulnerabilities). The matrix records which modules are exposed to entity expansion, external entity retrieval, decompression, and related behaviours. XML safety is parser- and configuration-specific; “XML” is not one uniform risk. Bound input and expanded output, disable unnecessary features, prevent network and file resolution, and test the actual library version and wrapper path through which documents reach the parser.

26. OWASP Foundation. *Application Security Verification Standard (ASVS)*, Version 5.0. [Official project](https://owasp.org/www-project-application-security-verification-standard/). ASVS turns broad application-security intentions into verifiable requirements grouped by topic and assurance level. It is useful for requirements, architecture review, test planning, procurement, and supplier conversations. It is not proof of compliance by assertion: projects should select an explicit version and level, document applicability and tailoring, map requirements to components and evidence, and preserve failed or partial verification results.

27. OWASP Foundation. *OWASP Top 10: 2025*. [Official project](https://owasp.org/Top10/2025/). The Top 10 communicates prominent web-application risk categories to a broad audience. Categories aggregate many distinct weaknesses and change between editions as evidence and framing evolve. Use the list to prompt investigation and education, not to declare an application secure after ten checks. A mature programme combines it with ASVS, threat modelling, language and framework guidance, dependency evidence, abuse cases, and system-specific authorisation tests.

28. OWASP Foundation. “Input Validation Cheat Sheet.” *OWASP Cheat Sheet Series*. [Maintained source](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Input_Validation_Cheat_Sheet.html). This source distinguishes syntactic from semantic validation and recommends allowlisting, normalisation awareness, length bounds, and validation at trust boundaries. The chapter extends that guidance by insisting on parse–canonicalise–validate–authorise–act ordering. Validation does not replace context-specific output encoding or parameterisation, and a regular expression should not silently become the complete grammar for a rich format.

29. OWASP Foundation. “Deserialization Cheat Sheet.” *OWASP Cheat Sheet Series*. [Maintained source](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Deserialization_Cheat_Sheet.html). The guidance catalogues execution risks associated with native object formats and recommends avoiding untrusted deserialisation, constraining types, authenticating where appropriate, and monitoring relevant APIs. Treat any allowlist as part of a versioned protocol, not a permanent proof: permitted classes may acquire dangerous constructors or hooks, dependency behaviour may change, and resource exhaustion can remain possible without code execution.

30. OWASP Foundation. “Injection Prevention Cheat Sheet.” *OWASP Cheat Sheet Series*. [Maintained source](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Injection_Prevention_Cheat_Sheet.html). The principal defence is to keep code structure separate from untrusted values through safe APIs such as parameterised queries, followed by least privilege and contextual validation. “Escaping” is not a universal operation: SQL, shell, HTML, LDAP, regular expressions, templates, logs, and spreadsheet formulae have different grammars and consumers. Verify the last interpreter boundary rather than sanitising a string once and assuming it is inert everywhere.

31. OWASP Foundation. “File Upload Cheat Sheet.” *OWASP Cheat Sheet Series*. [Maintained source](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/File_Upload_Cheat_Sheet.html). File ingestion composes parser, storage, name, authorisation, malware, quota, serving, and lifecycle risks. The guidance supports isolation, generated names, allowlisted formats, size limits, content inspection, access control, and storage outside directly served paths. The synthesis in §19.32 adds asynchronous quarantine, immutable evidence, bounded workers, and explicit promotion so that “uploaded” never automatically means “trusted and publishable”.

### 19.36.3 Identity, secure development, and supply-chain integrity

32. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Digital Identity Guidelines*. NIST SP 800-63-4, 2025. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-63-4). The umbrella volume defines identity proofing, authentication, and federation assurance concepts and their risk-management context. It should be read with its A, B, and C companion volumes rather than reduced to password rules. An implementer must select assurance levels from actual harms and populations, address privacy and usability, document compensating controls, and preserve interoperability boundaries between credential service providers, verifiers, relying parties, and identity providers.

33. Temoshok, David, Yee-Yin Choong, Andrew Regenscheid, Ryan Galluzzo, James Fenton, and others. *Digital Identity Guidelines: Authentication and Authenticator Management*. NIST SP 800-63B-4, 2025. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-63B-4). This volume specifies authenticator assurance, verifier requirements, phishing resistance, session management, recovery-related considerations, and controls for passwords and cryptographic authenticators. Consult the exact clauses and errata rather than inherited folklore. Its password guidance is one component of an end-to-end lifecycle that also includes enrolment, binding, rate limiting, compromised-authenticator response, reauthentication, notification, and accessible alternatives.

34. World Wide Web Consortium. *Web Authentication: An API for Accessing Public Key Credentials—Level 3*. W3C Recommendation, 25 August 2026. [Normative specification](https://www.w3.org/TR/webauthn-3/). WebAuthn defines origin-scoped public-key credential creation and assertion ceremonies mediated by user agents and authenticators. Its phishing resistance depends on correct relying-party identifiers, origins, challenge freshness, ceremony state, verification, transport security, and account lifecycle. The specification does not decide recovery policy, identity proofing, authorisation, device-loss handling, or whether attestation is appropriate for a particular privacy and threat context.

35. OWASP Foundation. “Authentication Cheat Sheet.” *OWASP Cheat Sheet Series*. [Maintained source](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Authentication_Cheat_Sheet.html). The cheat sheet collects application-level controls for identifiers, responses, password and multifactor flows, reauthentication, session transitions, and sensitive operations. Use it as a review aid alongside NIST SP 800-63B-4 and protocol specifications. Generic error messages can reduce account enumeration, but timing, status codes, recovery behaviour, support channels, and downstream side effects must also be made observationally consistent where the threat warrants it.

36. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Secure Software Development Framework (SSDF) Version 1.1: Recommendations for Mitigating the Risk of Software Vulnerabilities*. NIST SP 800-218, 2022. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-218). SSDF organises practices under preparing the organisation, protecting software, producing well-secured software, and responding to vulnerabilities. It is outcome-oriented so organisations can integrate it into differing lifecycles. Evidence should therefore identify actual repositories, identities, reviews, build controls, tests, release gates, disclosure paths, response actions, and measurements rather than claiming that a named agile or DevOps process inherently satisfies it.

37. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. *Secure by Design* principles and pledges, maintained programme. [Official guidance](https://www.cisa.gov/securebydesign). The programme places responsibility on technology producers to make products secure out of the box, eliminate entire defect classes where feasible, supply logging and multifactor capabilities, and treat customer security outcomes as core product quality. Its value is organisational as well as technical: incentives, default settings, support lifetimes, vulnerability transparency, and executive ownership determine whether downstream users can operate a product safely.

38. Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts project. *SLSA Specification*, Version 1.2. [Normative specification](https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/). SLSA defines models and increasing requirements for trustworthy build provenance and build platforms. A provenance statement is meaningful only when its subject digest, builder identity, invocation, source, dependencies, signing path, and verification policy match the released artifact. SLSA does not establish that source code is non-malicious or that every dependency is safe; it strengthens claims about how an identified artifact was produced.

39. Sigstore Project. “Sigstore Threat Model.” [Maintained project document](https://docs.sigstore.dev/about/security/). The threat model describes assumptions and failure modes across identity-based signing, transparency logs, certificate issuance, clients, and trust roots. It is a valuable example of documenting what a supply-chain system does not protect. Users must verify identity claims and artifact digests under a pinned policy, monitor trust-root changes, handle availability and privacy consequences, and avoid treating the mere presence of a signature as approval.

40. The Update Framework project. *The Update Framework Specification*, Version 1.0.33. [Normative specification](https://theupdateframework.io/spec/v1.0.33/). TUF uses separately scoped metadata roles, thresholds, versions, expiries, and client update rules to remain useful under several repository and key compromises. Its rollback, freeze, mix-and-match, and fast-forward protections illustrate why “download over TLS and check one signature” is an incomplete update design. Deployments must protect offline root authority, rotate and revoke keys, publish consistently, and implement the client state machine exactly.

41. Trail of Bits, Dustin Ingram, and Python packaging contributors. “PEP 740—Index Support for Digital Attestations.” [Official PEP](https://peps.python.org/pep-0740/). PEP 740 specifies how package indexes accept, store, and serve attestations associated with distribution files. It supplies a standard transport and data model, not an automatic trust decision. A consumer still requires policy for attestation type, publisher or builder identity, subject digest, transparency evidence, freshness, revocation, project ownership changes, and what to do when a platform cannot verify a required claim.

42. Python packaging contributors. “PEP 807—Wheel Variants.” [Official PEP](https://peps.python.org/pep-0807/). This document is useful for studying how installable artifacts may be selected for hardware or software properties beyond traditional wheel tags. Security reviewers should attend to selector authority, metadata binding, fallback, cache identity, index behaviour, reproducibility, and native-code attack surface. Because PEP status and implementation support can change, record the adopted revision and distinguish a proposal's design from behaviour actually implemented by a resolver and index.

43. Python Packaging Authority. “`pylock.toml` Specification.” *PyPA Specifications*. [Normative packaging specification](https://packaging.python.org/en/latest/specifications/pylock-toml/). The format represents locked packages, artifacts, hashes, environments, and related resolution data in a tool-independent structure. A lock file narrows resolver variability but is not self-authenticating and does not guarantee that locked code is benign. Review source and index constraints, require expected hashes, protect lock changes, test target environments, and bind the installed artifact set to build provenance and deployment admission evidence.

44. pip maintainers. “Secure Installs” and “Hash-Checking Mode.” *pip Documentation*. [Versioned documentation](https://pip.pypa.io/en/stable/topics/secure-installs/). The guidance explains use of pinned requirements, artifact hashes, binary-only policy where appropriate, and the all-or-nothing discipline of hash-checking mode. It directly supports repeatable dependency installation but does not remove risks in the selected artifact or build environment. Prefer verified wheels when avoiding arbitrary build execution, control every configured package source, and regenerate pins through a reviewed update process rather than accepting opportunistic resolution.

45. Linux Foundation. *SPDX Specification*, Version 3.0.1. [Normative specification](https://spdx.github.io/spdx-spec/v3.0.1/). SPDX defines a machine-readable model for software, licensing, security, build, and provenance information. An SBOM expressed in SPDX is evidence about declared components and relationships, not proof of completeness or vulnerability status. Producers should state namespace, creation information, identity, artifact hashes, relationships, generation method, and omissions; consumers should validate syntax, authenticity, subject identity, freshness, and correspondence to the deployed object.

46. OWASP Foundation. *CycloneDX Specification*, current versioned release. [Normative specification](https://cyclonedx.org/specification/overview/). CycloneDX models bills of materials, compositions, services, dependencies, vulnerabilities, formulation, attestations, and related lifecycle information. Its breadth supports several assurance workflows, but fields become evidence only when populated accurately and bound to a concrete subject. Record the schema version, validate documents, distinguish known absence from unknown completeness, authenticate distribution, and reconcile generated inventories with runtime and deployment reality.

47. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. *Framing Software Component Transparency: Establishing a Common Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)*, third edition, 2024. [Official resource](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/framing-software-component-transparency-2024). This framing document explains baseline SBOM concepts, minimum elements, processes, and use cases across supplier and consumer communities. It is valuable for governance and exchange expectations. An SBOM remains a time-bounded inventory: it must be delivered, protected, discovered, correlated, updated, and acted upon, and it may not describe dynamically loaded, generated, optional, or externally hosted components without additional evidence.

48. Torres-Arias, Santiago, Hammad Afzali, Trishank Karthik Kuppusamy, Reza Curtmola, and Justin Cappos. “in-toto: Providing Farm-to-Table Guarantees for Bits and Bytes.” In *28th USENIX Security Symposium*, 1393–1410, 2019; see also the [in-toto specification](https://in-toto.io/). in-toto models authorised supply-chain steps and signed link metadata so a verifier can test whether an artifact followed an intended layout. It does not judge the semantic correctness of authorised steps. Key custody, functionary identity, threshold policy, material and product rules, inspection isolation, and final-product binding remain part of the assurance case.

49. Reproducible Builds project. *Reproducible Builds Documentation*. [Maintained project documentation](https://reproducible-builds.org/docs/). The project catalogues techniques for making build outputs independently reproducible, including control of timestamps, paths, ordering, locales, archives, and tool inputs. Reproducibility permits comparison against an independently built artifact; it does not prove source correctness and can reproduce a malicious input faithfully. Strong use therefore combines declared inputs, isolated builders, diversity, provenance, signatures, and investigation of every unexplained byte difference.

### 19.36.4 Cryptography and key lifecycle

50. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Secure Hash Standard (SHS)*. FIPS PUB 180-4, 2015, including current notices. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.FIPS.180-4). FIPS 180-4 specifies SHA-1 and the SHA-2 family. Contemporary designs should select approved modern members with sufficient output length and should not infer authentication from an unkeyed digest. The standard defines algorithms; protocol security still requires canonical message framing, domain separation, correct truncation rules, collision and preimage assumptions appropriate to the use, and a migration plan for any algorithm whose approval status changes.

51. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *SHA-3 Standard: Permutation-Based Hash and Extendable-Output Functions*. FIPS PUB 202, 2015. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.FIPS.202). FIPS 202 specifies SHA3-224, SHA3-256, SHA3-384, SHA3-512, SHAKE128, and SHAKE256 using the Keccak permutation. Extendable-output functions require explicit output-length and domain decisions. SHA-3 is not a drop-in excuse to invent constructions: use a standard MAC, KDF, signature prehash mode, or password-hashing design when that is the actual security objective.

52. Krawczyk, Hugo, Mihir Bellare, and Ran Canetti. “HMAC: Keyed-Hashing for Message Authentication.” RFC 2104, 1997. [Normative RFC](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2104). HMAC is a reviewed construction for message authentication from an iterative hash function. Correct use still requires a high-entropy secret key, purpose separation, unambiguous input encoding, an agreed tag length, constant-time verification, and replay controls when freshness matters. Because every verifier shares signing capability, an HMAC does not provide the asymmetric attribution properties expected from a public-key signature.

53. Krawczyk, Hugo, and Pasi Eronen. “HMAC-based Extract-and-Expand Key Derivation Function (HKDF).” RFC 5869, 2010. [Normative RFC](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5869). HKDF separates extraction of concentrated pseudorandom key material from expansion into context-specific output. Salt and `info` have different roles; neither should be improvised as a password-hardening substitute. Protocols should bind version, algorithm, participants, transcript or purpose into documented context and enforce output-length limits. Independent derived keys reduce cross-protocol reuse only when labels are unique and source material has the assumed entropy.

54. Nir, Yoav, and Adam Langley. “ChaCha20 and Poly1305 for IETF Protocols.” RFC 8439, 2018. [Normative RFC](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8439). The RFC specifies the ChaCha20 stream cipher, Poly1305 authenticator, and their AEAD composition, including nonce and counter requirements and test vectors. Implementations should use a maintained high-level AEAD API, not translate the primitive pseudocode into production Python. Nonce reuse under one key destroys essential guarantees; large systems therefore need a construction and state model that make uniqueness dependable across concurrency, retries, crash recovery, and restores.

55. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Recommendation for Block Cipher Modes of Operation: Galois/Counter Mode (GCM) and GMAC*. NIST SP 800-38D, 2007, with current revision notices. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-38D). GCM provides authenticated encryption and GMAC authentication under strict IV and usage requirements. The probability bounds degrade with forgery attempts and processed data, so “AES-256” alone does not state a safe deployment. Systems must count or conservatively bound invocations and data per key, generate nonces correctly, authenticate associated metadata, use adequate tags, and rotate before applicable limits.

56. Biryukov, Alex, Daniel Dinu, Dmitry Khovratovich, and Simon Josefsson. “Argon2 Memory-Hard Function for Password Hashing and Proof-of-Work Applications.” RFC 9106, 2021. [Normative RFC](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9106). RFC 9106 describes Argon2 variants, parameter choices, and rationale, recommending Argon2id for principal password-hashing uses. Parameters are an operational policy: memory, iterations, and parallelism should be benchmarked on the verifier's hardware and revisited as economics change. Per-record salts, encoded parameters, input limits, admission controls, upgrade-on-success, and recovery design remain necessary.

57. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Recommendation for Key Management: Part 1—General*. NIST SP 800-57 Part 1 Rev. 5, 2020. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-57pt1r5). This publication defines key states, cryptoperiods, protection requirements, algorithm and key-length considerations, compromise handling, archival, recovery, destruction, and organisational responsibilities. It reveals why selecting an algorithm is a small part of cryptographic engineering. Map every key to owner, purpose, environment, generation method, allowed operations, storage boundary, backup, rotation, revocation, logging, dependency, and tested recovery path.

58. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Recommendation for Random Number Generation Using Deterministic Random Bit Generators*. NIST SP 800-90A Rev. 1, 2015. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-90Ar1). The standard specifies approved DRBG mechanisms and their instantiate, reseed, generate, and uninstantiate operations. Application programmers ordinarily rely on the operating system and `secrets`, but the model explains why entropy acquisition, internal state, fork behaviour, reseeding, health, failure, and backtracking resistance matter. Replacing this stack with a familiar pseudorandom generator silently converts secret generation into prediction.

59. Josefsson, Simon, and Ilari Liusvaara. “Edwards-Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (EdDSA).” RFC 8032, 2017. [Normative RFC](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8032). RFC 8032 specifies Ed25519, Ed448, their prehash variants, encodings, and test vectors. Signatures authenticate exact bytes under a public key; they do not define who should trust that key, what the signed bytes mean, or whether an artifact is safe. Protocols must define canonical representation, context, key identifier, version, anti-replay state, public-key distribution, revocation, and acceptance policy.

60. Rescorla, Eric. “The Transport Layer Security (TLS) Protocol Version 1.3.” RFC 8446, 2018, with applicable errata. [Normative RFC](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8446). TLS 1.3 integrates authenticated key exchange, transcript binding, key derivation, record protection, and negotiated algorithms. Correct deployment requires trustworthy endpoint identity, hostname validation, certificate and trust-anchor lifecycle, current libraries, and guarded configuration. TLS protects a channel between authenticated endpoints under its assumptions; it does not validate application payloads, authorise principals, protect compromised endpoints, or make early data replay-safe by itself.

61. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism Standard*. FIPS PUB 203, 2024. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.FIPS.203). FIPS 203 specifies ML-KEM parameter sets for post-quantum key establishment. Migration is a protocol and lifecycle problem, not a textual replacement of “RSA” with “ML-KEM”: implementations must consider public-key and ciphertext sizes, randomness, decapsulation behaviour, side channels, certificates, negotiation, downgrade resistance, inventories, interoperability, hardware, and hybrid-transition policy. Use validated, maintained implementations and the standard's exact encodings and checks.

62. National Institute of Standards and Technology. *Recommendations for Key-Encapsulation Mechanisms*. NIST SP 800-227, 2025. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-227). This guide explains KEM properties, interfaces, approved use, shared-secret handling, validation, and protocol integration, including distinctions between raw KEM output and keys derived for applications. It provides the conceptual bridge between an algorithm standard and safe construction. A KEM alone does not authenticate a peer or bind a complete transcript; those properties must arise from the enclosing protocol and key-derivation context.

63. Python Cryptographic Authority. “Authenticated Encryption.” *cryptography Hazmat Primitives Documentation*, version corresponding to the deployed package. [Maintained documentation](https://cryptography.io/en/latest/hazmat/primitives/aead/). The AEAD interfaces document key and nonce sizes, associated data, ciphertext/tag representation, limits, and failure exceptions for AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305 families. “Hazardous materials” is a warning about composition, not merely syntax. Prefer a protocol-level or high-level envelope library when available; otherwise pin the package version and test nonce management, malformed envelopes, metadata binding, rotation, and authentication failure uniformly.

64. Python Cryptographic Authority. “Key Derivation Functions.” *cryptography Hazmat Primitives Documentation*, version corresponding to the deployed package. [Maintained documentation](https://cryptography.io/en/latest/hazmat/primitives/key-derivation-functions/). The documentation covers HKDF and password-oriented KDFs such as PBKDF2, scrypt, and Argon2id where supported, including parameter and API constraints. Choose a function according to source material and purpose: extracting protocol keys from high-entropy material differs from resisting offline password guesses. Store algorithm and parameters with verifiers or envelopes, set explicit input and resource bounds, and plan upgrades.

65. Python Software Foundation. “`secrets` — Generate Secure Random Numbers for Managing Secrets.” *Python 3.14 Standard Library Documentation*. [Versioned documentation](https://docs.python.org/3/library/secrets.html). `secrets` exposes operating-system-backed generation, convenient token encodings, and comparison support for security-sensitive values. Token strength is governed by entropy before encoding, not visual length alone. A bearer token must additionally have a single purpose, audience, short lifetime, protected transport and storage, non-logging policy, revocation or atomic consumption where needed, and a safely stored verifier rather than unnecessary plaintext retention.

### 19.36.5 Vulnerability management, assurance, and incident response

66. Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams. *Common Vulnerability Scoring System Version 4.0: Specification Document*, 2023. [Official specification](https://www.first.org/cvss/v4-0/specification-document). CVSS v4.0 separates Base, Threat, Environmental, and Supplemental metric groups and specifies vector construction and scoring. A Base score is not an enterprise remediation deadline. Apply Threat and Environmental information using documented evidence, preserve the vector rather than only its decimal score, and combine it with asset criticality, reachability, deployed configuration, control effectiveness, exploit evidence, safety consequences, and remediation risk.

67. Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams. *Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS): Model and Data*. [Official project](https://www.first.org/epss/). EPSS estimates the probability that a published CVE will be exploited in the wild during a stated future interval, using a changing statistical model and observed features. It is probabilistic prioritisation evidence, not a vulnerability severity measure or guarantee. Record the date and model version of a score, consider calibration and population, and never infer that low probability makes catastrophic exposed impact acceptable indefinitely.

68. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. *Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog*. [Official catalogue](https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog). KEV records vulnerabilities for which CISA has evidence of exploitation and provides required actions and dates for in-scope United States federal civilian agencies. Other organisations can use it as strong threat evidence, but absence is not evidence of non-exploitation. Match products and versions carefully, monitor catalogue changes, assess local exposure and compensating controls, and verify remediation rather than closing a ticket when a patch command is issued.

69. Souppaya, Murugiah, and Karen Scarfone. *Guide to Enterprise Patch Management Planning: Preventive Maintenance for an Organization's Technology*. NIST SP 800-40 Rev. 4, 2022. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-40r4). This guide frames patching as preventive maintenance supported by enterprise strategy, inventories, prioritisation, testing, deployment, verification, exception handling, and metrics. It helps reconcile security urgency with service and safety constraints. Patch management should cover firmware, appliances, libraries, images, build tools, and ephemeral instances, while proving that vulnerable artifacts cannot silently return through rollback or redeployment.

70. Nelson, Alexander, Sanjay Rekhi, Murugiah Souppaya, and Karen Scarfone. *Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cybersecurity Risk Management: A CSF 2.0 Community Profile*. NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3, 2025. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-61r3). Revision 3 integrates incident response across all six CSF 2.0 functions rather than presenting it as an isolated linear phase. It supports preparation, governance, continuous improvement, detection, analysis, containment, eradication, recovery, communication, and learning. Tailor playbooks to system dependencies and decision authority, exercise them, and define evidence-based recovery gates before crisis conditions.

71. Kent, Karen, Suzanne Chevalier, Tim Grance, and Hung Dang. *Guide to Integrating Forensic Techniques into Incident Response*. NIST SP 800-86, 2006. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-86). The guide covers collection, examination, analysis, and reporting across files, operating systems, network traffic, and applications. Although particular tools and platforms have aged, its disciplined distinction between evidence acquisition and interpretation remains fundamental. Cloud, container, mobile, encrypted, and volatile environments require updated procedures, but provenance, integrity checks, time sources, chain of custody, reproducibility, legal authority, and stated limitations remain indispensable.

72. Scarfone, Karen, Murugiah Souppaya, Amanda Cody, and Angela Orebaugh. *Technical Guide to Information Security Testing and Assessment*. NIST SP 800-115, 2008. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-115). This guide covers review, target identification, vulnerability validation, penetration testing, planning, execution, and reporting. Its strongest contribution is methodological: tests require objectives, authorisation, rules of engagement, logistics, data handling, stop conditions, and remediation verification. Because tools and attack surfaces evolve, combine its process discipline with current technique sources and never interpret an automated scanner's output as a completed assessment.

73. OWASP Foundation. *Web Security Testing Guide*, stable Version 4.2 and current versioned development material. [Official project](https://owasp.org/www-project-web-security-testing-guide/). WSTG supplies a systematic catalogue of web-application test objectives spanning configuration, identity, authentication, authorisation, sessions, validation, cryptography, business logic, clients, and APIs. Select cases from the application's data flows and threat model, record the guide version, and add system-specific abuse cases. Passing generic tests cannot establish correct tenant isolation, workflow integrity, availability, or controls outside the web tier.

74. Miller, Barton P., Lars Fredriksen, and Bryan So. “An Empirical Study of the Reliability of UNIX Utilities.” *Communications of the ACM* 33, no. 12 (1990): 32–44. [doi:10.1145/96267.96279](https://doi.org/10.1145/96267.96279). This foundational empirical study used streams of random and mutated input to expose crashes and hangs in widely deployed utilities. It demonstrates the disproportionate value of simple automated adversarial input generation and observable failure oracles. Modern fuzzing adds coverage guidance, structure awareness, sanitizers, corpus management, minimisation, parallelism, and differential properties, but still depends on bounded harnesses and durable regression tests.

75. Bartock, Michael, Jeffrey Cichonski, Murugiah Souppaya, Matthew Smith, Greg Witte, and Karen Scarfone. *Guide for Cybersecurity Event Recovery*. NIST SP 800-184, 2016. [Official publication](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-184). The guide develops recovery planning, playbooks, testing, improvement, communications, and metrics for restoring organisational functions after cybersecurity events. Recovery is not equivalent to restarting from a backup: responders must establish a clean trust basis, understand persistence and vulnerable configuration, rotate affected authority, validate restored invariants, reconcile lost transactions, monitor recurrence, and communicate residual uncertainty to decision-makers.

These sources should be read comparatively. A control catalogue states candidate outcomes; a language or library reference states actual interface behaviour; a protocol standard fixes message and algorithm rules; an adversary corpus prompts detection hypotheses; and empirical testing supplies evidence about one implementation under one environment. None alone proves system security. The scholarly habit required here is therefore triangulation: state the claim, identify the governing model and versioned normative source, test the concrete mechanism, record contrary evidence and limitations, and revise the residual-risk decision when any premise changes.
