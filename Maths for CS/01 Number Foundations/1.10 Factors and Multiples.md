## Introduction

In [[1.9 Order of Operations]], you combined all four arithmetic operations in a single expression. Now you will look at the structure that multiplication creates inside whole numbers.

Factors, multiples, and primes describe the multiplication structure of whole numbers.

```text
3 x 4 = 12
```

So:
- 3 and 4 are **factors** of 12
- 12 is a **multiple** of 3
- 12 is a **multiple** of 4

**Prime numbers** are numbers with exactly two positive factors: 1 and themselves. **Composite numbers** have more than two.

This topic introduces:
- [[factor]] — a whole number that divides exactly into another
- [[multiple]] — a number made by multiplying a whole number by another
- [[prime number]] — a whole number > 1 with exactly two factors
- [[composite number]] — a whole number > 1 with more than two factors
- [[prime factorisation]] — writing a number as a product of primes

> [!example] Everyday idea
> If 24 students can be split into groups of 6 without leftovers, then 6 is a factor of 24 — the groups are equal. If you stack 6 rows of chairs, the total chairs is a multiple of 6.

## New Words

- [[factor]] — a whole number that divides exactly into another whole number.
- [[multiple]] — a number made by multiplying a whole number by another whole number.
- [[prime number]] — a whole number greater than 1 with exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself.
- [[composite number]] — a whole number greater than 1 with more than two positive factors.
- [[prime factorisation]] — writing a number as a multiplication of prime factors.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Factors

**Builds on:** [[1.4 Breadcrumb 1|Multiplication as Equal Groups]] (factors are group sizes that fit exactly), [[1.5 Breadcrumb 4|Remainders]] (a factor divides with remainder 0)

A [[factor]] divides into a number with no remainder.

Example:

```text
12 / 3 = 4
```

There is no remainder, so 3 is a factor of 12.

Also:

```text
3 x 4 = 12
```

So 3 and 4 are both factors of 12.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Is 5 a factor of 35?

Step 1: Divide 35 by 5.

```text
35 / 5 = 7
```

Step 2: Check for a remainder.

```text
remainder = 0
```

Answer: Yes, 5 is a factor of 35.

---

### Breadcrumb 2: Factor Pairs

**Builds on:** [[1.10 Breadcrumb 1|Factors]] (pairs are two factors that multiply to the target)

Factor pairs are two numbers that multiply to make the target number.

Factor pairs of 24:

```text
1 x 24 = 24
2 x 12 = 24
3 x 8 = 24
4 x 6 = 24
```

So the factors of 24 are:

```text
1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24
```

> [!tip] Stop when pairs repeat
> Once the smaller factor passes the larger factor, you are repeating pairs.

---

### Breadcrumb 3: Multiples

**Builds on:** [[1.4 Breadcrumb 1|Multiplication as Equal Groups]] (multiples are the result of repeated groups), [[1.10 Breadcrumb 1|Factors]] (factors and multiples are opposite views)

A [[multiple]] is what you get by multiplying.

Multiples of 6:

```text
6 x 1 = 6
6 x 2 = 12
6 x 3 = 18
6 x 4 = 24
6 x 5 = 30
```

So the first multiples of 6 are:

```text
6, 12, 18, 24, 30, ...
```

Multiples continue forever.

#### Chunked Example
Question: List the first five multiples of 8.

Step 1: Multiply 8 by 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5.

```text
8 x 1 = 8
8 x 2 = 16
8 x 3 = 24
8 x 4 = 32
8 x 5 = 40
```

Answer:

```text
8, 16, 24, 32, 40
```

---

### Breadcrumb 4: Factors vs Multiples

**Builds on:** [[1.10 Breadcrumb 1|Factors]] and [[1.10 Breadcrumb 3|Multiples]] (they are different questions about the same relationship)

Factors are usually smaller than or equal to the number.

Multiples are usually larger than or equal to the number.

For 12:

```text
factors:   1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
multiples: 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, ...
```

> [!warning] Common mix-up
> A factor goes into a number. A multiple is built from a number.

---

### Breadcrumb 5: Divisibility Checks

**Builds on:** [[1.10 Breadcrumb 1|Factors]] (checks help you find factors quickly), [[1.9 Breadcrumb 4|Multiplication and Division Before Addition and Subtraction]] (digit sum uses addition)

Divisibility checks help you test factors quickly.

Common checks:

- divisible by 2: last digit is even
- divisible by 3: digit sum is divisible by 3
- divisible by 5: last digit is 0 or 5
- divisible by 10: last digit is 0

#### Chunked Example
Question: Is 84 divisible by 3?

Step 1: Add the digits.

```text
8 + 4 = 12
```

Step 2: Check whether 12 is divisible by 3.

```text
12 / 3 = 4
```

Answer: Yes, 84 is divisible by 3.

---

### Breadcrumb 6: Prime Numbers

**Builds on:** [[1.10 Breadcrumb 1|Factors]] (primes have exactly two factors), [[1.5 Breadcrumb 4|Remainders]] (division by a prime always leaves a remainder unless you use 1 or the number itself)

A [[prime number]] has exactly two positive factors:

```text
1 and itself
```

Examples:

```text
2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19
```

2 is the only even prime number. Every other even number is divisible by 2 and has more than two factors.

> [!important] 1 is not prime
> 1 has only one positive factor: 1. Prime numbers must have exactly two positive factors.

---

### Breadcrumb 7: Composite Numbers

**Builds on:** [[1.10 Breadcrumb 6|Prime Numbers]] (composites are the opposite), [[1.10 Breadcrumb 2|Factor Pairs]] (composites have factor pairs beyond 1 and itself)

A [[composite number]] has more than two positive factors.

Examples:

```text
4 = 1 x 4 and 2 x 2
6 = 1 x 6 and 2 x 3
12 = 1 x 12, 2 x 6, 3 x 4
```

Composite numbers can be broken into smaller factors.

---

### Breadcrumb 8: Prime Factorisation

**Builds on:** [[1.10 Breadcrumb 6|Prime Numbers]] and [[1.10 Breadcrumb 7|Composite Numbers]] (break composites into prime building blocks), [[1.4 Breadcrumb 5|Times Tables Are Stored Facts]] (factor pairs come from times tables)

[[prime factorisation]] breaks a number into prime building blocks.

Example:

```text
60 = 2 x 2 x 3 x 5
```

All the factors on the right are prime.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Find the prime factorisation of 84.

Step 1: Split 84 into any factor pair.

```text
84 = 2 x 42
```

Step 2: Keep splitting composite factors.

```text
42 = 2 x 21
21 = 3 x 7
```

Step 3: Write all prime factors.

```text
84 = 2 x 2 x 3 x 7
```

Answer:

```text
84 = 2^2 x 3 x 7
```

The power notation is optional here, but it prepares you for [[2.9 Indices and Powers]].

---

### Breadcrumb 9: Why CS Cares About Primes

**Builds on:** [[1.10 Breadcrumb 8|Prime Factorisation]] (the difficulty of factorisation is a foundation of security), [[1.5 Breadcrumb 4|Remainders]] (modular arithmetic with primes)

Prime numbers matter in computer science because they interact with division and remainders in useful ways.

Examples:

- cryptography uses large prime numbers
- hash tables often use prime-sized buckets
- random-looking cycles can be improved with coprime sizes
- factorisation difficulty supports security assumptions

> [!example] CS connection
> RSA cryptography uses the fact that multiplying two large primes is easy, but recovering the original primes from the product can be extremely hard for large enough numbers.

## Why This Works

Multiplication creates structure inside whole numbers.

If a number can be arranged into equal groups, the group size is a factor and the total is a multiple.

Prime numbers are the building blocks because they cannot be broken into smaller whole-number factors except 1 and themselves.

This prepares you for [[1.12 Exponents and Roots]], where factors multiply repeatedly, and for [[6.3 Modular Arithmetic]], where factor structure is used to manage remainders.

## Worked Examples

For fully worked solutions, use [[1.10 Factors and Multiples - Worked Examples]].

### Example 1 — Factors

Question: List all the factors of 18.

Step 1: Find factor pairs that multiply to 18.

```text
1 x 18 = 18
2 x 9 = 18
3 x 6 = 18
```

Step 2: Stop when pairs would repeat.

Answer: **1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18**.

### Example 2 — Multiples

Question: List the first five multiples of 7.

Step 1: Multiply 7 by 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5.

```text
7 x 1 = 7
7 x 2 = 14
7 x 3 = 21
7 x 4 = 28
7 x 5 = 35
```

Answer: **7, 14, 21, 28, 35**.

### Example 3 — Prime or Composite

Question: Is 11 prime or composite?

Step 1: Find all factors of 11.

```text
11 = 1 x 11
```

Step 2: Count the factors.

```text
11 has exactly 2 factors: 1 and 11.
```

Answer: **11 is prime**.

### Example 4 — Prime Factorisation

Question: Find the prime factorisation of 72.

Step 1: Split 72 into a factor pair.

```text
72 = 2 x 36
```

Step 2: Keep splitting composite factors.

```text
36 = 2 x 18
18 = 2 x 9
9 = 3 x 3
```

Step 3: Write all prime factors.

```text
72 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 3 x 3
```

Answer: **72 = 2³ x 3²**.

## Common Mistakes

- Mixing up factors and multiples.
- Saying 1 is prime.
- Forgetting that 2 is prime.
- Stopping a factor list too early.
- Listing repeated factors as if they are new factors.
- Thinking every odd number is prime. For example, 9 and 15 are odd but composite.
- Writing a prime factorisation that still contains a composite number.

## Where This Shows Up in CS

Factors, multiples, and primes appear in:

- cryptography
- hashing
- modular arithmetic
- memory alignment
- scheduling cycles
- random number generation
- algorithm optimisation
- compression and coding theory

Example:

```text
bucket = hash_value remainder table_size
```

Choosing a good table size often depends on factor and remainder behaviour.
