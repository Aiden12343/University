## Introduction

In [[1.10 Factors and Multiples]] and [[1.11 HCF and LCM]], you explored how numbers relate through factors and multiples. Relationships between quantities are the focus here — how they compare (ratio), how they scale together (proportion), and what they measure (units).

A **ratio** compares two quantities. **Proportion** keeps relationships the same while scaling. **Units** tell you what a number measures.

```text
red : blue = 2 : 3
```

This means for every 2 red items, there are 3 blue items. Scaled up:

```text
2 : 3 = 4 : 6 = 20 : 30
```

The relationship stays the same.

This topic introduces:
- [[ratio notation]] — writing a comparison between quantities
- [[unit rates]] — rates written per one unit
- [[direct proportion]] — when two quantities scale by the same factor
- [[scaling recipes]] — multiplying all parts by the same factor
- [[unit conversion]] — rewriting a measurement in different units

> [!example] Everyday idea
> If a program processes 240 files in 4 minutes, the unit rate is 60 files per minute. That tells you the speed clearly regardless of total time.

## New Words

- [[ratio notation]] — writing a comparison between quantities, such as `2:3`.
- [[unit rates]] — rates written per one unit, such as 60 miles per 1 hour.
- [[direct proportion]] — when one quantity scales by the same factor as another.
- [[scaling recipes]] — multiplying all parts by the same factor to keep a ratio unchanged.
- [[unit conversion]] — rewriting a measurement in different units without changing the amount.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Ratio Notation

**Builds on:** [[1.10 Breadcrumb 1|Factors]] (ratios simplify using HCF), [[1.10 Breadcrumb 4|Factors vs Multiples]] (ratio compares quantities)

A ratio compares quantities.

```text
2 : 5
```

reads as:

```text
2 to 5
```

Example:

```text
2 red counters : 5 blue counters
```

The order matters. `2:5` is not the same description as `5:2`.

> [!warning] Order matters
> If the question says red to blue, write red first and blue second.

---

### Breadcrumb 2: Simplifying Ratios

**Builds on:** [[1.11 Breadcrumb 2|Highest Common Factor]] (HCF simplifies ratios), [[1.12 Breadcrumb 1|Ratio Notation]] (ratios can be simplified like fractions)

Ratios can be simplified like fractions by dividing all parts by the same factor.

Question: Simplify `12:18`.

Step 1: Find the HCF.

```text
HCF(12, 18) = 6
```

Step 2: Divide both parts by 6.

```text
12 / 6 = 2
18 / 6 = 3
```

Answer:

```text
12:18 = 2:3
```

---

### Breadcrumb 3: Scaling Ratios

**Builds on:** [[1.12 Breadcrumb 1|Ratio Notation]] (multiplying all parts preserves the relationship), [[1.4 Breadcrumb 1|Multiplication as Equal Groups]] (scaling a ratio is scaling groups)

To scale a ratio, multiply every part by the same factor.

Example:

```text
2:3
```

Scale by 4:

```text
2 x 4 : 3 x 4 = 8:12
```

So:

```text
2:3 = 8:12
```

This is why ratios connect closely to [[equivalent fractions]].

---

### Breadcrumb 4: Sharing in a Ratio

**Builds on:** [[1.12 Breadcrumb 1|Ratio Notation]] (ratio tells you the relative shares), [[1.5 Breadcrumb 1|Division as Sharing Equally]] (dividing into parts)

Question: Share 50 in the ratio `2:3`.

Step 1: Add the ratio parts.

```text
2 + 3 = 5 parts
```

Step 2: Find one part.

```text
50 / 5 = 10
```

Step 3: Scale each ratio part.

```text
2 parts = 2 x 10 = 20
3 parts = 3 x 10 = 30
```

Answer:

```text
20 and 30
```

Check:

```text
20 + 30 = 50
```

### Breadcrumb 5: Unit Rates

**Builds on:** [[1.5 Breadcrumb 1|Division as Sharing Equally]] (divide to find per-one amount), [[1.7 Breadcrumb 6|Finding a Percentage of an Amount]] (rates are fractions of a total per unit time)

A [[unit rates|unit rate]] says how much happens per 1 unit.

Examples:

```text
60 miles per hour
100 requests per second
5 pounds per kilogram
```

Question: A program processes 240 files in 4 minutes. What is the rate per minute?

Step 1: Divide total files by total minutes.

```text
240 / 4 = 60
```

Answer:

```text
60 files per minute
```

> [!example] CS connection
> Throughput is often a unit rate, such as requests per second or records per minute.

---

### Breadcrumb 6: Direct Proportion

**Builds on:** [[1.12 Breadcrumb 5|Unit Rates]] (unit rate tells you the constant multiplier), [[1.4 Breadcrumb 1|Multiplication as Equal Groups]] (scaling one quantity scales the other)

[[direct proportion]] means two quantities scale together by the same factor.

Example:

```text
3 notebooks cost 6 pounds
6 notebooks cost 12 pounds
9 notebooks cost 18 pounds
```

Double the notebooks, double the cost.

Question: If 5 GB costs 10 pounds, how much does 8 GB cost at the same rate?

Step 1: Find cost per GB.

```text
10 / 5 = 2 pounds per GB
```

Step 2: Multiply by 8.

```text
8 x 2 = 16
```

Answer: 8 GB costs **16 pounds**.

---

### Breadcrumb 7: Unit Conversion

**Builds on:** [[1.2 Breadcrumb 2|Position Gives Value]] (place value shifts when converting between metric units), [[1.7 Breadcrumb 4|Converting Fractions to Decimals]] (conversions use multiplication/division by powers of 10)

[[unit conversion]] rewrites a measurement in another unit.

Common metric conversions:

```text
1 kilometre = 1000 metres
1 metre = 100 centimetres
1 kilogram = 1000 grams
1 second = 1000 milliseconds
1 minute = 60 seconds
1 hour = 60 minutes
```

Question: Convert 3 seconds to milliseconds.

```text
1 second = 1000 milliseconds
3 seconds = 3 x 1000 = 3000 milliseconds
```

Answer: 3000 ms.

### Breadcrumb 8: Units in Computer Science

**Builds on:** [[1.12 Breadcrumb 7|Unit Conversion]] (CS uses many standard units), [[1.2 Breadcrumb 2|Position Gives Value]] (metric prefixes are place-value shifts)

Computer Science uses many units:

- bytes, kilobytes, megabytes, gigabytes
- milliseconds and seconds
- requests per second
- frames per second
- pixels
- bits per second
- operations per second

For this foundation course, use simple decimal approximations unless a later note says otherwise:

```text
1 KB about 1000 bytes
1 MB about 1000 KB
1 GB about 1000 MB
```

Binary units are handled more carefully in [[10.3 Binary Hexadecimal and Number Bases]].

### Breadcrumb 9: Screen Scaling

**Builds on:** [[1.12 Breadcrumb 3|Scaling Ratios]] (screen resolution is a ratio), [[1.11 Breadcrumb 2|Highest Common Factor]] (simplify by the HCF)

Ratios describe screen shapes.

Example:

```text
1920:1080
```

Simplify by HCF 120:

```text
1920 / 120 = 16
1080 / 120 = 9
```

So:

```text
1920:1080 = 16:9
```

That is a common widescreen ratio.

---

## Why This Works

Ratios work because multiplying or dividing every part by the same non-zero number preserves the relationship.

$$
2:3 = 4:6 = 20:30
$$

Unit rates work because they standardise comparison to one unit.

If two computers process different numbers of records in different times, converting both to records per second makes the comparison fair.

## Worked Examples

For fully worked solutions, use [[1.12 Ratio Proportion and Units - Worked Examples]].

### Example 1 — Simplify Ratio

Question: Simplify the ratio `18:24`.

Step 1: Find the HCF of 18 and 24.

```text
HCF(18, 24) = 6
```

Step 2: Divide both parts by 6.

```text
18 / 6 = 3
24 / 6 = 4
```

Answer: **`18:24 = 3:4`**.

### Example 2 — Share in a Ratio

Question: Share 80 in the ratio `3:5`.

Step 1: Add the ratio parts.

```text
3 + 5 = 8 parts
```

Step 2: Find the value of one part.

```text
80 / 8 = 10
```

Step 3: Multiply each ratio part.

```text
3 parts = 3 x 10 = 30
5 parts = 5 x 10 = 50
```

Answer: **30 and 50**.

### Example 3 — Unit Rate

Question: A server handles 500 requests in 5 seconds. What is the rate per second?

Step 1: Divide total requests by total time.

```text
500 / 5 = 100
```

Answer: **100 requests/second**.

## Common Mistakes

- Reversing the order of a ratio.
- Adding or subtracting the same amount from ratio parts instead of multiplying/dividing.
- Simplifying only one part of a ratio.
- Forgetting units.
- Comparing rates without converting to the same unit.
- Treating all storage units as exact powers of 1000 in contexts where binary units are required.
- Rounding too early in a rate calculation.

## Where This Shows Up in CS

Ratio, proportion, and units appear in:

- performance benchmarks
- throughput
- latency
- memory and storage sizes
- network speed
- image resolution
- screen scaling
- data compression
- train/test splits in machine learning

Example:

```text
requests = 1200
time = 10 seconds
rate = 1200 / 10 = 120 requests/second
```
