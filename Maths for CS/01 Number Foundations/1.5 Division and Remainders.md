## Introduction

In [[1.4 Multiplication and Times Tables]], you learned to combine equal groups quickly. Division reverses that process: it splits a quantity into equal parts or equal groups.

Division answers two different kinds of questions:

1. **Sharing:** If I share this fairly, how many does each person get?
2. **Grouping:** How many equal groups of a given size can I make?

Sometimes division does not split exactly. The amount left over is a [[remainders|remainder]].

This topic introduces:
- [[sharing equally]] — splitting a total so each person gets the same amount
- [[grouping]] — counting how many equal groups fit into a total
- [[inverse of multiplication]] — division undoes multiplication
- [[remainders]] — what is left after making as many equal groups as possible
- [[division by 10 and 100]] — using place value to scale a number down

> [!example] Everyday idea
> If 14 sweets are shared between 4 people, each person gets 3 sweets and 2 sweets are left over. That leftover amount is the remainder.

## New Words

- [[sharing equally]] — splitting a total so each person or group receives the same amount.
- [[grouping]] — making equal-size groups from a total and counting how many groups fit.
- [[remainders]] — amounts left over after making equal groups.
- [[inverse of multiplication]] — the idea that division undoes multiplication.
- [[division by 10 and 100]] — using place value to scale a number down by ten or one hundred.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Division as Sharing Equally

**Builds on:** [[1.4 Breadcrumb 1|Multiplication as Equal Groups]] (you already know equal groups; now you start with the total and split it)

Sharing asks:

> How many does each group get?

Example:

```text
12 sweets shared between 3 people
```

Visual form:

```text
Person A: * * * *
Person B: * * * *
Person C: * * * *
```

Each person gets 4.

```text
12 / 3 = 4
```

#### Chunked Example
Question: Share 15 counters equally between 5 people.

Step 1: Identify the total.

```text
15 counters
```

Step 2: Identify the number of people.

```text
5 people
```

Step 3: Find how many each person gets.

```text
15 / 5 = 3
```

Answer: Each person gets **3 counters**.

---

### Breadcrumb 2: Division as Grouping

**Builds on:** [[1.4 Breadcrumb 1|Multiplication as Equal Groups]] (groups have a known size; we count how many fit)

Grouping asks:

> How many groups of this size can I make?

Example:

```text
12 sweets grouped into groups of 3
```

Visual form:

```text
***   ***   ***   ***
 3     3     3     3
```

There are 4 groups.

```text
12 / 3 = 4
```

#### Chunked Example
Question: You have 20 files. Each folder holds 5 files. How many folders are needed?

Step 1: Identify the total.

```text
20 files
```

Step 2: Identify the group size.

```text
5 files per folder
```

Step 3: Count how many groups of 5 fit into 20.

```text
20 / 5 = 4
```

Answer: You need **4 folders**.

---

### Breadcrumb 3: Division Undoes Multiplication

**Builds on:** [[1.4 Breadcrumb 1|Multiplication as Equal Groups]] (division reverses the equal-group process)

Division is the [[inverse of multiplication]].

If:

```text
4 x 6 = 24
```

then:

```text
24 / 6 = 4
```

and:

```text
24 / 4 = 6
```

This gives a reliable checking method.

> [!important] Division fact family
> One multiplication fact gives related division facts.

```text
7 x 8 = 56
56 / 8 = 7
56 / 7 = 8
```

### Breadcrumb 4: Remainders

**Builds on:** [[1.5 Breadcrumb 1|Division as Sharing Equally]] (sharing does not always work out evenly), [[1.5 Breadcrumb 2|Division as Grouping]] (a group may be incomplete)

A [[remainders|remainder]] is what is left after making as many equal groups as possible.

Example:

```text
17 / 5
```

How many groups of 5 fit into 17?

```text
5 + 5 + 5 = 15
```

Three groups fit. There are 2 left over.

```text
17 / 5 = 3 remainder 2
```

Short form:

```text
17 / 5 = 3 r2
```

#### Chunked Example
Question: Divide 23 by 4.

Step 1: Find the nearest lower multiple of 4.

```text
4 x 5 = 20
4 x 6 = 24
```

24 is too high, so use 20.

Step 2: Count the groups.

```text
20 / 4 = 5 groups
```

Step 3: Find the leftover.

```text
23 - 20 = 3
```

Answer:

```text
23 / 4 = 5 r3
```

> [!warning] Remainder size rule
> The remainder must be smaller than the divisor. In `23 / 4`, a remainder of 7 would be impossible because another group of 4 could still be made.

### Breadcrumb 5: Checking Division with Multiplication

**Builds on:** [[1.5 Breadcrumb 3|Division Undoes Multiplication]] (the inverse relationship gives the check), [[1.5 Breadcrumb 4|Remainders]] (the check includes the leftover)

To check:

```text
23 / 4 = 5 r3
```

use:

```text
(divisor x quotient) + remainder = original number
```

So:

```text
(4 x 5) + 3 = 23
```

The check works.

> [!tip] Division check formula
> Original number = divisor x quotient + remainder.

In symbols:

$$
n = dq + r
$$

where:

- `n` is the original number
- `d` is the divisor
- `q` is the quotient
- `r` is the remainder

You do not need to memorise the symbols yet, but this exact idea returns in [[6.3 Modular Arithmetic]].

### Breadcrumb 6: Division by 10 and 100

**Builds on:** [[1.4 Breadcrumb 6|Multiplying by 10 and 100]] (the reverse operation), [[1.2 Breadcrumb 2|Position Gives Value]] (digits shift right in the place-value table)

Dividing by 10 moves digits one place-value column to the right.

```text
230 / 10 = 23
```

Dividing by 100 moves digits two place-value columns to the right.

```text
2300 / 100 = 23
```

> [!warning] Do not only say "remove a zero"
> That shortcut works for some whole numbers, but place value is the real reason. It becomes unsafe when decimals arrive.

### Breadcrumb 7: Division in Computer Science

**Builds on:** [[1.5 Breadcrumb 4|Remainders]] (remainders drive many CS applications)

Division and remainders appear constantly in programming.

Examples:

- putting items into pages
- splitting work across processors
- finding row and column positions in a grid
- wrapping around a circular list
- checking whether a number is even
- hashing data into buckets

#### Pagination Example
Question: A website shows 10 results per page. There are 47 results. How many full pages are there, and how many results are left?

```text
47 / 10 = 4 r7
```

Answer: There are 4 full pages and 7 results left for another page.

In a real website, that means you need 5 pages total.

## Why This Works

Division works because multiplication builds equal groups, and division takes equal groups apart.

If:

```text
6 x 4 = 24
```

then 24 can be split into 6 groups of 4 or 4 groups of 6.

Remainders happen when the original number is not exactly a multiple of the group size.

```text
26 / 6 = 4 r2
```

because:

```text
6 x 4 = 24
24 + 2 = 26
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing sharing and grouping questions.
- Forgetting that division reverses multiplication.
- Writing a remainder that is larger than the divisor.
- Rounding a remainder away when the problem needs the leftover.
- Forgetting that some real situations need an extra group, such as an extra page or box.
- Saying "remove a zero" without understanding division by 10 or 100.

---

## Where This Shows Up in CS

Division and remainders appear in:

- pagination
- chunking arrays
- distributing jobs across workers
- modulo arithmetic
- hash tables
- circular buffers
- grid coordinates
- even/odd tests

Example:

```text
item_count = 47
page_size = 10
full_pages = 47 / 10 = 4 r7
```

The remainder tells you there are extra results that do not fill a whole page.
