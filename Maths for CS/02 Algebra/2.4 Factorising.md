## Introduction

**Factorising** is the reverse of expanding brackets. Instead of removing brackets by multiplying out, you **put brackets back in** by finding a shared structure. The expression means the same thing — just written in a factored form that is often shorter or more useful.

A **common factor** divides every term of an expression. A **factor pair** consists of two expressions that multiply to give a quadratic. **Quadratic factorisation** writes a quadratic as a product of two brackets. The **difference of squares** is a special pattern where a² - b² factorises as (a + b)(a - b). You can always verify your work by **checking by expansion** — multiply the brackets back out.

> [!example] Everyday idea
> "6 apples and 9 oranges" can be regrouped as "3 bags, each with 2 apples and 3 oranges." The total fruit is the same; you have just found a common grouping — a common factor of 3.

## New Words

- [[common factor]] — a number or expression that divides exactly into every term of an expression.
- [[factor pair]] — two expressions that multiply together to give a quadratic, such as (x + 2) and (x + 3).
- [[quadratic factorisation]] — writing a quadratic expression as the product of two bracketed linear factors.
- [[difference of squares]] — the pattern a² - b² that factorises to (a + b)(a - b).
- [[checking by expansion]] — verifying a factorisation by multiplying the brackets back out.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Common Factors

**Builds on:** [[1.10 Breadcrumb 2|Finding Factors]] (listing factors of a number), [[1.10 Breadcrumb 4|Highest Common Factor]] (largest shared factor)

A [[common factor]] divides into **every term** of an expression with no remainder.

```text
12 = 3 × 4
18 = 3 × 6
```

So 3 is a common factor of 12 and 18.

In algebra:

```text
6x + 9
```

Both terms are divisible by 3:

```text
6x = 3 × 2x
9  = 3 × 3
```

So 3 is a common factor.

> [!important] Look for the highest common factor
> Taking out the largest common factor gives the neatest answer. For `6x + 9`, the HCF is 3, not 1.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Find the common factors of the terms in `8x + 12`.

Step 1: List factors of 8: 1, 2, 4, 8.

Step 2: List factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.

Step 3: Find shared factors: 1, 2, 4.

Answer: The common factors are **1, 2, and 4**. The highest is **4**.

### Breadcrumb 2: Taking Out a Common Factor

**Builds on:** [[2.4 Breadcrumb 1|Common Factors]] (identifying what to take out), [[2.3 Breadcrumb 1|The Distributive Law]] (factorising reverses distribution)

To factorise by taking out a [[common factor]], rewrite each term as (factor) × (something), then put the factor outside brackets.

```text
6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3)
```

Check — this is the reverse of the [[distributive law]] from [[2.3 Expanding Brackets]]:

```text
3(2x + 3) = 6x + 9  ✓
```

> [!tip] Divide each term by the common factor
> `6x ÷ 3 = 2x` and `9 ÷ 3 = 3`. What remains inside the bracket is `(2x + 3)`.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Factorise `10x - 15`.

Step 1: Find the HCF of 10 and 15 — it is 5.

Step 2: Divide each term by 5.

```text
10x ÷ 5 = 2x
-15 ÷ 5 = -3
```

Step 3: Write the factor outside brackets.

Answer: **5(2x - 3)**.

### Breadcrumb 3: Factor Pairs for Quadratics

**Builds on:** [[2.4 Breadcrumb 2|Taking Out a Common Factor]] (same idea, applied backwards), [[1.10 Breadcrumb 2|Finding Factors]] (listing factor pairs of a number)

[[quadratic factorisation]] means writing a quadratic like `x² + 5x + 6` as a product of two brackets.

You need a [[factor pair]] of numbers that:

- **multiply** to give the constant term (6)
- **add** to give the coefficient of x (5)

```text
2 × 3 = 6   ✓
2 + 3 = 5   ✓
```

So:

```text
x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)
```

> [!warning] Sign matters
> For `x² - 5x + 6`, you need numbers that multiply to +6 and add to -5: **-2** and **-3**.

```text
x² - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3)
```

#### Chunked Example
Question: Factorise `x² + 7x + 12`.

Step 1: Find factor pairs of 12.

```text
1 × 12,  2 × 6,  3 × 4
```

Step 2: Find the pair that adds to 7.

```text
3 + 4 = 7  ✓
```

Step 3: Write the brackets.

Answer: **(x + 3)(x + 4)**.

### Breadcrumb 4: Difference of Squares

**Builds on:** [[2.4 Breadcrumb 3|Factor Pairs for Quadratics]] (special case where middle term is zero), [[2.3 Breadcrumb 5|Expanding Double Brackets]] (cross terms cancel)

The [[difference of squares]] is a special pattern:

```text
a² - b² = (a + b)(a - b)
```

Examples:

```text
x² - 9   = (x + 3)(x - 3)
x² - 1   = (x + 1)(x - 1)
4x² - 25 = (2x + 5)(2x - 5)
```

Both terms must be **perfect squares** and connected by **subtraction**.

> [!example] Why the pattern works
> Expand `(x + 3)(x - 3)`:

```text
x² - 3x + 3x - 9 = x² - 9
```

The middle terms cancel — that is why the pattern has no `x` term.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Factorise `x² - 16`.

Step 1: Recognise the pattern — two squares with subtraction.

```text
x² - 16 = x² - 4²
```

Step 2: Apply the difference of squares formula.

```text
a = x,  b = 4
```

Step 3: Write the factors.

Answer: **(x + 4)(x - 4)**.

### Breadcrumb 5: Checking by Expansion

**Builds on:** [[2.4 Breadcrumb 2|Taking Out a Common Factor]] (verify common factor work), [[2.4 Breadcrumb 3|Factor Pairs for Quadratics]] (verify quadratic work), [[2.3 Breadcrumb 5|Expanding Double Brackets]] (multiply back out)

Always use [[checking by expansion]] to verify your factorisation.

If you factorised `x² + 5x + 6` as `(x + 2)(x + 3)`, expand back:

```text
(x + 2)(x + 3)
= x² + 3x + 2x + 6
= x² + 5x + 6  ✓
```

> [!important] Expansion is the inverse of factorisation
> If expansion does not return the original expression, the factorisation is wrong. Go back and check your factor pair or common factor.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Check whether `(x - 1)(x + 6)` factorises `x² + 5x - 6` correctly.

Step 1: Expand the proposed factorisation.

```text
(x - 1)(x + 6) = x² + 6x - x - 6 = x² + 5x - 6
```

Step 2: Compare to the original.

```text
x² + 5x - 6 = x² + 5x - 6  ✓
```

Answer: **Yes**, the factorisation is correct.

### Breadcrumb 6: Extracting Common Structure in Code

**Builds on:** [[2.4 Breadcrumb 2|Taking Out a Common Factor]] (same operation in code), [[1.4 Breadcrumb 8|Nested Loops]] (repeated computation)

Factorising is a form of **simplification** — finding structure that makes an expression easier to work with.

In code, recognising common structure avoids repeated computation:

```python
# Before (expanded)
result = a*x + a*y + a*z

# After (factorised)
result = a * (x + y + z)
```

One multiplication instead of three — the same idea as taking out a common factor.

> [!example] CS connection
> Compiler optimisers detect common subexpressions and factor them out automatically, exactly as you factor an algebraic expression.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Rewrite `2x + 2y + 2z` in factorised form and explain the CS benefit.

Step 1: Identify the common factor — 2.

Step 2: Factorise.

```text
2(x + y + z)
```

Step 3: CS benefit — one multiplication by 2 instead of three separate doublings; clearer intent in code.

Answer: **2(x + y + z)** — fewer operations and clearer structure.

## Why This Works

Factorising works because multiplication and division are inverse operations, just as expanding and factorising are reverse processes.

Taking out a common factor reverses the distributive law:

```text
6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3)  ⇔  3(2x + 3) = 6x + 9
```

Quadratic factorisation works because if `(x + p)(x + q)` expands to `x² + (p+q)x + pq`, then finding `p` and `q` from the constant and the middle coefficient reconstructs the brackets.

The difference of squares works because the cross terms cancel on expansion — a structural zero that leaves only the square terms.

## Common Mistakes

- Taking out only part of a common factor: `6x + 9 = 3(6x + 9)` instead of **3(2x + 3)**.
- Wrong factor pair: for `x² + 5x + 6`, writing `(x + 1)(x + 6)` — check: `1 + 6 = 7`, not 5.
- Sign errors: for `x² - 5x + 6`, using `(x + 2)(x + 3)` instead of **(x - 2)(x - 3)**.
- Applying difference of squares to a sum: `x² + 9` does **not** factorise over the integers.
- Forgetting to check by expanding.
- Stopping before taking the HCF: `4x + 6 = 2(2x + 3)`, not `2(4x + 6)`.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern
> If expansion of your answer does not match the original, your factor pair or common factor is wrong. Always expand to verify.

## Where This Shows Up in CS

Factorising appears wherever structure is extracted for efficiency:

```python
from sympy import factor
factor(6*x + 9)          # 3*(2*x + 3)
factor(x**2 - 16)        # (x - 4)*(x + 4)
```

Uses include:

- compiler common subexpression elimination
- polynomial algorithms in graphics and signal processing
- RSA and prime factorisation in cryptography ([[10.6 Cryptography RSA and Diffie Hellman]])
- Boolean algebra simplification
- machine learning feature factorisation
