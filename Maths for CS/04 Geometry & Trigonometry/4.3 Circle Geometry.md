## Introduction

A **circle** is the set of all points at a fixed distance (the [[radius]]) from a central point. Circles have special names for every part: the diameter is twice the radius; a chord is a segment connecting any two points on the circumference; an arc is a curved portion of the circumference; a sector is a wedge cut from the centre. The constant [[π]] (pi ≈ 3.1416) relates every circle's circumference to its diameter.

> [!example] Everyday idea
> A clock face is a circle. The hands sweep through sectors. The rim is the circumference. A pizza slice is a sector. The crust is an arc.

## New Words

- [[circle]] — all points at a fixed distance (the [[radius]]) from a centre.
- [[radius]] — the distance from the centre to any point on the circle (plural: radii).
- [[diameter]] — a chord that passes through the centre; twice the radius.
- [[π]] — the constant pi, approximately 3.1416, the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter.
- [[circumference]] — the perimeter (distance around) a circle.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Radius and Diameter

**Builds on:** [[4.1 Breadcrumb 1|Lines Are Straight Paths]] (distance between two points), [[1.8 Breadcrumb 1|Decimals as Tenths and Hundredths]] (decimal multiplication)

A [[circle]]'s [[radius]] is the distance from its centre to any point on the edge. The [[diameter]] is twice the radius — the longest distance across the circle.

```text
d = 2r
r = d/2
```

> [!example] Radius example
> A clock has a 5 cm radius. The diameter is 10 cm — the distance from 12 o'clock straight through the centre to 6 o'clock.

#### Chunked Example
Question: A circle has a radius of 7 cm. What is its diameter?

d = 2 × 7 = 14.

Answer: **14 cm**.

---

### Breadcrumb 2: Pi (π) — The Circle Constant

**Builds on:** [[4.3 Breadcrumb 1|Radius and Diameter]] (measurement of circles), [[1.11 Breadcrumb 1|Rational Numbers as Fractions]] (π is irrational), [[2.10 Breadcrumb 1|Surds and Rationalising]] (irrational constants)

[[π]] is the ratio of a circle's [[circumference]] to its [[diameter]]. This number is the same for every circle, large or small.

```text
π = C/d ≈ 3.14159...
```

> [!important] π is irrational
> π can never be written as a terminating or repeating decimal. All calculations using π are approximations.

#### Chunked Example
Question: A circle has a diameter of 10 cm. Estimate its circumference using π ≈ 3.14.

C = π × d ≈ 3.14 × 10 = 31.4.

Answer: **31.4 cm**.

---

### Breadcrumb 3: Circumference of a Circle

**Builds on:** [[4.3 Breadcrumb 2|Pi (π) — The Circle Constant]] (C = πd), [[4.2 Breadcrumb 1|Perimeter Is the Sum of Edge Lengths]] (perimeter concept)

The [[circumference]] is the circle's perimeter — the distance once around the edge.

```text
C = πd   or   C = 2πr
```

> [!tip] Use whichever is convenient
> If you have the radius, use C = 2πr. If you have the diameter, use C = πd. Either is correct.

#### Chunked Example
Question: A circle has radius 4 cm. Find its circumference (use π = 3.14).

C = 2 × 3.14 × 4 = 25.12.

Answer: **25.12 cm**.

---

### Breadcrumb 4: Arcs and Sectors of a Circle

**Builds on:** [[4.3 Breadcrumb 3|Circumference of a Circle]] (partial circle), [[4.1 Breadcrumb 2|Angles Measure the Turn Between Two Lines]] (central angle)

An [[arc]] is a portion of the circumference. A [[sector]] is the wedge from the centre to two points on the circle.

```text
Arc length = (θ/360) × 2πr
Sector area = (θ/360) × πr²
```

where θ is the central angle in degrees.

> [!example] Pizza slice
> A 90° slice (quarter of a pizza) has arc length = (90/360) × 2πr = ¼ × 2πr = ½πr.

#### Chunked Example
Question: A circle of radius 6 cm has a 60° sector. Find arc length (π = 3.14).

Arc = (60/360) × 2 × 3.14 × 6 = (1/6) × 37.68 = 6.28.

Answer: **6.28 cm**.

---

### Breadcrumb 5: Area of a Circle

**Builds on:** [[4.3 Breadcrumb 1|Radius and Diameter]] (radius measure), [[4.2 Breadcrumb 2|Area of Rectangles and Triangles]] (area concept)

```text
A = πr²
```

> [!warning] Area of a circle vs circumference
> Circumference uses r (one power of radius). Area uses r² (two powers of radius). The units are different: cm vs cm².

#### Chunked Example
Question: Find the area of a circle with radius 5 cm (π = 3.14).

A = 3.14 × 25 = 78.5.

Answer: **78.5 cm²**.

---

### Breadcrumb 6: Circle Vocabulary — Chord, Tangent, Segment

**Builds on:** [[4.3 Breadcrumb 1|Radius and Diameter]] (radius as distance), [[4.1 Breadcrumb 3|Parallel and Perpendicular Lines]] (tangent ⊥ radius)

A [[chord]] is a line segment whose endpoints are on the circle (diameter is the longest chord). A [[tangent]] is a line that touches the circle at exactly one point — it is perpendicular to the radius at that point.

```text
Tangent theorem: tangent ⟂ radius at point of contact
```

The area between a chord and its arc is a [[segment]] — think of a chord slicing off a cap of the circle.

> [!tip] Tangent ⟂ radius
> The tangent is always at right angles to the radius where they meet. This property is used in collision detection.

#### Chunked Example
Question: A tangent touches a circle of radius 3 cm at point T. What is the angle between the tangent and the radius OT?

The tangent theorem states tangent ⟂ radius. The angle is 90°.

Answer: **90°**.

---

### Breadcrumb 7: Composite Shapes with Circles

**Builds on:** [[4.3 Breadcrumb 5|Area of a Circle]] (area of whole circle), [[4.3 Breadcrumb 4|Arcs and Sectors of a Circle]] (partial circle), [[4.2 Breadcrumb 6|Composite Shapes by Split and Add]] (add/subtract areas)

Many shapes combine circles with rectangles or triangles. Find each piece separately.

> [!example] Running track
> A running track is two semicircles connected by two straight sections. The area is one full circle plus two rectangles.

#### Chunked Example
Question: A 20 m × 8 m rectangle has a semicircle on each short end. Find the total area (π = 3.14).

Straight section: 20 × 8 = 160. Two semicircles = one full circle: radius 4 m, area = 3.14 × 16 = 50.24. Total = 160 + 50.24 = 210.24.

Answer: **210.24 m²**.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Circumference from Radius

Question: A circle has a radius of 9 cm. Find its circumference. Use π = 3.14.

Step 1: C = 2πr = 2 × 3.14 × 9.

Step 2: 2 × 9 = 18, 18 × 3.14 = 56.52.

Answer: The circumference is **56.52 cm**.

---

### Example 2 — Area from Diameter

Question: A circle has a diameter of 14 cm. Find its area. Use π = 3.14.

Step 1: Radius = diameter ÷ 2 = 14 ÷ 2 = 7 cm.

Step 2: A = πr² = 3.14 × 7² = 3.14 × 49 = 153.86.

Answer: The area is **153.86 cm²**.

---

### Example 3 — Arc Length and Sector Area

Question: A circle of radius 10 cm has a 72° sector. Find the arc length and the sector area. Use π = 3.14.

Step 1: Arc length = (θ/360) × 2πr = (72/360) × 2 × 3.14 × 10 = (1/5) × 62.8 = 12.56 cm.

Step 2: Sector area = (θ/360) × πr² = (72/360) × 3.14 × 100 = (1/5) × 314 = 62.8 cm².

Answer: Arc length = **12.56 cm**, sector area = **62.8 cm²**.

---

### Example 4 — Composite Shape Area

Question: A rectangle 12 m × 6 m has a semicircle on one of its 6 m sides. Find the total area. Use π = 3.14.

Step 1: Rectangle area = 12 × 6 = 72 m².

Step 2: Semicircle radius = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 m. Full circle area = 3.14 × 9 = 28.26 m². Semicircle = 28.26 ÷ 2 = 14.13 m².

Step 3: Total = 72 + 14.13 = 86.13 m².

Answer: Total area = **86.13 m²**.

## Why This Works

Every circle's circumference and diameter have the same ratio — that [[π]] is universal. The tangent-radius right angle comes from the definition of the circle: the radius length changes fastest when you move perpendicular to it. The area formula arises from slicing a circle into thin triangular sectors and reassembling them into a near-rectangle.

## Common Mistakes

1. Confusing circumference (2πr, length units) with area (πr², squared units).
2. Using diameter instead of radius in area (you need r², not d²).
3. Forgetting to convert angle fraction for arcs and sectors (θ/360).
4. Assuming chord is always diameter.
5. Treating π as exactly 3.14 (it is an approximation; leave π symbol for exact answers).

> [!failure] Mistake pattern
> "I used radius 10 when the diameter was 10" — the most common circle error. Always double-check radius vs diameter.

---

## Where This Shows Up in CS

- **Collision detection**: Circle-vs-circle collision checking compares `distance(centre1, centre2) ≤ r₁ + r₂`.
- **Sprites with circular hitboxes**: Many games use a "bounding circle" for fast collision culling.
- **Image rotation**: Pixel coordinates traced in an arc use sine and cosine (coming in [[4.5 Trig Ratios SOHCAHTOA]]).
- **Field of view cone**: A FOV cone is a sector with its tip at the camera.
