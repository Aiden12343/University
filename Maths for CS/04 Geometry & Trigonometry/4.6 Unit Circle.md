## Introduction

The **unit circle** is a circle of radius 1 centred at (0, 0). For any angle θ measured from the positive x-axis, the coordinates of the point on the circle are (cos θ, sin θ). This connects trig ratios to coordinates, letting sine and cosine be defined for **any** angle — not just those inside a right triangle. Instead of asking "what is the opposite over hypotenuse?", we ask "what are the x and y coordinates of a point after rotating by θ?".

> [!example] Everyday idea
> A Ferris wheel is a unit circle. Your height above the centre is sin θ — it goes up and down as you rotate. Your horizontal offset from the centre is cos θ.

## New Words

- [[unit circle]] — a circle of radius 1 centred at the origin, used to define trig functions for all angles.
- [[cosine coordinate]] — the x-coordinate of a point on the unit circle at angle θ; equal to cos θ.
- [[sine coordinate]] — the y-coordinate of a point on the unit circle at angle θ; equal to sin θ.
- [[period]] — the interval after which a function repeats; sine and cosine repeat every 360°.
- [[quadrant signs]] — which trig functions are positive in each of the four quadrants.
- [[reference angle]] — the acute angle between the terminal side and the x-axis, used to evaluate trig functions in any quadrant.

> [!important] You pass this topic only when you can define all six terms without looking.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: The Unit Circle Definition

**Builds on:** [[4.4 Breadcrumb 4|The Distance Formula (2D)]] (x² + y² = 1 as distance), [[3.1 Breadcrumb 1|Axes and Coordinates]] (origin and axes)

The unit circle is every point (x, y) satisfying x² + y² = 1 — all points exactly 1 unit from the origin.

```text
                  y
                  ↑
        II        |        I
     (-,+)        |     (+,+)
                  |
    ——————————————+——————————————→ x
                  |
        III       |        IV
     (-,-)        |     (+,-)
                  |
```

The circle is divided into four **quadrants** by the axes. Any point on the circle can be reached by rotating from the positive x-axis by some angle θ.

> [!info] Why radius 1?
> Radius 1 makes the algebra clean. From Pythagoras: for a right triangle with hypotenuse = 1, the legs are exactly sin θ and cos θ. No scaling factor.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Verify that (3/5, 4/5) lies on the unit circle.

(3/5)² + (4/5)² = 9/25 + 16/25 = 25/25 = 1. Since x² + y² = 1, the point is on the circle.

Answer: Yes, (3/5, 4/5) is on the unit circle. This means there exists θ where cos θ = 3/5 and sin θ = 4/5.

### Breadcrumb 2: Coordinates Are Cosine and Sine

**Builds on:** [[4.5 Breadcrumb 2|Sine (SOH)]] and [[4.5 Breadcrumb 3|Cosine (CAH)]] (trig ratios), [[4.5 Breadcrumb 1|Labelling a Right Triangle for Trig]] (adjacent = x, opposite = y)

For an angle θ measured counter-clockwise from the positive x-axis, the point P on the unit circle has coordinates:

$$P = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$

This is the **central insight** of the unit circle. In a right triangle with hypotenuse H: sin θ = O/H, cos θ = A/H. In the unit circle, H = 1, so:

- Adjacent = x = cos θ × 1 = cos θ
- Opposite = y = sin θ × 1 = sin θ

When H = 1, the trig ratios are **exactly** the coordinates.

> [!tip] Mnemonic
> **C**osine is the **x**-coordinate (both start with /k/). **S**ine is the **y**-coordinate (sine goes up).

#### Chunked Example
Question: Find the coordinates at θ = 45°.

cos 45° = √2/2, sin 45° = √2/2. P = (√2/2, √2/2).

Check: (√2/2)² + (√2/2)² = 2/4 + 2/4 = 1. ✓

#### Chunked Example
Question: Given P = (−0.5, √3/2), find θ.

cos θ = −0.5, sin θ = √3/2. Reference = 60° (cos 60° = 0.5). cos negative, sin positive → QII. θ = 180° − 60° = 120°.

Answer: θ = **120°**.

### Breadcrumb 3: Sine and Cosine for Angles Beyond 90°

**Builds on:** [[4.6 Breadcrumb 2|Coordinates Are Cosine and Sine]] (P = (cos θ, sin θ)), [[4.6 Breadcrumb 1|The Unit Circle Definition]] (quadrant signs)

Right triangles only work for 0°–90°. The unit circle removes this restriction. Any angle — 120°, 270°, 450°, even negative — corresponds to a point on the circle.

For θ = 120°:
1. The **reference angle** is 180° − 120° = 60° (acute angle to x-axis).
2. Magnitudes match 60°: cos 60° = 1/2, sin 60° = √3/2.
3. Signs from quadrant II: x negative, y positive.
4. cos 120° = −1/2, sin 120° = √3/2.

```text
                  y
                  ↑
                  |   P = (−½, √3/2)
                  |    \
                  |     \  120°
                  |      \
                  |60°_____\
    ——————————————+——————————————→ x
```

> [!warning] Common trap
> Use the reference angle for magnitude, then apply the quadrant sign. Do not memorise signs alone — reason from whether x, y are positive in that quadrant.

**Negative angles** go clockwise: −90° = 270° → (0, −1).
**Angles > 360°** wrap around: 390° = 360° + 30°, same point as 30°.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Evaluate cos 150° and sin 150°.

Reference = 180° − 150° = 30°. cos 30° = √3/2, sin 30° = 1/2. QII: x−, y+. cos 150° = −√3/2, sin 150° = 1/2.

Check: 3/4 + 1/4 = 1. ✓

Answer: cos 150° = **−√3/2**, sin 150° = **1/2**.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Evaluate cos 240° and sin 240°.

Reference = 240° − 180° = 60°. QIII: both negative. cos 240° = −1/2, sin 240° = −√3/2.

Answer: cos 240° = **−1/2**, sin 240° = **−√3/2**.

### Breadcrumb 4: Quadrant Signs (CAST)

**Builds on:** [[4.6 Breadcrumb 1|The Unit Circle Definition]] (four quadrants with sign pairs), [[4.6 Breadcrumb 3|Sine and Cosine for Angles Beyond 90°]] (sign changes by quadrant)

The **CAST** mnemonic (reading counter-clockwise from QIV):

```text
                  y
                  ↑
        S   |    A
      sin   |   all
       +    |    +
            |
     QII     |      QI
    —————————+————————→ x
            |
    QIII     |     QIV
       +    |    +
      tan   |   cos
        T   |    C
```

- **C** — Cosine positive in QIV
- **A** — All positive in QI
- **S** — Sine positive in QII
- **T** — Tangent positive in QIII

| Quadrant | Range | cos θ | sin θ | tan θ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| QI | 0°–90° | + | + | + |
| QII | 90°–180° | − | + | − |
| QIII | 180°–270° | − | − | + |
| QIV | 270°–360° | + | − | − |

> [!info] Tangent hint
> tan θ = sin θ / cos θ, so tan is positive when sin and cos have the **same** sign (QI, QIII) and negative when opposite (QII, QIV).

#### Chunked Example
Question: Determine the sign of cos 200°.

200° → QIII → x negative → cos 200° < 0.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Given cos θ > 0 and sin θ < 0, find the quadrant.

cos > 0 → QI or QIV. sin < 0 → QIII or QIV. Intersection → QIV.

Answer: **QIV**.

### Breadcrumb 5: Special Angles

**Builds on:** [[4.6 Breadcrumb 2|Coordinates Are Cosine and Sine]] (coordinates from cos/sin), [[4.5 Breadcrumb 2|Sine (SOH)]] (exact value patterns)

Five key angles: **0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90°**. Their sin/cos values are exact.

| θ | sin θ | cos θ | tan θ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| 0° | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 30° | 1/2 | √3/2 | 1/√3 |
| 45° | √2/2 | √2/2 | 1 |
| 60° | √3/2 | 1/2 | √3 |
| 90° | 1 | 0 | undefined |

**The √/2 pattern** (sin θ from 0° to 90°):
√0/2 = 0 → 0°, √1/2 = 1/2 → 30°, √2/2 = √2/2 → 45°, √3/2 = √3/2 → 60°, √4/2 = 1 → 90°.

For cos θ, reverse the sequence.

```text
                  y
                  ↑
          90°     |     60°
      (0,1)       |   (½, √3/2)
                  |
          120°    |    45°
       (−½, √3/2) |   (√2/2, √2/2)
                  |
          135°    |    30°
       (−√2/2, √2/2) | (√3/2, ½)
                  |
      150°        |    0°
    (−√3/2, ½)    |   (1, 0)
    ——————————————+——————————————→ x
                  |
      180°        |    330°
      (−1,0)      |   (√3/2, −½)
                  |
      210°        |    315°
    (−√3/2, −½)   |   (√2/2, −√2/2)
                  |
      225°        |    300°
    (−√2/2, −√2/2)|   (½, −√3/2)
                  |
      240°        |    270°
      (−½, −√3/2) |   (0, −1)
```

#### Chunked Example
Question: Find sin 60° and cos 60° using the √/2 pattern.

sin 60° = √3/2, cos 60° = √1/2 = 1/2.

Check: (√3/2)² + (1/2)² = 3/4 + 1/4 = 1. ✓

#### Chunked Example
Question: Find sin 120° using special angles.

Reference = 180° − 120° = 60°. sin 60° = √3/2. QII: sin positive. sin 120° = √3/2.

Answer: sin 120° = **√3/2**.

### Breadcrumb 6: Periodicity

**Builds on:** [[4.6 Breadcrumb 1|The Unit Circle Definition]] (full rotation = 360°), [[2.11 Breadcrumb 2|Modulo and Remainder]] (modular arithmetic)

Sine and cosine are **periodic** with period 360°:

$$\sin(\theta + 360^\circ) = \sin\theta \quad \cos(\theta + 360^\circ) = \cos\theta$$

A full rotation returns to the same point. Angles are taken modulo 360°: 390° ≡ 30° (mod 360°).

**Tangent period:** tan(θ + 180°) = tan θ — tan repeats twice as fast because sin and cos both flip sign every 180°, making the quotient unchanged.

> [!warning] tan has period 180°, not 360°. Forgetting this loses solutions when solving equations.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Simplify sin 405°.

405° − 360° = 45°. sin 405° = sin 45° = √2/2.

Answer: sin 405° = **√2/2**.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Simplify cos 510°.

510° − 360° = 150°. cos 150° = −√3/2. cos 510° = −√3/2.

Answer: cos 510° = **−√3/2**.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Verifying a Point on the Unit Circle

Question: Determine whether P = (−√3/2, 1/2) lies on the unit circle. If it does, find the angle θ (0° ≤ θ < 360°) that corresponds to this point.

Step 1: Check x² + y² = 1. x² = (−√3/2)² = 3/4, y² = (1/2)² = 1/4. Sum = 3/4 + 1/4 = 1. The point lies on the unit circle.

Step 2: cos θ = −√3/2, sin θ = 1/2. Cosine negative, sine positive → Quadrant II.

Step 3: Reference angle: cos(30°) = √3/2, sin(30°) = 1/2 → reference = 30°. QII angle = 180° − 30° = 150°.

Answer: Yes, P is on the unit circle at θ = **150°**.

---

### Example 2 — Evaluating Trig Functions Using the Reference Angle

Question: Evaluate cos 225° and sin 225° using exact values.

Step 1: 225° is in Quadrant III (180° to 270°). Reference angle = 225° − 180° = 45°.

Step 2: cos 45° = √2/2, sin 45° = √2/2. In QIII both x and y are negative.

Step 3: cos 225° = −√2/2, sin 225° = −√2/2.

Answer: cos 225° = **−√2/2**, sin 225° = **−√2/2**.

---

### Example 3 — Evaluating Sine in Quadrant IV

Question: Evaluate sin 330° using the reference angle and quadrant signs.

Step 1: 330° is in Quadrant IV (270° to 360°). Reference angle = 360° − 330° = 30°.

Step 2: sin 30° = 1/2. In QIV sine (y-coordinate) is negative.

Step 3: sin 330° = −1/2.

Answer: sin 330° = **−1/2**.

---

### Example 4 — Using Periodicity

Question: Simplify sin 690° and cos 690°.

Step 1: Subtract multiples of 360°: 690° − 360° = 330°.

Step 2: sin 690° = sin 330°. Reference = 360° − 330° = 30°. QIV → sin negative. sin 330° = −1/2.

Step 3: cos 690° = cos 330°. QIV → cos positive. cos 330° = √3/2.

Answer: sin 690° = **−1/2**, cos 690° = **√3/2**.

## Why This Works

The unit circle merges three earlier ideas:

1. **Pythagoras' theorem** — x² + y² = 1 is Pythagoras applied to radius 1.
2. **SOHCAHTOA** — When H = 1, adjacent = cos and opposite = sin directly.
3. **Coordinates** — The x‑y plane gives sign conventions via quadrants.

The generalisation: triangles only work for 0°–90°. Rotation works for any angle — past 360°, backwards, negative. The unit circle is the bridge from triangle geometry to the continuous-wave world of signals, graphics, and physics.

---

## Common Mistakes

1. **Mixing cos and y.** Cosine is the x-coordinate. Mnemonic: alphabetical — x before y, cos before sin.
2. **Forgetting quadrant signs.** cos 120° = −1/2, not 1/2. Apply the quadrant sign.
3. **Wrong reference angle.** For 150°, reference = 180° − 150° = 30°, NOT 90° − 150°. Always measure to the x-axis.
4. **Confusing periods.** sin/cos period = 360°. tan period = 180°.
5. **Stopping at one solution.** cos θ = 0.5 has TWO solutions in 0°–360°: 60° AND 300°.
6. **Rounding special values.** Keep √3/2, not 0.866. Exact forms prevent cascading errors.
7. **Treating sin and cos as "only for triangles".** The unit circle defines them for ALL angles — rotation, not just triangles.

## Where This Shows Up in CS

- **Graphics:** Rotation matrices, direction vectors, lighting dot product.
- **Audio:** Sine wave oscillators sin(2πft), Fourier Transform (JPEG, MP3, WiFi).
- **Machine learning:** Transformer positional encoding (GPT, BERT) encodes token positions as sin/cos of different frequencies; cyclic features (hour-of-day as (cos θ, sin θ)).
- **Robotics:** Circular motion (R cos ωt, R sin ωt), inverse kinematics, IMU sensor fusion.
- **CSS/Web:** `transform: rotate()`, radial layouts, D3.js pie charts.
