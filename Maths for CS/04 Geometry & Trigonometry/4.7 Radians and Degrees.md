## Introduction

Degrees and radians are two different units for measuring the same thing — angles. Degrees are the everyday unit: a full circle is 360°, a right angle is 90°. Radians are the natural mathematical unit: a full circle is 2π radians, a right angle is π/2 radians. Radians make calculus and physics formulas simpler, and most programming languages expect angles in radians.

> [!example] Everyday idea
> If you cut a pizza into 360 slices, each slice's tip angle is 1°. If you cut it so the crust length equals the radius, that angle is 1 radian ≈ 57.3°. About 6.28 radians (2π) fit in a full pizza.

## New Words

- [[degree]] — a unit of angle where a full circle equals 360°; one degree is 1/360 of a full turn.
- [[radian]] — a unit of angle where a full circle equals 2π radians; one radian is the angle where arc length equals radius.
- [[arc length]] — the distance along the circumference of a circle; in radians, arc length = r × θ.
- [[full turn]] — a complete rotation of 360° or 2π radians.
- [[conversion]] — changing between degrees and radians using the factor π rad = 180°.

> [!important] You pass this topic only when you can define all five terms without looking.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Degrees — The Familiar Unit

**Builds on:** [[4.1 Breadcrumb 2|Angles Measure the Turn Between Two Lines]] (angle in degrees), [[4.1 Breadcrumb 3|Parallel and Perpendicular Lines]] (90° right angle)

Degrees divide a full rotation into 360 equal parts. The number 360 was chosen for its many factors (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, ...), making fractions of a turn easy to express.

```text
Full turn = 360°
Half turn = 180°
Right angle = 90°
```

> [!example] Clock degrees
> A clock's minute hand moves 360° in one hour. In 15 minutes it moves 90°. The hour hand moves 360° in 12 hours, or 30° per hour.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Through how many degrees does a clock's hour hand move in 4 hours?

Step 1: The hour hand moves 360° in 12 hours → 30° per hour.
Step 2: In 4 hours: 4 × 30° = 120°.

Answer: **120°**.

---

### Breadcrumb 2: Radians — The Natural Unit

**Builds on:** [[4.3 Breadcrumb 1|Radius and Diameter]] (radius measure), [[4.3 Breadcrumb 4|Arcs and Sectors of a Circle]] (arc length concept)

One [[radian]] is the angle created when the arc length along a circle equals the radius. Since the full circumference is 2πr, a full circle has 2π radians.

```text
Full circle: C = 2πr, so angle = C/r = 2π radians
Half circle: π radians
Right angle: π/2 radians
```

> [!important] Why π appears in radians
> Circumference = 2πr. If you measure angle by "how many radii fit along the circumference," a full circle fits 2π radii = 2π radians.

#### Chunked Example
Question: A circle has radius 3 cm. What angle (in radians) corresponds to an arc length of 6 cm?

Step 1: Arc length s = r × θ (θ in radians).
Step 2: Rearrange: θ = s ÷ r = 6 ÷ 3 = 2.

Answer: **2 radians**.

Check: 2 rad ≈ 2 × 57.3° ≈ 114.6° — a little more than a right angle (90°). ✓

### Breadcrumb 3: Converting Between Degrees and Radians

**Builds on:** [[4.7 Breadcrumb 1|Degrees — The Familiar Unit]] (degree measure), [[4.7 Breadcrumb 2|Radians — The Natural Unit]] (radian measure), [[1.6 Fractions and Mixed Numbers]] (multiplying by a fraction equal to 1)

The key relationship:

$$ \pi \text{ radians} = 180^\circ $$

From this:

$$ \text{radians} = \text{degrees} \times \frac{\pi}{180} \qquad \text{degrees} = \text{radians} \times \frac{180}{\pi} $$

> [!tip] Quick method
> To convert degrees to radians: multiply by π/180. To convert radians to degrees: multiply by 180/π.

#### Chunked Example
Question A: Convert 60° to radians.

60 × π/180 = 60π/180 = π/3.

Answer: 60° = **π/3 radians**.

Question B: Convert 3π/4 radians to degrees.

(3π/4) × (180/π) = (3/4) × 180 = 540/4 = 135.

Answer: 3π/4 rad = **135°**.

### Breadcrumb 4: Common Angles Table

**Builds on:** [[4.7 Breadcrumb 3|Converting Between Degrees and Radians]] (using conversion), [[4.6 Breadcrumb 5|Special Angles]] (exact values at these angles)

| Degrees | Radians | Why |
|---------|---------|-----|
| 0° | 0 | No turn |
| 30° | π/6 | 1/12 of full turn |
| 45° | π/4 | 1/8 of full turn |
| 60° | π/3 | 1/6 of full turn |
| 90° | π/2 | 1/4 of full turn |
| 180° | π | 1/2 of full turn |
| 270° | 3π/2 | 3/4 of full turn |
| 360° | 2π | Full turn |

> [!tip] Pattern recognition
> 30° = π/6, 45° = π/4, 60° = π/3. The denominator tells you the fraction of the semicircle (π).

#### Chunked Example
Question: What is 150° in radians?

150° = 180° − 30° = π − π/6 = 6π/6 − π/6 = 5π/6.

Answer: 150° = **5π/6 radians**.

### Breadcrumb 5: Arc Length and Sector Area in Radians

**Builds on:** [[4.7 Breadcrumb 2|Radians — The Natural Unit]] (definition θ = s/r), [[4.3 Breadcrumb 4|Arcs and Sectors of a Circle]] (degree-based formulas)

In radians, formulas become simpler:

$$ \text{Arc length: } s = r\theta $$

$$ \text{Sector area: } A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta $$

No fractions of 360. The "2π/360" is already baked into the definition of the radian.

> [!example] Why simpler
> Degree version: s = (θ/360) × 2πr. Radian version: s = rθ. The simplification shows radians are natural.

#### Chunked Example
Question: A sector has radius 8 cm and angle 2 radians. Find arc length and area.

s = rθ = 8 × 2 = 16 cm. A = ½ r²θ = ½ × 64 × 2 = 64 cm².

Answer: Arc length = **16 cm**, sector area = **64 cm²**.

Check: Full circle area = π × 64 ≈ 201 cm². Sector fraction = 2/(2π) = 1/π ≈ 0.318. 0.318 × 201 ≈ 64 cm². ✓

### Breadcrumb 6: Why Code Uses Radians

**Builds on:** [[4.7 Breadcrumb 3|Converting Between Degrees and Radians]] (conversion factor π/180), [[2.9 Breadcrumb 1|Repeated Multiplication as a Power]] (understanding derivative notation conceptually)

Every standard math library expects radians:

```python
import math
math.sin(30)       # Wrong! Expects radians, not degrees
math.sin(math.pi/6)  # Correct: sin(30°) = 0.5
```

If you pass degrees to `math.sin` in Python, `Math.sin` in Java, or `sin` in C++, you get wrong results because these functions are defined using calculus, and the derivative rules only work in radians:

$$ \frac{d}{dx} \sin x = \cos x \quad \text{(only when x is in radians)} $$

> [!warning] Degrees in code = bugs
> Forgetting to convert degrees to radians is one of the most common bugs in game development and graphics programming.

#### Chunked Example
Question: In a Unity game, you want to rotate a character by 45° per second. The rotation function expects radians. What value do you pass?

45 × π/180 = π/4 ≈ 0.7854.

Answer: Pass **π/4** (≈ 0.7854 radians).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Converting Degrees to Radians

Question: Convert 150° to radians. Leave your answer in terms of π.

Step 1: Multiply by π/180: 150 × π/180 = 150π/180.

Step 2: Simplify: divide numerator and denominator by 30 → 5π/6.

Answer: 150° = **5π/6 radians**.

---

### Example 2 — Converting Radians to Degrees

Question: Convert 7π/4 radians to degrees.

Step 1: Multiply by 180/π: (7π/4) × (180/π) = 7 × 180 / 4.

Step 2: 7 × 180 = 1260. 1260 ÷ 4 = 315.

Answer: 7π/4 radians = **315°**.

---

### Example 3 — Arc Length in Radians

Question: A circle has radius 12 cm and a central angle of 2.5 radians. Find the arc length.

Step 1: Arc length formula in radians: s = rθ.

Step 2: s = 12 × 2.5 = 30.

Answer: The arc length is **30 cm**.

---

### Example 4 — Sector Area in Radians

Question: A sector has radius 6 m and an angle of π/3 radians. Find the sector area in terms of π and as a decimal (π ≈ 3.14).

Step 1: Sector area formula in radians: A = ½ r²θ.

Step 2: A = ½ × 6² × (π/3) = ½ × 36 × π/3 = 18 × π/3 = 6π m².

Step 3: Using π ≈ 3.14: A ≈ 6 × 3.14 = 18.84 m².

Answer: Sector area = **6π m²** (≈ **18.84 m²**).

## Why This Works

Radians connect angle measure to the geometry of circles. A degree is an arbitrary division (360 is convenient but has no mathematical reason). A radian is natural: it says "the angle whose arc is exactly one radius long."

Because of this natural definition, formulas simplify. In calculus, derivatives of trig functions only work in radians. In code, all standard math libraries use radians.

This connects back to [[1.1 Counting and Number Sense]] — radians count how many radius-lengths fit along the arc.

## Common Mistakes

1. Forgetting to convert when calling a math function — passing degrees to a radian function.
2. Using the wrong conversion direction (multiplying by 180/π instead of π/180).
3. Thinking π rad = 360° (it is 180°; 2π rad = 360°).
4. Not simplifying the fraction (leaving 240π/180 instead of 4π/3).
5. Confusing arc length (s = rθ) with chord length (different formula).
6. Assuming "degrees mode" on a calculator is the default (always check).

> [!failure] Mistake pattern
> "I converted 90° to radians by doing 90 × 180/π = 16200/π ≈ 5156°" — wrong direction! Use × π/180 for degrees → radians. 90 × π/180 = π/2.

## Where This Shows Up in CS

- **Math libraries**: `math.sin()`, `Math.sin()`, `numpy.sin()`, `std::sin()` — all expect radians.
- **Unity / Unreal**: Unity's `Mathf.Rad2Deg` and `Mathf.Deg2Rad` exist because conversion is so common.
- **Computer graphics**: rotation matrices, camera FOV, lighting angles.
- **Game physics**: angular velocity (rad/s), torque calculations.
- **Signal processing**: frequency in radians per second (ω = 2πf).
- **WebGL / OpenGL**: `glRotatef()` uses degrees, but shader math uses radians.

Example — converting in Python:
```python
import math
def move_character(speed_deg_per_sec, delta_time):
    angle_rad = speed_deg_per_sec * math.pi / 180
    dx = math.cos(angle_rad) * speed * delta_time
    dy = math.sin(angle_rad) * speed * delta_time
    return dx, dy
```
