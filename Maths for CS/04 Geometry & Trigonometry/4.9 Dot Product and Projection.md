## Introduction

The **dot product** is a way to multiply two vectors to get a single number (a scalar). It measures how much one vector extends in the direction of another. If two vectors point the same way, their dot product is large and positive. If they are at right angles, it is zero. If they point opposite ways, it is negative.

**Projection** answers: "How long is the shadow of one vector when light shines perpendicular onto the other vector?"

> [!example] Everyday idea
> If you pull a box with a rope at an angle, only the part of your force that points forward actually moves the box. The dot product tells you how much of the force is "effective" in the direction of motion.

## New Words

- [[dot product]] — a scalar operation on two vectors: a·b = a_x b_x + a_y b_y = |a||b|cos θ.
- [[projection]] — the component of one vector along the direction of another: proj_a(b) = (a·b / |a|²) a.
- [[scalar]] — a single number (not a vector); the result of a dot product.
- [[orthogonal]] — two vectors are orthogonal if their dot product is zero; they are at right angles.
- [[normal vector]] — a vector perpendicular to a surface, used in graphics for lighting.

> [!important] You pass this topic only when you can define all five terms without looking.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: The Dot Product — Algebraic Definition

**Builds on:** [[4.8 Breadcrumb 1|What Is a Vector?]] (vector components), [[1.4 Breadcrumb 2|Multiplication as Repeated Addition]] (multiply then add)

For a = (a_x, a_y) and b = (b_x, b_y):

$$ \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = a_x b_x + a_y b_y $$

Multiply matching components, then add. The result is a scalar (a single number).

> [!important] Dot product is commutative
> a·b = b·a. Order does not matter.

#### Chunked Example
Question: a = (3, 4), b = (2, 5). Find a·b.

3×2 = 6. 4×5 = 20. 6 + 20 = 26.

Answer: **a·b = 26**.

---

### Breadcrumb 2: The Dot Product — Geometric Definition

**Builds on:** [[4.5 Breadcrumb 3|Cosine (CAH)]] (cos θ), [[4.8 Breadcrumb 2|Magnitude (Length) of a Vector]] (|a|, |b|)

The geometric definition relates dot product to the angle between vectors:

$$ \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = |\mathbf{a}| |\mathbf{b}| \cos \theta $$

where θ is the angle between the vectors' directions.

- θ = 0° (same direction): cos 0 = 1 → a·b = |a||b|
- θ = 90° (perpendicular): cos 90 = 0 → a·b = 0
- θ = 180° (opposite): cos 180 = −1 → a·b = −|a||b|

#### Chunked Example
Question: |a| = 5, |b| = 3, angle = 60°. Find a·b.

a·b = 5 × 3 × cos 60° = 5 × 3 × 0.5 = 7.5.

Answer: **a·b = 7.5**.

---

### Breadcrumb 3: Finding the Angle Between Vectors

**Builds on:** [[4.9 Breadcrumb 1|The Dot Product — Algebraic Definition]] (computing a·b), [[4.9 Breadcrumb 2|The Dot Product — Geometric Definition]] (rearranging), [[4.5 Breadcrumb 6|Finding an Angle Using Inverse Trig]] (cos⁻¹)

Rearrange the geometric definition:

$$ \cos \theta = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}| |\mathbf{b}|} \qquad \theta = \cos^{-1} \left( \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}| |\mathbf{b}|} \right) $$

This is used constantly in graphics (lighting, back-face culling) and game AI (FOV checks).

#### Chunked Example
Question: a = (3, 4), b = (5, 0). Find the angle between them.

a·b = 3×5 + 4×0 = 15. |a| = 5, |b| = 5. cos θ = 15/25 = 0.6. θ = cos⁻¹(0.6) ≈ 53.1°.

Answer: The angle is approximately **53.1°**.

---

### Breadcrumb 4: Projection of One Vector Onto Another

**Builds on:** [[4.9 Breadcrumb 1|The Dot Product — Algebraic Definition]] (a·b), [[4.8 Breadcrumb 4|Scalar Multiplication]] (multiplying vector by scalar)

The **projection** of b onto a tells you "how much of b points in the direction of a."

$$ \text{proj}_\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{b}) = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|^2} \; \mathbf{a} $$

The result is a **vector**. The scalar part (a·b)/(|a|²) is the **scalar projection** — the length of the shadow.

```text
     b
    /|
   / |
  /  |  (perpendicular component)
 /   |
a----+---->
 proj_a(b)
```

> [!tip] Perpendicular component
> The component of b perpendicular to a is: b − proj_a(b). Together they decompose b into parallel and perpendicular parts.

#### Chunked Example
Question: a = (3, 0), b = (4, 3). Find the projection of b onto a.

a·b = 3×4 + 0×3 = 12. |a|² = 9. scalar = 12/9 = 4/3. proj = (4/3) × (3, 0) = (4, 0).

Answer: The projection is **(4, 0)**. Perpendicular component: (4, 3) − (4, 0) = (0, 3).

Check: (4, 0) is parallel to a. (0, 3) is perpendicular to a. ✓

---

### Breadcrumb 5: Orthogonal (Perpendicular) Vectors

**Builds on:** [[4.9 Breadcrumb 1|The Dot Product — Algebraic Definition]] (a·b = 0), [[4.9 Breadcrumb 2|The Dot Product — Geometric Definition]] (cos 90° = 0)

Vectors are **orthogonal** (perpendicular) iff their dot product is zero:

$$ \mathbf{a} \perp \mathbf{b} \iff \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0 $$

> [!important] Zero dot product = perpendicular
> This works in 2D, 3D, and higher dimensions — a simple, fast check.

#### Chunked Example
Question: Are a = (2, −3) and b = (6, 4) orthogonal?

a·b = 2×6 + (−3)×4 = 12 − 12 = 0. Yes, they are orthogonal.

Answer: **Yes, a and b are orthogonal**.

---

### Breadcrumb 6: Normal Vectors in 3D

**Builds on:** [[4.9 Breadcrumb 5|Orthogonal (Perpendicular) Vectors]] (perpendicular direction), [[4.8 Breadcrumb 6|Vectors in 3D]] (3D vector notation)

A **normal vector** is a vector perpendicular to a surface. In 3D graphics, every triangle has a normal vector pointing outward. The normal is used for lighting: if the light direction aligns with the normal, the surface is brightly lit.

> [!example] Phong lighting
> Diffuse brightness = max(L·N, 0). If L·N is near 1, light hits straight-on (bright). If L·N = 0, light grazes the surface (dark).

#### Chunked Example
Question: Surface normal n = (0, 0, 1). Light direction L = (1, 1, 1). Normalise L and compute diffuse brightness.

|L| = √(1+1+1) = √3 ≈ 1.732. L̂ = (0.577, 0.577, 0.577). n·L̂ = 0.577. brightness = max(0.577, 0) = 0.577.

Answer: The diffuse brightness is approximately **0.577** (≈ 58% of maximum).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Algebraic Dot Product

Question: a = (2, −5) and b = (3, 4). Compute a·b.

Step 1: a·b = a_x × b_x + a_y × b_y.

Step 2: a·b = 2 × 3 + (−5) × 4 = 6 − 20 = −14.

Answer: **a·b = −14**.

---

### Example 2 — Finding the Angle Between Two Vectors

Question: a = (4, 0) and b = (3, 3). Find the angle θ between them (to 1 decimal place).

Step 1: a·b = 4×3 + 0×3 = 12.

Step 2: |a| = 4, |b| = √(3² + 3²) = √18 ≈ 4.243.

Step 3: cos θ = (a·b) / (|a||b|) = 12 / (4 × 4.243) = 12 / 16.971 ≈ 0.7071.

Step 4: θ = cos⁻¹(0.7071) ≈ 45.0°.

Answer: The angle is approximately **45.0°**.

---

### Example 3 — Checking Orthogonality

Question: Determine whether a = (3, −2, 1) and b = (2, 3, 0) are orthogonal.

Step 1: Compute a·b = 3×2 + (−2)×3 + 1×0 = 6 − 6 + 0 = 0.

Step 2: If a·b = 0, the vectors are orthogonal.

Answer: Yes, **a and b are orthogonal**.

---

### Example 4 — Projection of One Vector Onto Another

Question: a = (6, 0) and b = (2, 5). Find the projection of b onto a.

Step 1: a·b = 6×2 + 0×5 = 12. |a|² = 6² + 0² = 36.

Step 2: Scalar projection = (a·b) / |a|² = 12 / 36 = 1/3.

Step 3: Vector projection = (1/3) × (6, 0) = (2, 0).

Answer: proj_a(b) = **(2, 0)**.

## Why This Works

The dot product combines algebra (component multiplication) with geometry (cos θ). It works because vectors in Euclidean space obey the law of cosines — the same principle that gives us Pythagoras.

When we multiply components and add, we compute how much a and b "agree" along each axis, summed across all axes.

This connects to [[1.4 Multiplication and Division]] (multiply then add) and [[4.4 Pythagoras Theorem]] (|a|² = a·a).

## Common Mistakes

1. Computing dot product as a vector (it is a scalar — a single number).
2. Forgetting that a·b = 0 means orthogonal, not "no result."
3. Finding the angle using the wrong inverse trig function (use cos⁻¹, not sin⁻¹).
4. Confusing projection (a vector) with scalar projection (a scalar).
5. Applying the dot product to non-vector quantities.
6. Forgetting to normalise vectors before computing cos θ.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern
> "a = (1, 2), b = (3, 4). a·b = (1×3, 2×4) = (3, 8)" — wrong! a·b = 1×3 + 2×4 = 3 + 8 = 11 (a scalar, not a vector).

## Where This Shows Up in CS

- **Lighting**: diffuse brightness = max(L·N, 0) in the Phong model.
- **Back-face culling**: remove triangles facing away using dot product of camera direction and surface normal.
- **Collision detection**: separating axis theorem uses dot products.
- **Machine learning**: cosine similarity = (a·b) / (|a||b|) measures similarity between vectors.
- **Game AI**: FOV check — dot product of facing direction and direction to player.
- **Physics**: work = force · displacement.

Example — FOV check:
```python
def is_in_fov(ai_pos, ai_facing, player_pos, fov_angle):
    to_player = (player_pos[0] - ai_pos[0], player_pos[1] - ai_pos[1])
    to_player_mag = math.sqrt(to_player[0]**2 + to_player[1]**2)
    to_player_unit = (to_player[0]/to_player_mag, to_player[1]/to_player_mag)
    ai_facing_mag = math.sqrt(ai_facing[0]**2 + ai_facing[1]**2)
    ai_facing_unit = (ai_facing[0]/ai_facing_mag, ai_facing[1]/ai_facing_mag)
    cos_angle = ai_facing_unit[0]*to_player_unit[0] + ai_facing_unit[1]*to_player_unit[1]
    angle = math.acos(cos_angle)
    return angle <= fov_angle
```
