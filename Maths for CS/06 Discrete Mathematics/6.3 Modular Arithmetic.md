## Introduction

Modular arithmetic (also called **clock arithmetic**) is arithmetic where numbers wrap around after reaching a certain value called the **modulus**. `17 mod 5 = 2` because 17 ÷ 5 = 3 remainder 2.

The 12-hour clock works mod 12: if it is 11 o'clock and you wait 3 hours, the time is 2 o'clock, not 14 o'clock — `(11 + 3) mod 12 = 2`.

Modular arithmetic is the mathematical foundation of hash tables, RSA encryption, checksums (ISBN, credit cards), circular buffers, and calendar arithmetic. The key insight: you can reduce numbers before operating on them, preventing large intermediate results.

## New Words

- [[modular arithmetic]] — arithmetic where numbers are considered modulo m: only the remainder matters.
- [[modulus]] — the number m that determines the wrap-around point in "mod m".
- [[congruence]] — a ≡ b (mod m) means a and b have the same remainder when divided by m.
- [[remainder]] — the amount left over after integer division. a mod m = remainder.
- [[clock arithmetic]] — another name for modular arithmetic, named after the 12-hour clock.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: The Modulo Operation

**Builds on:** [[1.5 Division and Remainders]] (quotient and remainder), [[1.4 Multiplication and Times Tables]] (a = q×m + r)

The operation **a mod m** gives the remainder when a is divided by m.

```text
a = q×m + r     where 0 ≤ r < m
```

Then a mod m = r.

### Chunked Example

Question: Compute 23 mod 5.

Step 1: Divide 23 by 5.

```text
23 ÷ 5 = 4 remainder 3
```

Step 2: Check: 4 × 5 + 3 = 20 + 3 = 23. ✓

Step 3: The remainder is 3.

Answer: **23 mod 5 = 3**.

### Chunked Example

Question: Compute 100 mod 7.

Step 1: 7 × 14 = 98. 100 - 98 = 2.

Answer: **100 mod 7 = 2**.

> [!tip] Finding mod without dividing
> Subtract multiples of m until the result is between 0 and m-1. For 100 mod 7: 100-70=30, 30-28=2. Answer: 2.

### Breadcrumb 2: Congruence

**Builds on:** [[6.3 Modular Arithmetic]] (modulo operation), [[1.5 Division and Remainders]] (difference divisible by m)

Two numbers are **congruent modulo m** if they have the same remainder.

Notation:

```text
a ≡ b (mod m)
```

This means a - b is divisible by m.

### Chunked Example

Question: Is 17 ≡ 2 (mod 5)?

Step 1: 17 mod 5 = 2. 2 mod 5 = 2. Same remainder.

Step 2: Check: 17 - 2 = 15, and 15 ÷ 5 = 3 (divisible).

Answer: **Yes, 17 ≡ 2 (mod 5)**.

### Chunked Example

Question: Is 29 ≡ 4 (mod 6)?

Step 1: 29 mod 6 = 5 (since 24+5=29). 4 mod 6 = 4.

Step 2: 5 ≠ 4.

Answer: **No, 29 ≢ 4 (mod 6)**.

### Breadcrumb 3: Modular Addition and Subtraction

**Builds on:** [[6.3 Modular Arithmetic]] (modulo operation), [[1.3 Addition and Subtraction]] (addition properties)

Modular arithmetic works nicely with addition:

```text
(a + b) mod m = [(a mod m) + (b mod m)] mod m
```

You can reduce each number modulo m BEFORE adding. This prevents large intermediate numbers.

### Chunked Example

Question: (24 + 37) mod 10

Step 1: Reduce each: 24 mod 10 = 4. 37 mod 10 = 7.

Step 2: Add: 4 + 7 = 11.

Step 3: Reduce again: 11 mod 10 = 1.

Step 4: Check: 24 + 37 = 61, 61 mod 10 = 1. ✓

Answer: **1**.

### Chunked Example

Question: (134 + 256) mod 10

Step 1: 134 mod 10 = 4. 256 mod 10 = 6.

Step 2: 4 + 6 = 10. 10 mod 10 = 0.

Step 3: Check: 134 + 256 = 390, 390 mod 10 = 0. ✓

Answer: **0**.

> [!tip] This works the same way for subtraction
> (a - b) mod m = [(a mod m) - (b mod m)] mod m. If the result is negative, add m.

### Breadcrumb 4: Modular Multiplication

**Builds on:** [[6.3 Modular Arithmetic]] (modular addition and subtraction), [[1.4 Multiplication and Times Tables]] (multiplication properties)

Same idea as addition — reduce first, then multiply:

```text
(a × b) mod m = [(a mod m) × (b mod m)] mod m
```

### Chunked Example

Question: (23 × 42) mod 7

Step 1: 23 mod 7 = 2 (21+2). 42 mod 7 = 0.

Step 2: 2 × 0 = 0.

Answer: **0**.

### Chunked Example

Question: (17 × 19 × 23) mod 5

Step 1: 17 mod 5 = 2. 19 mod 5 = 4. 23 mod 5 = 3.

Step 2: 2 × 4 = 8. 8 mod 5 = 3. 3 × 3 = 9. 9 mod 5 = 4.

Step 3: Check: 17 × 19 = 323. 323 × 23 = 7429. 7429 mod 5 = 4. ✓

Answer: **4**.

> [!tip] Big numbers become small
> Without reducing first, you would multiply 17×19×23=7429 in your head. With modular arithmetic, you only handle numbers 2, 4, 3, which are much smaller.

### Breadcrumb 5: Modular Exponentiation

**Builds on:** [[6.3 Modular Arithmetic]] (modular multiplication), [[2.9 Indices and Powers]] (aᵇ × aᶜ = aᵇ⁺ᶜ)

Computing aᵇ mod m for large b is surprisingly easy with a trick: **exponentiation by squaring**.

For example, to compute 3⁸ mod 7:

```text
3¹ = 3  mod 7 = 3
3² = 9  mod 7 = 2  (square 3¹)
3⁴ = 2² = 4  mod 7 = 4  (square 3²)
3⁸ = 4² = 16 mod 7 = 2  (square 3⁴)
```

### Chunked Example

Question: Compute 2¹⁰ mod 5.

Step 1: Compute powers by repeated squaring.

```text
2¹ = 2 mod 5 = 2
2² = 4 mod 5 = 4
2⁴ = 4² = 16 mod 5 = 1
2⁸ = 1² = 1 mod 5 = 1
```

Step 2: Write 10 as 8+2: 2¹⁰ = 2⁸ × 2² = 1 × 4 = 4 mod 5.

Answer: **4**.

> [!example] CS connection
> RSA encryption uses modular exponentiation with exponents that are hundreds of digits long. The repeated squaring trick makes this fast — O(log b) multiplications instead of O(b).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Computing a Mod

Question: Compute 157 mod 12.

Step 1: Find the largest multiple of 12 that does not exceed 157.
12 × 13 = 156.

Step 2: Subtract: 157 − 156 = 1.

Step 3: Check: 0 ≤ 1 < 12, so 1 is the remainder.

Answer: **157 mod 12 = 1**.

### Example 2 — Checking Congruence

Question: Is 73 ≡ 1 (mod 8)?

Step 1: Compute 73 mod 8.
8 × 9 = 72, so 73 − 72 = 1. 73 mod 8 = 1.

Step 2: Compute 1 mod 8 = 1.

Step 3: Both have remainder 1, so they are congruent.

Answer: **Yes, 73 ≡ 1 (mod 8)**.

### Example 3 — Modular Multiplication

Question: Compute (48 × 35) mod 11.

Step 1: Reduce each factor modulo 11.
48 mod 11: 11 × 4 = 44, 48 − 44 = 4.
35 mod 11: 11 × 3 = 33, 35 − 33 = 2.

Step 2: Multiply the reduced values.
4 × 2 = 8.

Step 3: Reduce the result modulo 11.
8 mod 11 = 8.

Step 4: Check: 48 × 35 = 1680. 1680 ÷ 11 = 152 remainder 8. ✓

Answer: **8**.

### Example 4 — Modular Exponentiation by Repeated Squaring

Question: Compute 3¹² mod 7.

Step 1: Compute powers by repeated squaring.
3¹ = 3 mod 7 = 3
3² = 3² = 9 mod 7 = 2
3⁴ = 2² = 4 mod 7 = 4
3⁸ = 4² = 16 mod 7 = 2

Step 2: Write 12 as 8 + 4.
3¹² = 3⁸ × 3⁴ = 2 × 4 = 8 mod 7 = 1.

Answer: **3¹² mod 7 = 1**.

## Why This Works

Modular arithmetic works because the remainder operation distributes over addition and multiplication. Adding 14 hours on a 12-hour clock is the same as adding 2 hours — the 12 extra hours wrap around and cancel. Formally, the set {0, 1, ..., m-1} with modular addition and multiplication forms a **ring**: all normal arithmetic rules apply, but with wrap-around.

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Negative remainders
> -17 mod 5 = 3, NOT -2. The remainder r must satisfy 0 ≤ r < m. If your answer is negative, add m to bring it into range.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Confusing modulus and remainder
> When people say "mod", they usually mean the remainder operation. But "the modulus is 7" means the wrap-around point is 7. Keep the two uses straight.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Treating mod like division
> mod gives the remainder, not the quotient. 17 mod 5 = 2 (remainder), not 3 (quotient).

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting to reduce after each operation
> When computing (a × b × c) mod m, reduce after each multiplication. If you multiply first and then reduce, you may overflow in a computer or lose track manually.

## Where This Shows Up in CS

### Hash Tables
```python
index = hash(key) % table_size  # mod operation maps to bucket
```

### Cryptography
RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and elliptic curve cryptography all use modular arithmetic. RSA involves computing c = mᵉ mod n, where e and n are hundreds of digits long.

### Checksums
```text
ISBN-10: checksum = (1×x₁ + 2×x₂ + ... + 10×x₁₀) mod 11
ISBN-13: checksum = (1×x₁ + 3×x₂ + 1×x₃ + 3×x₄ + ...) mod 10
```

### Other applications
- **Circular buffers**: wrap-around using (index+1) mod buffer_size
- **Cyclic redundancy checks**: polynomial division mod 2
- **Random number generators**: linear congruential generators use mod
- **Calendar arithmetic**: days of the week, leap years
- **Colour palettes**: (r, g, b) cycled using mod
