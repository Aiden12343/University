## Introduction

Counting rules answer: **"How many ways?"** without listing every possibility. The product rule (multiplication principle) counts sequential choices; the sum rule counts mutually exclusive options; inclusion-exclusion handles overlapping categories; the complement rule counts by subtracting what you do not want.

These rules are the foundation of probability (how likely is an outcome?), algorithm analysis (how many execution paths?), password security (how many possible passwords?), and combinatorics (permutations and combinations).

The key underlying idea: counting is about **set cardinality**. The product rule corresponds to the Cartesian product |A × B| = |A| × |B|. The sum rule corresponds to union of disjoint sets. Inclusion-exclusion handles non-disjoint unions.

## New Words

- [[product rule]] — if event A can happen in m ways and event B in n ways, then A and B together can happen in m × n ways.
- [[sum rule]] — if events A and B are mutually exclusive (cannot both happen), A or B can happen in |A| + |B| ways.
- [[inclusion-exclusion]] — for overlapping events: |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| - |A ∩ B|.
- [[rule of complement]] — number of ways = total − number of ways it does NOT happen.
- [[counting]] — the process of determining the number of possibilities without enumerating them.

> [!important] You pass this topic only when you can define all five terms without looking.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: The Product Rule

**Builds on:** [[5.1 Set Theory Basics]] (set cardinality), [[1.4 Multiplication and Times Tables]] (multiplication for counting)

If a process has k steps, and step i has nᵢ choices, the total number of outcomes is:

```text
n₁ × n₂ × n₃ × ... × nₖ
```

This is the **fundamental counting principle**.

### Chunked Example

Question: A restaurant has 4 starters, 6 mains, and 3 desserts. How many 3-course meals?

Step 1: Identify each choice stage.

```text
Starter: 4 choices
Main:    6 choices
Dessert: 3 choices
```

Step 2: Multiply: 4 × 6 × 3 = 72.

Answer: **72 different 3-course meals**.

### Chunked Example

Question: How many 4-digit PIN codes are possible? (Digits 0-9, digits can repeat.)

Step 1: Each of the 4 positions has 10 choices (0 through 9).

```text
10 × 10 × 10 × 10
```

Step 2: 10⁴ = 10000.

Answer: **10000 possible PINs**.

### Breadcrumb 2: The Sum Rule

**Builds on:** [[6.4 Counting Rules]] (product rule), [[5.1 Set Theory Basics]] (|A| + |B| for disjoint sets)

If two events cannot happen at the same time (mutually exclusive), the number of ways either can happen is the sum.

### Chunked Example

Question: How many ways to pick a king OR a queen from a standard 52-card deck?

Step 1: There are 4 kings and 4 queens.

Step 2: They cannot be the same card → mutually exclusive.

Step 3: 4 + 4 = 8.

Answer: **8 ways**.

### Chunked Example

Question: A CS student can take a database course from CS (3 offerings) OR a maths course (4 offerings) as an elective. Only one elective is needed. Total choices?

Step 1: The student picks one course, not both. Mutually exclusive options.

Step 2: 3 + 4 = 7.

Answer: **7 choices**.

> [!tip] Product vs Sum
> Product rule: "AND" — both happen together. Multiply.
> Sum rule: "OR" — one of them happens (mutually exclusive). Add.

### Breadcrumb 3: Inclusion-Exclusion

**Builds on:** [[6.4 Counting Rules]] (sum rule), [[5.1 Set Theory Basics]] (set operations ∪, ∩, complement)

When events overlap, adding double-counts the overlap. Subtract it once.

```text
|A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| - |A ∩ B|
```

### Chunked Example

Question: 20 students take Java, 15 take Python, 8 take both. How many take either?

Step 1: |Java| = 20, |Python| = 15, |both| = 8.

Step 2: Apply the formula.

```text
|Java ∪ Python| = 20 + 15 - 8 = 27
```

Step 3: Check by drawing a Venn diagram.

```text
Java only: 20-8 = 12  |  Both: 8  |  Python only: 15-8 = 7
Total: 12 + 8 + 7 = 27  ✓
```

Answer: **27 students take at least one of the two**.

### Three-Set Inclusion-Exclusion

For three sets:

```text
|A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| - |A∩B| - |A∩C| - |B∩C| + |A∩B∩C|
```

### Breadcrumb 4: The Complement Rule

**Builds on:** [[6.4 Counting Rules]] (inclusion-exclusion), [[5.1 Set Theory Basics]] (universal set and complement)

Sometimes it is easier to count what you do NOT want, then subtract from the total.

```text
Number of ways = total - number of ways that do NOT satisfy condition
```

### Chunked Example

Question: How many 3-digit numbers (100 to 999) have at least one digit equal to 7?

Step 1: Total 3-digit numbers: 9×10×10 = 900 (first digit 1-9, others 0-9).

Step 2: Count numbers with NO 7.

```text
First digit: {1,2,3,4,5,6,8,9} = 8 choices (exclude 7)
Second digit: {0,1,2,3,4,5,6,8,9} = 9 choices
Third digit: 9 choices (same as second)
Total without 7: 8 × 9 × 9 = 648
```

Step 3: 900 - 648 = 252.

Answer: **252 numbers**.

### Chunked Example

Question: A password must be exactly 4 lowercase letters. How many passwords contain at least one vowel? (26 letters, 5 vowels: a, e, i, o, u)

Step 1: Total passwords: 26⁴ = 456976.

Step 2: Passwords with NO vowels: 21⁴ (letters b,c,d,f,... no a,e,i,o,u) = 194481.

Step 3: At least one vowel: 456976 - 194481 = 262495.

Answer: **262495 passwords**.

### Breadcrumb 5: Counting Through Products of Choices

**Builds on:** [[6.4 Counting Rules]] (product rule), [[2.9 Indices and Powers]] (repeated multiplication as powers)

In many problems, you combine product rule, sum rule, and complement across steps.

### Chunked Example

Question: How many 4-digit numbers are even and have no repeated digits?

Step 1: The last digit must be even: {0,2,4,6,8} = 5 choices.

Step 2: Case analysis because 0 in the last position is special (the first digit can be 0 if 0 is last? No — the first digit must be 1-9).

Step 3: Split into two cases.

**Case A: Last digit is 0**.

```text
Last digit: 0 = 1 choice.
First digit: 1-9 = 9 choices (cannot be 0, cannot repeat 0 → no extra restriction).
Second digit: remaining 8 choices (exclude first and last).
Third digit: remaining 7 choices.
Total Case A: 1 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 504.
```

**Case B: Last digit is 2,4,6,8** = 4 choices.

```text
Last digit: 4 choices.
First digit: 1-9 except the chosen last digit = 8 choices.
Second digit: remaining 8 choices (all digits 0-9 except first and last).
Third digit: remaining 7 choices.
Total Case B: 4 × 8 × 8 × 7 = 1792.
```

Step 4: Sum the cases: 504 + 1792 = 2296.

Answer: **2296 numbers**.

> [!tip] When to use cases
> If the count changes based on some condition (like whether 0 is used), split into cases, count each case, then add.

## Why This Works

Counting rules come from sets. The product rule corresponds to the size of a Cartesian product: |A × B| = |A| × |B|. The sum rule corresponds to the size of a disjoint union: |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| when A∩B=∅. Inclusion-exclusion adjusts for the overlap.

In [[5.1 Set Theory Basics]], you learned that sets count distinct elements. Counting rules extend this to more complex scenarios.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 - Password Combinations

Question: A password has 3-5 characters. Each character is a lowercase letter (26) or a digit (10). How many passwords?

Step 1: Each character has 26+10=36 choices.

Step 2: 3 chars: 36³, 4 chars: 36⁴, 5 chars: 36⁵. These are mutually exclusive (different lengths).

Step 3: Total = 36³ + 36⁴ + 36⁵.

```text
= 46656 + 1679616 + 60466176 = 622, ... let me compute:
46656 + 1679616 = 1726272
1726272 + 60466176 = 62192448
```

Answer: **62,192,448 passwords**.

### Example 2 - Bit Strings

Question: How many 8-bit strings either start with 1 or end with 00?

Step 1: Total = 2⁸ = 256.

Step 2: A = "starts with 1": 1×2⁷ = 128.
B = "ends with 00": 2⁶×1 = 64.

Step 3: |A∩B| = "starts with 1 AND ends with 00": 1×2⁵×1 = 32.

Step 4: |A ∪ B| = 128 + 64 - 32 = 160.

Answer: **160 bit strings**.

### Example 3 - CS Application: Function Count

Question: How many functions f: {1,2,3} → {a,b,c,d} are there?

Step 1: A function assigns each of the 3 inputs to one of 4 outputs.

Step 2: For input 1: 4 choices. Input 2: 4 choices. Input 3: 4 choices.

Step 3: Product rule: 4 × 4 × 4 = 4³ = 64.

Answer: **64 functions**.

### Example 4 - Number of Subsets

Question: How many subsets does a set of n elements have?

Step 1: For each of the n elements, it is either IN or OUT of the subset.

Step 2: 2 choices per element × n elements = 2ⁿ.

Answer: **2ⁿ subsets** (e.g., n=3 → 8 subsets).

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Using product rule when sum rule applies
> "I can order pasta or salad" = sum, not product. If you can pick ONE of several categories, that is sum. If you pick one from EACH category, that is product.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting inclusion-exclusion for "at least one"
> When a problem says "has at least one X", use the complement: total - has no X. This avoids complex case analysis.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Double-counting overlapping cases
> If you count "starts with 1" and "ends with 00" separately and add without subtracting the overlap, you double-count strings that satisfy both.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Zero as a leading digit
> When counting numbers (not strings), the first digit cannot be 0. Always check this.

## Where This Shows Up in CS

### Password Strength
```text
Number of possible passwords = (charset_size)^(length)
Example: 8-char password with 95 printable chars = 95⁸ ≈ 6.6×10¹⁵
```

### Testing
```text
If a function has 3 boolean inputs, you need 2³ = 8 test cases to cover all combinations.
```

### Other applications
- **Algorithm complexity**: branching factor × depth = number of execution paths
- **Machine learning**: feature combinations = 2^n_features possible subsets
- **Database**: number of possible records in a join
- **Network routing**: number of paths through a network
- **Encryption key space**: product of choices per bit = 2^n
