## Introduction

A **matrix decomposition** factors a matrix into a product of simpler matrices, each with a special structure (triangular, orthogonal, diagonal). This is like factoring a number into primes — the factors reveal properties that are not visible in the original.

From [[7.6 Solving Linear Systems with Matrices]], you already know Gaussian elimination. LU decomposition is Gaussian elimination in matrix form. From [[7.10 Orthogonality and Least Squares]], you know orthogonal matrices and least squares — QR decomposition provides the numerically stable method.

From [[7.9 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors]], you know how eigenvalues describe a matrix's action along special directions. The SVD generalises eigenvalues to non-square matrices and is widely considered the most important matrix decomposition in all of linear algebra.

## New Words

- **[[LU decomposition]]** — A = LU, where L is lower triangular (1s on diagonal), U is upper triangular.
- **[[QR decomposition]]** — A = QR, where Q has orthonormal columns, R is upper triangular.
- **[[SVD (Singular Value Decomposition)]]** — A = UΣVᵀ, where U and V are orthogonal, Σ is diagonal.
- **[[singular values]]** — the diagonal entries of Σ (σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ ... ≥ σᵣ > 0).
- **[[low-rank approximation]]** — keeping only the k largest singular values to approximate A.

> [!important] You pass this topic only when you can define all five terms without looking.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Why Decompose?

**Builds on:** [[7.6 Solving Linear Systems with Matrices]] (solving Ax = b), [[7.10 Orthogonality and Least Squares]] (least squares), [[7.9 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors]] (diagonalization)

Why factor a matrix? Three reasons:

1. **Speed**: Once factored, solving Ax = b for many different b vectors is fast.
2. **Stability**: Computing with factors avoids numerical errors from direct methods.
3. **Insight**: The factors reveal properties (rank, null space, important directions) that are hidden in the original matrix.

| Decomposition | What It Gives | Best For |
|--------------|---------------|----------|
| LU | Triangular factors | Solving linear systems |
| QR | Orthogonal + triangular | Least squares, stable solving |
| SVD | Orthogonal + diagonal + orthogonal | Everything: rank, compression, PCA, pseudo-inverse |

### Breadcrumb 2: LU Decomposition

**Builds on:** [[7.6 Solving Linear Systems with Matrices]] (Gaussian elimination), [[7.3 Matrix Multiplication]]

**LU decomposition** writes A = LU, where L is lower triangular (1s on diagonal) and U is upper triangular.

This is Gaussian elimination in matrix form: the row operations are encoded in L, and the resulting triangular system is U.

Once we have A = LU, solving Ax = b becomes two triangular solves:
1. Solve Ly = b (forward substitution — L is lower triangular).
2. Solve Ux = y (back substitution — U is upper triangular).

These are O(n²) each, much faster than re-doing Gaussian elimination (O(n³)).

#### Chunked Example

Question: Find A = LU for A = [[2, 1], [1, 3]].

Step 1: Gaussian elimination: subtract 0.5 × row 1 from row 2.

Row 2 = Row 2 - 0.5 × Row 1: [[2, 1], [0, 2.5]].

Step 2: U = [[2, 1], [0, 2.5]]. L = [[1, 0], [0.5, 1]] (the multiplier goes in L at position (2,1)).

Step 3: Verify: L × U = [[1, 0], [0.5, 1]] × [[2, 1], [0, 2.5]] = [[2, 1], [1, 3]] = A. ✓

Answer: **L = [[1, 0], [0.5, 1]], U = [[2, 1], [0, 2.5]]**.

### Breadcrumb 3: QR Decomposition

**Builds on:** [[7.10 Orthogonality and Least Squares]] (Gram-Schmidt, orthogonal matrices), [[7.6 Solving Linear Systems with Matrices]]

**QR decomposition** writes A = QR, where Q has orthonormal columns and R is upper triangular.

QR can be obtained by applying Gram-Schmidt to the columns of A. The orthonormal columns of Q are the Gram-Schmidt result, and R records the coefficients.

QR is the method of choice for solving least squares problems numerically: instead of the normal equation (which squares the condition number), solve Rx = Qᵀb by back substitution.

#### Chunked Example

Question: Compute the QR decomposition of A = [[1, 1], [1, 0]] using Gram-Schmidt (from 7.10).

Step 1: We already computed the orthonormal basis from the columns:
u₁ = [0.707, 0.707]ᵀ, u₂ = [0.707, -0.707]ᵀ. These form Q.

Step 2: R = QᵀA:

```
R = QᵀA = [[0.707, 0.707], [0.707, -0.707]] × [[1, 1], [1, 0]]
  = [[0.707×1+0.707×1, 0.707×1+0.707×0],
     [0.707×1-0.707×1, 0.707×1-0.707×0]]
  = [[1.414, 0.707], [0, 0.707]]
```

Step 3: Verify QR: Q is 2×2 orthogonal, R is 2×2 upper triangular.

Answer: **Q = [[0.707, 0.707], [0.707, -0.707]], R = [[1.414, 0.707], [0, 0.707]]**.

### Breadcrumb 4: SVD — The Universal Decomposition

**Builds on:** [[7.9 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors]] (eigenvalues, diagonalization), [[7.10 Orthogonality and Least Squares]] (orthogonal matrices)

Every matrix A (square or rectangular) can be decomposed as:

```
A = UΣVᵀ
```

Where:
- **U** is m×m orthogonal (columns = left singular vectors).
- **Σ** is m×n diagonal (entries = singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ ... ≥ σᵣ > 0).
- **V** is n×n orthogonal (columns = right singular vectors).

The singular values σᵢ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of AᵀA (or AAᵀ). They are always non-negative.

SVD generalises eigenvalues to non-square matrices: instead of Av = λv (direction preserved), SVD gives Aᵥ = σu (input direction v maps to output direction u, scaled by σ).

#### Chunked Example

Question: Interpret the SVD of a diagonal matrix D = [[3, 0], [0, 1]].

Step 1: D is already diagonal. U = I, Σ = D, V = I.

Step 2: Singular values: σ₁ = 3, σ₂ = 1.

Step 3: This means D stretches the first direction by 3× and the second by 1×, with no rotation.

Answer: **For a diagonal matrix, the SVD just reads off the diagonal entries as singular values**.

### Breadcrumb 5: Low-Rank Approximation

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 4

The SVD reveals the rank of A (number of non-zero singular values). It also provides the best rank-k approximation to A:

```
Aₖ = UₖΣₖVₖᵀ
```

Where only the k largest singular values (and corresponding columns of U, V) are kept. This is the **Eckart-Young theorem**: Aₖ is the closest rank-k matrix to A in the least-squares sense.

Low-rank approximation is the foundation of:
- **PCA**: the top singular vectors are the principal components.
- **Image compression**: keep 10% of singular values, discard the rest.
- **Recommendation systems**: approximate the user-item matrix with a low-rank factorisation.

#### Chunked Example

Question: A = [[3, 0], [0, 1], [0, 0]] has SVD with σ₁ = 3, σ₂ = 1. What is the best rank-1 approximation?

Step 1: Keep only σ₁ = 3 and the corresponding columns of U and V.

Step 2: A₁ = σ₁ × u₁ × v₁ᵀ.

Step 3: This gives the closest rank-1 matrix to A, minimising ||A - A₁||.

Answer: **A₁ captures the dominant direction (the first singular vector). The second direction (σ₂=1) is treated as less important**.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — LU Decomposition

Question: Find the LU decomposition of A = [[4, 3], [2, 7]].

Step 1: Perform Gaussian elimination. Subtract 0.5 × row 1 from row 2: R₂ = R₂ - 0.5R₁.
Result: [[4, 3], [0, 5.5]].

Step 2: U = [[4, 3], [0, 5.5]]. The multiplier 0.5 goes into L at position (2,1): L = [[1, 0], [0.5, 1]].

Step 3: Verify: L×U = [[1, 0], [0.5, 1]] × [[4, 3], [0, 5.5]] = [[4, 3], [2, 7]] = A. ✓

Answer: **L = [[1, 0], [0.5, 1]], U = [[4, 3], [0, 5.5]]**.

### Example 2 — QR Decomposition via Gram-Schmidt

Question: Compute the QR decomposition of A = [[1, 0], [1, 1], [0, 1]].

Step 1: Columns of A: v₁ = [1, 1, 0]ᵀ, v₂ = [0, 1, 1]ᵀ.

Step 2: Apply Gram-Schmidt: u₁ = v₁/||v₁|| = [1, 1, 0]ᵀ/√2 = [0.707, 0.707, 0]ᵀ.

Step 3: v₂·u₁ = 0×0.707 + 1×0.707 + 1×0 = 0.707. proj = 0.707 × [0.707, 0.707, 0] = [0.5, 0.5, 0].
v₂ - proj = [-0.5, 0.5, 1]. ||v₂ - proj|| = √(0.25 + 0.25 + 1) = √1.5 = 1.225.
u₂ = [-0.5, 0.5, 1]/1.225 = [-0.408, 0.408, 0.816].

Step 4: Q = [[0.707, -0.408], [0.707, 0.408], [0, 0.816]].

Step 5: R = QᵀA = [[1.414, 0.707], [0, 1.225]].

Answer: **Q = [[0.707, -0.408], [0.707, 0.408], [0, 0.816]], R = [[1.414, 0.707], [0, 1.225]]**.

### Example 3 — Singular Values of a Diagonal Matrix

Question: What are the singular values of A = [[5, 0], [0, 3], [0, 0]]?

Step 1: A is a rectangular diagonal matrix. The non-zero entries on the diagonal are the singular values.

Step 2: σ₁ = 5, σ₂ = 3. The third row of zeros contributes no singular value.

Step 3: Rank of A = 2 (two non-zero singular values).

Answer: **σ₁ = 5, σ₂ = 3. Rank = 2**.

### Example 4 — Condition Number from Singular Values

Question: A matrix has singular values σ₁ = 8, σ₂ = 2, σ₃ = 0.01. What is its condition number κ(A)? How many decimal digits of accuracy are lost in double precision?

Step 1: κ(A) = σ_max / σ_min = 8 / 0.01 = 800.

Step 2: Digits lost = log₁₀(κ) = log₁₀(800) ≈ 2.9. So about 3 decimal digits of accuracy are lost.

Step 3: Best rank-1 approximation keeps only σ₁ = 8. Energy captured = σ₁² / (σ₁² + σ₂² + σ₃²) = 64 / (64 + 4 + 0.0001) = 64 / 68.0001 ≈ 94.1%.

Answer: **κ(A) = 800, losing about 3 digits of accuracy. Rank-1 approximation captures ≈ 94% of the energy**.

## Why This Works

Each decomposition reveals a different aspect of the matrix:

- **LU** shows the elimination steps — the multipliers in L and the pivots in U.
- **QR** shows the result of Gram-Schmidt — the orthonormal basis in Q and the coefficients in R.
- **SVD** shows the fundamental action of the matrix — it rotates (Vᵀ), scales (Σ), then rotates again (U).

The SVD is the most general because it works for any matrix (not just square, not just full-rank) and reveals the rank, the null space, the column space, and the optimal low-rank approximation.

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Assuming all decompositions are unique
> LU is unique if A is invertible and no row swaps are needed. QR is unique if A has full column rank. SVD is unique up to sign flips in corresponding columns of U and V.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Confusing the roles of U and V in SVD
> The columns of V (right singular vectors) are the input directions. The columns of U (left singular vectors) are the output directions. The singular values in Σ connect them: Aᵥ = σu.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Using LU for least squares
> LU is for square systems Ax = b. For overdetermined systems (more equations than unknowns), use QR or SVD.
