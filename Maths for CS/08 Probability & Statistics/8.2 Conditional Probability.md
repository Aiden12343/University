## Introduction

**Conditional probability** P(A|B) is the probability of A **given that B has occurred**. Knowing B changes the universe — we restrict attention to only those outcomes where B is true, and ask what fraction of them also satisfy A.

From [[8.1 Probability Basics]], you know the sample space and how to compute P(A) = count(A)/count(S). Conditional probability updates the denominator from S to B:

```
P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B)
```

Why is conditional probability so important? Because in real life we almost never reason about events in a vacuum — we always have context. A doctor knows the patient has a fever before diagnosing. A spam filter knows which words appear in the email. A self-driving car knows what its sensors detect. All of these are conditional probabilities: the probability of a hypothesis given the available evidence.

Without conditional probability, every CS system that makes decisions from data would be impossible. The entire fields of machine learning, Bayesian statistics, and probabilistic reasoning are built on this single concept.

## New Words

- **[[conditional probability]]** — P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B), probability of A given B.
- **[[joint probability]]** — P(A∩B), the probability both events occur.
- **[[marginal probability]]** — P(A), the probability of A alone (summed over all B outcomes).
- **[[independence]]** — P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B), or equivalently P(A|B) = P(A).
- **[[multiplication rule]]** — P(A∩B) = P(A) × P(B|A) = P(B) × P(A|B).

> [!important] You pass this topic only when you can define all five terms without looking.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Definition of Conditional Probability

**Builds on:** [[8.1 Probability Basics]] (sample space, events, P(A)), [[5.1 Set Theory Basics]] (intersection A∩B)

The conditional probability of A given B:

```
P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)    provided P(B) > 0
```

Think: "Out of all outcomes in B, what fraction are also in A?"

Visually, imagine the sample space S as a rectangle. A and B are overlapping circles inside. Conditioning on B means you zoom in so B fills the entire rectangle — then ask what fraction of B is also covered by A.

#### Chunked Example 1 — Students

Question: In a class of 30 students, 18 study maths, 12 study physics, and 8 study both. What is P(physics | maths)?

Step 1: P(M) = 18/30. P(M∩P) = 8/30.

Step 2: P(P|M) = P(M∩P) / P(M) = (8/30) / (18/30) = 8/18 = 4/9.

Answer: **P(physics | maths) = 4/9 ≈ 0.444**.

This means: if you know a student studies maths, there is a 44.4% chance they also study physics.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Dice

Question: You roll a fair die. What is P(roll > 3 | roll is even)?

Step 1: B = {2, 4, 6}, P(B) = 3/6 = 1/2.

Step 2: A = "roll > 3" = {4, 5, 6}. A∩B = {4, 6}. P(A∩B) = 2/6 = 1/3.

Step 3: P(A|B) = (1/3) / (1/2) = 2/3.

Answer: **P(roll > 3 | even) = 2/3**.

Notice: without conditioning, P(roll > 3) = 3/6 = 1/2. Knowing the roll is even increases the probability to 2/3.

### Breadcrumb 2: The Multiplication Rule

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1

Rearranging P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) gives:

```
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B|A) = P(B) × P(A|B)
```

This is how you compute joint probabilities from conditional ones. It is also how probability trees work: each branch multiplies by the conditional probability given the path so far.

#### Chunked Example 1 — Medical Test

Question: 1% of the population has a disease. A test detects it with 95% accuracy (P(positive | disease) = 0.95). What is P(disease AND positive)?

Step 1: P(disease) = 0.01. P(positive | disease) = 0.95.

Step 2: P(disease ∩ positive) = 0.01 × 0.95 = 0.0095.

Answer: **P(disease AND positive) = 0.0095 (0.95% of population)**.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Drawing Without Replacement

Question: A bag has 4 red and 6 blue marbles. Draw two without replacement. What is P(both red)?

Step 1: P(first red) = 4/10 = 2/5.

Step 2: P(second red | first red) = 3/9 = 1/3.

Step 3: P(both red) = (2/5) × (1/3) = 2/15.

Answer: **P(both red) = 2/15**.

### Breadcrumb 3: Independence

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1

Two events are **independent** if knowing B does not change the probability of A:

```
P(A|B) = P(A)    ↔    A and B are independent
```

Equivalently: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B).

Independence is a special and strong assumption. Many real-world events are dependent — knowing one gives information about the other. Independence lets you multiply probabilities directly without conditioning.

#### Chunked Example 1 — Coin Flips

Question: Flip a coin twice. Are "first flip heads" and "second flip heads" independent?

Step 1: P(H₁) = 1/2. P(H₂) = 1/2. P(H₁ ∩ H₂) = 1/4.

Step 2: P(H₁) × P(H₂) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 = P(H₁ ∩ H₂). ✓

Answer: **Yes. Coin flips are independent**.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Cards Without Replacement

Question: Draw two cards without replacement. Are "first is ace" and "second is ace" independent?

Step 1: P(A₁) = 4/52. P(A₂|A₁) = 3/51. These are different → dependent.

Answer: **No. Without replacement, the events are dependent**.

Step 2: Verify: P(A₁∩A₂) = (4/52)×(3/51) = 12/2652. P(A₁)P(A₂) = (4/52)×(4/52) = 16/2704. These are not equal.

#### Chunked Example 3 — Weather

Question: P(rain today) = 0.3. P(rain tomorrow) = 0.4. P(rain both days) = 0.12. Are today's and tomorrow's rain independent?

Step 1: P(today) × P(tomorrow) = 0.3 × 0.4 = 0.12.

Step 2: This equals P(both) = 0.12. ✓

Answer: **Yes, in this case they are independent** (though in real weather, consecutive days are typically dependent).

### Breadcrumb 4: The Law of Total Probability (Conditional Form)

**Builds on:** [[8.1 Probability Basics]] (law of total probability), Breadcrumb 1

If B₁, ..., Bₖ partition the sample space, then for any event A:

```
P(A) = Σᵢ P(A|Bᵢ) × P(Bᵢ)
```

This lets you compute P(A) by conditioning on which Bᵢ occurred. It is the same as the law of total probability from 8.1, but expressed in terms of conditional probabilities (which often come more naturally from data).

#### Chunked Example 1 — Left-handed Students

Question: 60% of students are male (P(M)=0.6), 40% female (P(F)=0.4). 10% of males and 5% of females are left-handed. P(left-handed)?

Step 1: P(L) = P(L|M)P(M) + P(L|F)P(F) = 0.10 × 0.6 + 0.05 × 0.4 = 0.06 + 0.02 = 0.08.

Answer: **P(left-handed) = 0.08 (8%)**.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Email Routing

Question: 70% of emails are short (< 100 words), 30% are long. 90% of short emails are delivered to inbox, but only 60% of long emails are. What fraction of all emails reach the inbox?

Step 1: P(inbox) = P(inbox|short)P(short) + P(inbox|long)P(long)
              = 0.90 × 0.70 + 0.60 × 0.30
              = 0.63 + 0.18 = 0.81

Answer: **P(inbox) = 0.81 (81%)**.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Medical Testing (Two-stage Conditioning)

Question: A disease affects 2% of the population. A test for it has 98% sensitivity (P(positive|disease) = 0.98) and 95% specificity (P(negative|healthy) = 0.95). What is P(disease AND positive)?

Step 1: P(disease) = 0.02. P(positive|disease) = 0.98.

Step 2: P(disease ∩ positive) = 0.02 × 0.98 = 0.0196.

Answer: **P(disease AND positive) = 0.0196 (1.96%)**.

Extension: What is the overall P(positive)? Use law of total probability:
P(positive) = P(positive|disease)P(disease) + P(positive|healthy)P(healthy)
P(healthy) = 1 - 0.02 = 0.98.
P(positive|healthy) = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05 (false positive rate).
P(positive) = 0.98 × 0.02 + 0.05 × 0.98 = 0.0196 + 0.049 = 0.0686.

So 6.86% of tests come back positive, but only 1.96% of people actually have the disease. This gap is why Bayes' theorem (next topic) is essential.

### Example 2 — Conditional Probability from a Table

Question: A survey of 100 people gives:

|           | Likes Coffee | No Coffee | Total |
|-----------|-------------|-----------|-------|
| Likes Tea | 30          | 20        | 50    |
| No Tea    | 15          | 35        | 50    |
| Total     | 45          | 55        | 100   |

Find P(likes coffee), P(likes coffee | likes tea), and determine if coffee and tea preference are independent.

Step 1: P(coffee) = 45/100 = 0.45.

Step 2: P(coffee | tea) = P(coffee ∩ tea) / P(tea) = (30/100) / (50/100) = 30/50 = 0.6.

Step 3: Since 0.6 ≠ 0.45, the events are dependent. People who like tea are more likely to like coffee.

Answer: **P(coffee) = 0.45. P(coffee|tea) = 0.6. Dependent**.

### Example 3 — System Reliability with Redundancy

Question: A server has two power supplies. The server stays up as long as at least one power supply works. P(PS₁ fails) = 0.1, P(PS₂ fails) = 0.1, and the failures are independent. What is P(server stays up)?

Step 1: Server stays up = NOT (both fail) = complement of P(both fail).

Step 2: P(both fail) = P(PS₁ fails) × P(PS₂ fails) = 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01.

Step 3: P(server up) = 1 - 0.01 = 0.99.

Answer: **P(server up) = 0.99 (99%)**. Redundancy dramatically improves reliability.

## Why This Works

Conditional probability restricts the sample space. Before conditioning, the universe is all outcomes S. After conditioning on B, the universe shrinks to B, and probabilities are re-scaled so they sum to 1 within B.

This "updating with evidence" is the foundation of Bayesian reasoning. Every time you learn something new, you condition on that information — the probability of everything else changes because the universe of possibilities has shrunk.

The multiplication rule P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B|A) is simply the definition of conditional probability rearranged. It is the mathematical basis for all sequential probability calculations (trees, chains, Markov processes).

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A)
> P(rain | clouds) is high. P(clouds | rain) is also high. But they are not the same. P(disease | positive) and P(positive | disease) are very different numbers.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Assuming independence without checking
> Just because events seem unrelated does not mean they are independent. Always verify with data.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Using P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B) when events are dependent
> This formula only holds for independent events. Use the multiplication rule P(A)P(B|A) in general.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting that P(B) > 0 is required
> You cannot condition on an impossible event. Division by zero is undefined.
