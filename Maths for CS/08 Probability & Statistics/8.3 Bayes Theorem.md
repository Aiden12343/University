## Introduction

Bayes Theorem tells you how to update your belief about A after seeing evidence B:

```
P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B)
```

In words: **posterior** = **likelihood** × **prior** / **evidence**.

From [[8.2 Conditional Probability]], you know that P(A|B) and P(B|A) are different. Bayes Theorem gives the exact relationship between them. It is the mathematical foundation of learning from data: start with a prior belief, observe evidence, compute an updated posterior belief.

Why is this revolutionary? Because it turns the scientific method into math:
1. Start with a hypothesis and a prior probability (how plausible it is before seeing data).
2. Collect data.
3. Compute how likely the data would be under your hypothesis (likelihood).
4. Combine them to get an updated probability (posterior).

This is exactly what machine learning classifiers do. A spam filter starts with a prior (e.g., "20% of emails are spam"), then uses the words in an email as evidence to compute the posterior probability that it is spam. Every recommendation system, medical diagnosis AI, and self-driving car perception module does the same calculation.

Bayes Theorem is not controversial — it is a direct consequence of the definition of conditional probability. What is revolutionary is using it as a recipe for learning from data.

## New Words

- **[[Bayes Theorem]]** — P(A|B)=P(B|A)P(A)/P(B).
- **[[prior]]** — P(A), the initial belief before seeing data.
- **[[posterior]]** — P(A|B), the updated belief after seeing data.
- **[[likelihood]]** — P(B|A), how probable the data is under hypothesis A.
- **[[evidence]]** — P(B), the total probability of the observed data.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: The Formula and Its Derivation

**Builds on:** [[8.2 Conditional Probability]] (P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B), multiplication rule)

Starting from the definition of conditional probability:

```
P(A|B) = P(A∩B) / P(B)
```

Using the multiplication rule P(A∩B) = P(B|A) × P(A):

```
P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B)
```

This flips the conditioning: from P(B|A) we get P(A|B).

#### Chunked Example 1 — Rain and Clouds

Question: P(rain) = 0.2. P(clouds|rain) = 0.9. P(clouds|no rain) = 0.3. What is P(rain|clouds)?

Step 1: Compute P(clouds) using law of total probability:
P(C) = P(C|R)P(R) + P(C|not R)P(not R) = 0.9×0.2 + 0.3×0.8 = 0.18 + 0.24 = 0.42.

Step 2: Apply Bayes: P(R|C) = P(C|R)P(R) / P(C) = 0.9×0.2 / 0.42 = 0.18 / 0.42 = 0.429.

Answer: **P(rain|clouds) ≈ 0.429 (42.9%)**.

Note: The prior was P(rain) = 0.2. After seeing clouds, it more than doubles to 0.429 — evidence matters.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Spam Detection

Question: 25% of emails are spam. P("free" | spam) = 0.6. P("free" | not spam) = 0.05. If an email contains "free", what is P(spam | "free")?

Step 1: P(spam) = 0.25. P("free"|spam) = 0.6. P("free"|not spam) = 0.05.

Step 2: P("free") = 0.6×0.25 + 0.05×0.75 = 0.15 + 0.0375 = 0.1875.

Step 3: P(spam|"free") = 0.6×0.25 / 0.1875 = 0.15 / 0.1875 = 0.8.

Answer: **P(spam | "free") = 0.8 (80%)**.

### Breadcrumb 2: The Medical Test Paradox

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1

This classic example shows why a "very accurate" test can still give a low probability.

A disease affects 1% of people. A test is 95% accurate: P(positive | disease) = 0.95, P(negative | healthy) = 0.95 (5% false positive rate). You test positive. What is P(disease | positive)?

#### Chunked Example

Step 1: P(D) = 0.01 (prior). P(not D) = 0.99. P(pos|D) = 0.95 (likelihood). P(pos|not D) = 0.05.

Step 2: P(pos) = P(pos|D)P(D) + P(pos|not D)P(not D) = 0.95×0.01 + 0.05×0.99 = 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059.

Step 3: P(D|pos) = 0.95 × 0.01 / 0.059 = 0.0095 / 0.059 ≈ 0.161.

Answer: **P(disease | positive) ≈ 16%. Even with a 95% accurate test, the probability is only 16% because the disease is rare**.

The intuition: the false positive rate (5%) applied to the 99% healthy population produces many false positives. Even though the test catches 95% of actual cases, the sheer number of healthy people means most positive tests are false. This is called the **base rate fallacy**.

### Breadcrumb 3: Sequential Updating

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1

Bayes Theorem supports sequential learning: the posterior from one observation becomes the prior for the next.

```
Prior → Evidence₁ → Posterior₁ → Evidence₂ → Posterior₂ → ...
```

This models how humans learn: each new piece of evidence refines our belief.

#### Chunked Example 1 — Two Positive Tests

Question: Continuing from Breadcrumb 2, you take a second independent test, also positive. What is P(D|both positive)?

Step 1: The posterior from the first test (16%) becomes the prior for the second test.
P(D) = 0.161, P(not D) = 0.839. Same test accuracy: P(pos|D)=0.95, P(pos|not D)=0.05.

Step 2: P(both pos) = 0.95×0.161 + 0.05×0.839 = 0.153 + 0.042 = 0.195.

Step 3: P(D|both pos) = 0.95 × 0.161 / 0.195 = 0.153 / 0.195 ≈ 0.785.

Answer: **After two positive tests, P(D) ≈ 78.5%**.

Two positive tests dramatically increase confidence because the false positive rate compounds: P(two false positives) = 0.05² = 0.0025.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Multiple Spam Words

Question: An email contains both "free" and "offer". P("free"|spam) = 0.6. P("offer"|spam) = 0.5. P("free"|not spam) = 0.05. P("offer"|not spam) = 0.04. Assume words are conditionally independent given spam/not spam. Prior P(spam) = 0.25. What is P(spam | both words)?

Step 1: P(spam) = 0.25 (prior). After seeing "free" (from Breadcrumb 1 Ex 2): P(spam|"free") = 0.8. This becomes the new prior.

Step 2: P("offer"|spam) = 0.5, P("offer"|not spam) = 0.04.
P("offer") = 0.5×0.8 + 0.04×0.2 = 0.4 + 0.008 = 0.408.

Step 3: P(spam|"free" and "offer") = 0.5 × 0.8 / 0.408 = 0.4 / 0.408 ≈ 0.98.

Answer: **P(spam | both words) ≈ 0.98 (98%)**.

### Breadcrumb 4: Multiple Hypotheses

**Builds on:** [[8.1 Probability Basics]] (law of total probability), Breadcrumb 1

When there are multiple hypotheses H₁, H₂, ..., Hₖ that partition the space:

```
P(Hᵢ|data) = P(data|Hᵢ) × P(Hᵢ) / Σⱼ P(data|Hⱼ)P(Hⱼ)
```

The denominator is P(data) computed via the law of total probability.

#### Chunked Example — Three Coins

Question: Three coins: one fair (P(H)=0.5), one biased (P(H)=0.7), one double-headed (P(H)=1). Pick one at random, flip, get heads. What is P(fair | heads)?

Step 1: Priors: P(F)=P(B)=P(D)=1/3.

Step 2: Likelihoods: P(H|F)=0.5, P(H|B)=0.7, P(H|D)=1.0.

Step 3: P(H) = 0.5×1/3 + 0.7×1/3 + 1.0×1/3 = (0.5+0.7+1.0)/3 = 2.2/3 ≈ 0.733.

Step 4: P(F|H) = 0.5 × (1/3) / 0.733 = 0.167 / 0.733 ≈ 0.227.

Answer: **P(fair | heads) ≈ 0.227 (22.7%)**.

Extension: What about P(double-headed | heads)? P(D|H) = 1.0 × (1/3) / 0.733 = 0.333 / 0.733 ≈ 0.455. The double-headed coin is now the most likely (45.5%).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Diagnostic Testing with Different Priors

Question: Two hospitals use the same test (95% sensitivity, 95% specificity). Hospital A serves a high-risk population with 10% disease prevalence. Hospital B serves the general population with 1% prevalence. In each hospital, a patient tests positive. What is P(disease | positive) at each hospital?

Hospital A (10% prevalence):
Step 1: P(D) = 0.10. P(pos) = 0.95×0.10 + 0.05×0.90 = 0.095 + 0.045 = 0.140.
Step 2: P(D|pos) = 0.95×0.10 / 0.140 = 0.095 / 0.140 ≈ 0.679.

Hospital B (1% prevalence):
Step 1: P(D) = 0.01. P(pos) = 0.95×0.01 + 0.05×0.99 = 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059.
Step 2: P(D|pos) = 0.95×0.01 / 0.059 = 0.0095 / 0.059 ≈ 0.161.

Answer: **Hospital A: 67.9%. Hospital B: 16.1%**. Same test, same result, but different priors → different posteriors. This shows why context (prior) is essential.

### Example 2 — The Prosecutor's Fallacy

Question: A crime is committed. A DNA match has P(match | innocent) = 1 in 1,000,000. The suspect's DNA matches. The prosecutor argues: "The probability of a match if innocent is 0.000001, so the probability the suspect is guilty is 0.999999." What is wrong with this reasoning?

Step 1: The prosecutor confused P(innocent | match) with P(match | innocent). These are different.

Step 2: Let city population = 1,000,000. Prior P(guilty) = 1/1,000,000 (one guilty person, assuming one perpetrator).

Step 3: P(match | guilty) = 1 (the guilty person's DNA matches). P(match | innocent) = 1/1,000,000.
P(match) = 1×(1/1,000,000) + (1/1,000,000)×(999,999/1,000,000) ≈ 2/1,000,000.

Step 4: P(guilty | match) = 1 × (1/1,000,000) / (2/1,000,000) = 0.5.

Answer: **P(guilty | DNA match) = 50%, not 99.9999%**. The prosecutor ignored how many innocent people would also match (about 1 in a million of the 999,999 innocent people ≈ 1 person). The match doubles the probability from 1 in a million to 2 in a million — meaningful but not certainty.

### Example 3 — Multi-Evidence Diagnostic Test

Question: A disease affects 1% of people. Test T₁ has 90% sensitivity (P(positive|disease)=0.9) and 90% specificity (P(negative|healthy)=0.9). A second independent test T₂ has 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity. A patient tests positive on both tests. What is P(disease | both positive)?

Step 1: Prior: P(D) = 0.01. P(not D) = 0.99.

Step 2: For T₁: P(pos₁|D)=0.9, P(pos₁|not D)=0.1.
For T₂ (independent given disease status): P(pos₂|D)=0.95, P(pos₂|not D)=0.05.

Step 3: P(both pos | D) = 0.9 × 0.95 = 0.855 (conditional independence).
P(both pos | not D) = 0.1 × 0.05 = 0.005.

Step 4: P(both pos) = 0.855 × 0.01 + 0.005 × 0.99 = 0.00855 + 0.00495 = 0.0135.

Step 5: P(D | both pos) = 0.855 × 0.01 / 0.0135 = 0.00855 / 0.0135 ≈ 0.633.

Answer: **P(disease | both positive) ≈ 63.3%**. With a single positive test (90% accuracy), the posterior would be about 8.3%. Two independent positive tests raise it to 63.3% — each test adds significant evidence.

## Why This Works

Bayes Theorem follows directly from the definition of conditional probability. Its power comes from flipping the conditioning — we often know P(B|A) (how likely the data is under a hypothesis) but want P(A|B) (how likely the hypothesis is given the data).

The sequential updating property models how learning works: every new piece of evidence updates our beliefs, and today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. This is why Bayesian methods are the dominant framework in modern ML — they naturally support learning from streaming data.

The denominator P(B) ensures normalisation: P(A|B) + P(not A|B) = 1. It can be thought of as the "total probability of the evidence across all hypotheses."

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Ignoring the prior
> P(A|B) depends on P(A). A rare disease with a positive test still likely means no disease because the prior was so low. Ignoring the prior gives a misleading answer.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Confusing P(B|A) with P(A|B)
> P(positive | disease) = 0.95 does NOT mean P(disease | positive) = 0.95. They are different quantities. This is called the **prosecutor's fallacy**.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting the denominator P(B)
> The denominator normalises: P(A|B) + P(not A|B) = 1. Without it, the posterior probabilities would not sum to 1.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Applying Bayes without checking the partition
> The hypotheses in the denominator must partition the space (mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive).
