## Introduction

A **continuous** random variable can take any value in an interval. Height, time, temperature, and test scores are continuous. Unlike discrete distributions with probability mass at specific points (like Binomial k=0,1,...,n), continuous distributions spread probability as a **density** over the real line.

Key idea: P(X = a) = 0 for any specific value a. Probability only exists over intervals: P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = area under the curve between a and b.

From [[8.5 Discrete Distributions]], you know PMFs — functions that give probability at a point. Continuous distributions use **PDFs** (probability density functions) instead. A PDF gives density at a point, and the **area** under the PDF gives probability.

Why do continuous distributions matter? Most real-world measurements are continuous: time, distance, temperature, voltage, pixel intensity, audio amplitude. Every sensor produces continuous values. Machine learning models must handle continuous features. The normal distribution in particular appears everywhere because of the **Central Limit Theorem**: whenever you average many independent random variables, the result is approximately normal. This is why IQ scores, measurement errors, stock returns (over long periods), and sampling distributions all follow the bell curve.

## New Words

- **[[probability density function (PDF)]]** — f(x), the curve that describes relative likelihood. Total area under f(x) = 1.
- **[[cumulative distribution function (CDF)]]** — F(x) = P(X ≤ x), the area under the PDF to the left of x.
- **[[normal distribution]]** — X ∼ N(μ, σ²), the bell curve, the most common continuous distribution.
- **[[standard normal distribution]]** — Z ∼ N(0, 1), a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1.
- **[[z-score]]** — z = (x - μ)/σ, how many standard deviations x is from the mean.
- **[[empirical rule]]** — 68-95-99.7: 68% within 1σ, 95% within 2σ, 99.7% within 3σ.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: From Discrete to Continuous — PDFs and Area

**Builds on:** [[8.5 Discrete Distributions]] (PMF vs CDF for discrete), [[8.4 Random Variables and Expected Value]] (E[X], Var(X))

For discrete distributions, we can ask "what is P(X=3)?" and get a nonzero answer. For continuous distributions, P(X=a)=0 because there are infinitely many possible values. Instead, probability is the **area under the PDF**:

```
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = area under f(x) from a to b
```

Properties of a PDF f(x):
- f(x) ≥ 0 for all x
- Total area under f(x) = 1
- F(x) = P(X ≤ x) = area under f(x) from -∞ to x (the CDF)

#### Chunked Example 1 — Uniform Distribution (Rectangle)

Question: X is uniform on [0, 10]. The PDF is f(x) = 0.1 for 0 ≤ x ≤ 10, and 0 elsewhere. Why is the total area 1? Find P(2 ≤ X ≤ 5).

Step 1: The PDF is a rectangle with base = 10 (from 0 to 10) and height = 0.1. Area = base × height = 10 × 0.1 = 1. ✓

Step 2: P(2 ≤ X ≤ 5) = area of the rectangle from 2 to 5. Base = 3, height = 0.1. Area = 3 × 0.1 = 0.3.

Answer: **P(2 ≤ X ≤ 5) = 0.3**.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Triangular PDF

Question: A PDF is shaped like a triangle from x=0 to x=2, with peak at x=0. f(0)=1, f(2)=0. Show the total area is 1. Find P(0 ≤ X ≤ 1).

Step 1: The PDF is a triangle with base = 2 (from 0 to 2) and height = 1. Area of a triangle = ½ × base × height = ½ × 2 × 1 = 1. ✓

Step 2: P(0 ≤ X ≤ 1) is the area of a smaller triangle from 0 to 1. At x=1, f(1)=0.5 (linear slope). Area = ½ × 1 × 0.5 = 0.25.

Answer: **P(0 ≤ X ≤ 1) = 0.25**.

#### Chunked Example 3 — CDF from PDF

Question: For the uniform distribution on [0, 10], what is the CDF F(7)?

Step 1: F(7) = P(X ≤ 7) = area under f(x) from 0 to 7.

Step 2: Base = 7, height = 0.1. Area = 7 × 0.1 = 0.7.

Answer: **F(7) = 0.7. 70% of values are ≤ 7**.

### Breadcrumb 2: Expected Value and Variance for Continuous

**Builds on:** [[8.4 Random Variables and Expected Value]] (E[X] and Var(X) formulas)

For continuous distributions with PDF f(x), the expected value is the "centre of mass" of the shape. Variance measures spread around that centre.

For the uniform distribution on [a,b]:
```
E[X] = (a+b)/2      (midpoint of the interval)
Var(X) = (b-a)²/12  (spread depends on interval width)
```

#### Chunked Example 1 — Uniform Distribution

Question: X is uniform on [0, 10]. Find E[X] and Var(X).

Step 1: E[X] = (0+10)/2 = 5.

Step 2: Var(X) = (10-0)²/12 = 100/12 ≈ 8.333.

Answer: **E[X] = 5, Var(X) ≈ 8.333**.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Wider Uniform

Question: X is uniform on [0, 20]. Compare E[X] and Var(X) to the [0,10] case.

Step 1: E[X] = (0+20)/2 = 10 (larger centre).

Step 2: Var(X) = (20-0)²/12 = 400/12 ≈ 33.33 (4× the variance of [0,10]).

Answer: **Doubling the interval width quadruples the variance (spread increases dramatically)**.

### Breadcrumb 3: The Normal Distribution

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1 (PDF concept)

The normal distribution is a symmetric bell-shaped curve. It is the most important distribution in statistics because of the Central Limit Theorem (sums of independent random variables approach a normal distribution).

```
X ∼ N(μ, σ²)
```

The PDF is bell-shaped, symmetric around μ. The mean, median, and mode are all at μ. The spread is controlled by σ (standard deviation). The total area under the curve is 1, with most area concentrated near μ.

#### Chunked Example — Comparing Normal Curves

Question: Two normal distributions: X ∼ N(0, 1) and Y ∼ N(3, 4). Which has a taller peak? Which is more spread out?

Step 1: σ² = 1 (σ = 1) for X. σ² = 4 (σ = 2) for Y.

Step 2: A smaller σ means a taller, narrower bell. A larger σ means a shorter, wider bell.

Answer: **X has a taller peak. Y is more spread out (σ=2 vs σ=1)**.

### Breadcrumb 4: The Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7)

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 3

For any normal distribution:

```
P(μ - σ ≤ X ≤ μ + σ) ≈ 0.68   (68%)
P(μ - 2σ ≤ X ≤ μ + 2σ) ≈ 0.95   (95%)
P(μ - 3σ ≤ X ≤ μ + 3σ) ≈ 0.997  (99.7%)
```

This means: about 68% of values are within 1 standard deviation of the mean, 95% within 2σ, and 99.7% within 3σ. The remaining 0.3% beyond 3σ are considered extreme outliers.

#### Chunked Example 1 — IQ Scores

Question: IQ scores are N(100, 15²). What percentage of people have IQ between 85 and 115? Between 70 and 130?

Step 1: 85 = 100 - 1×15 = μ - σ. 115 = μ + σ.

Step 2: 68% of people have IQ between 85 and 115 (within 1σ).

Step 3: 70 = 100 - 2×15 = μ - 2σ. 130 = μ + 2σ.

Answer: **68% between 85-115. 95% between 70-130**.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Unusual Observations

Question: An IQ of 145 is observed. How unusual is this?

Step 1: z = (145 - 100)/15 = 45/15 = 3. This is 3σ above the mean.

Step 2: Only 0.15% of people have IQ > 145 (half of the 0.3% outside 3σ is in the upper tail).

Answer: **Only about 0.15% of people have IQ ≥ 145 — very unusual**.

### Breadcrumb 5: Z-Scores and the Standard Normal Table

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 3, Breadcrumb 4

The standard normal distribution Z ∼ N(0, 1) has mean 0 and variance 1. Any normal distribution can be converted to the standard normal:

```
z = (x - μ)/σ
```

This z-score tells you how many standard deviations x is from the mean. You can then look up P(Z ≤ z) using a **z-table** (standard normal table).

The z-table gives the area under the standard normal curve to the left of a given z-score.

#### Chunked Example 1 — IQ ≤ 130

Question: IQ scores N(100, 15²). What percentage of people have IQ ≤ 130?

Step 1: z = (130 - 100)/15 = 30/15 = 2.

Step 2: Using the empirical rule: P(Z ≤ 2) = 97.5% (half of the 5% outside 2σ is in the upper tail, so 95% + 2.5% = 97.5%).

Answer: **≈ 97.5% of people have IQ ≤ 130**.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Percentile to Score

Question: What IQ score is at the 84th percentile?

Step 1: From the empirical rule, 84th percentile ≈ 1 standard deviation above the mean (50% + 34% = 84%).

Step 2: x = μ + zσ = 100 + 1×15 = 115.

Answer: **IQ ≈ 115 is at the 84th percentile**.

### Breadcrumb 6: Using Z-Tables for Precise Probabilities

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 5

When the z-score is not a whole number, use a z-table. The table rows give the first two digits of z, and columns give the second decimal place.

For example, to find P(Z ≤ 1.23): look up row 1.2, column 0.03 → table value ≈ 0.8907.

#### Chunked Example 1 — IQ Score

Question: IQ scores N(100, 15²). Find P(IQ ≤ 110).

Step 1: z = (110-100)/15 = 10/15 ≈ 0.67.

Step 2: Z-table for z=0.67: P(Z ≤ 0.67) ≈ 0.7486.

Answer: **P(IQ ≤ 110) ≈ 0.749 (74.9%)**.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Between Two Values

Question: N(100, 15²). Find P(90 ≤ IQ ≤ 120).

Step 1: z₁ = (90-100)/15 = -10/15 = -0.67. z₂ = (120-100)/15 = 20/15 = 1.33.

Step 2: From z-table: P(Z ≤ -0.67) ≈ 0.2514. P(Z ≤ 1.33) ≈ 0.9082.

Step 3: P(-0.67 ≤ Z ≤ 1.33) = 0.9082 - 0.2514 = 0.6568.

Answer: **P(90 ≤ IQ ≤ 120) ≈ 0.657 (65.7%)**.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Normal Approximation to Binomial

Question: A fair coin is flipped 100 times. Let X = number of heads. We can approximate X as N(np, np(1-p)) = N(50, 25). Using this normal approximation, estimate P(45 ≤ X ≤ 55).

Step 1: μ = 50, σ = √25 = 5.

Step 2: z₁ = (45-50)/5 = -1. z₂ = (55-50)/5 = 1.

Step 3: By the empirical rule: P(-1 ≤ Z ≤ 1) ≈ 0.68.

Answer: **P(45 ≤ X ≤ 55) ≈ 68%** (the exact Binomial probability is about 72.8% — the normal approximation is reasonably close).

### Example 2 — Outlier Detection with Z-Scores

Question: A server's response times (ms) follow N(200, 30²). A particular request takes 290 ms. Should this be flagged as an anomaly?

Step 1: z = (290 - 200)/30 = 90/30 = 3.

Step 2: By the empirical rule, only 0.15% of requests take longer than 3σ above the mean.

Answer: **Yes, 290 ms (z=3) is an outlier — only 0.15% of requests are slower**.

### Example 3 — Combining Means and Variances

Question: Independent random variables: X₁ ∼ N(10, 1), X₂ ∼ N(15, 4), X₃ ∼ N(20, 9). Let S = X₁ + X₂ + X₃. Find E[S] and Var(S). What normal distribution does S follow?

Step 1: E[S] = 10 + 15 + 20 = 45. (Linearity of expectation.)

Step 2: Var(S) = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14. (Variance adds for independent variables.)

Step 3: S ∼ N(45, 14).

Answer: **S ∼ N(45, 14). σ = √14 ≈ 3.74**.

### Example 4 — Central Limit Theorem Intuition

Question: You roll a fair die 30 times and average the results. The die has mean 3.5 and variance 2.917. What is the approximate distribution of the sample mean?

Step 1: Each die roll has μ = 3.5, σ² = 2.917.

Step 2: By the CLT, the average of 30 rolls is approximately normal with:
Mean = μ = 3.5
Variance = σ²/n = 2.917/30 ≈ 0.0972
σ = √0.0972 ≈ 0.312

Answer: **The sample mean ≈ N(3.5, 0.0972)**. This is why polling works: even if individual responses are far from normal, the average is approximately normal.

## Why This Works

Continuous distributions model real-world quantities that can take any value. The "area = probability" interpretation comes from approximating a continuous PDF with many thin rectangles — as the rectangles get thinner and more numerous, the sum approaches the area.

The normal distribution arises naturally because of the Central Limit Theorem: whenever you sum many small independent effects, the total is approximately normal. This is why height, IQ, measurement errors, and many other quantities follow a bell curve.

Z-scores standardise any normal distribution so you can use a single table for all problems. The empirical rule (68-95-99.7) is a quick mental approximation that avoids table lookups for common z-values.

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Using PMF instead of PDF
> P(X = a) = 0 for continuous distributions. Always think in terms of intervals and areas.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Confusing σ and σ²
> N(μ, σ²) — the second parameter is VARIANCE, not standard deviation. If X ∼ N(0, 4), then σ = 2 (not 4).

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Interpreting the PDF value as a probability
> f(x) can be larger than 1 (e.g., uniform on [0, 0.1] has f(x)=10). The height of the PDF is NOT a probability; only areas under the PDF are probabilities.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Applying the empirical rule to non-normal data
> The empirical rule only applies to normal distributions. For other distributions, the percentages within 1σ/2σ/3σ can be very different.
