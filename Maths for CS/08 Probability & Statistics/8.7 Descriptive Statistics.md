## Introduction

**Descriptive statistics** turn raw data into understandable summaries. Instead of looking at 1000 numbers, you compute a few key statistics: centre (mean, median), spread (range, IQR, standard deviation), and shape (skew, outliers).

From [[8.1 Probability Basics]], you understand randomness and samples. Descriptive statistics describe the sample you actually have — they do not infer anything about a larger population (that is inferential statistics, covered later in 8.9 Hypothesis Testing).

Why are descriptive statistics essential in CS? Every data science project starts with exploratory data analysis (EDA): before building any model, you must understand your data. What is the typical value? How spread out is it? Are there outliers? Is the data skewed? Answering these questions determines which algorithms will work, whether you need to normalise features, and whether your assumptions are valid. A model built without first examining the data is almost certain to fail.

## New Words

- **[[mean]]** — arithmetic average: μ = (Σ xᵢ)/n (population) or x̄ = (Σ xᵢ)/n (sample).
- **[[median]]** — the middle value when sorted: 50th percentile.
- **[[mode]]** — the most frequent value.
- **[[range]]** — max - min.
- **[[interquartile range (IQR)]]** — Q3 - Q1, the middle 50% of data.
- **[[variance]]** — average squared deviation from the mean (sample: s² = Σ(xᵢ - x̄)²/(n-1)).
- **[[standard deviation]]** — s = √variance, in original units.
- **[[histogram]]** — bar chart showing frequency distribution.
- **[[box plot]]** — shows median, Q1, Q3, and outliers.
- **[[skew]]** — asymmetry: left-skewed (tail on left), right-skewed (tail on right).

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Measures of Centre

**Builds on:** [[8.1 Probability Basics]] (basic arithmetic)

The three measures of centre: mean, median, mode.

**Mean**: Sum all values, divide by count. Sensitive to outliers.

**Median**: Sort values, pick the middle one (or average of two middle ones). Robust to outliers.

**Mode**: The most common value. Useful for categorical data.

Rule of thumb: for symmetric data, mean ≈ median. For skewed data, use the median. For categorical data, the mode is the only measure that makes sense.

#### Chunked Example 1 — Small Dataset

Question: Dataset: {2, 3, 3, 5, 7}. Find mean, median, mode.

Step 1: Mean = (2+3+3+5+7)/5 = 20/5 = 4.

Step 2: Median: sorted [2, 3, 3, 5, 7], middle value = 3.

Step 3: Mode = 3 (appears twice).

Answer: **Mean = 4, Median = 3, Mode = 3**.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Effect of an Outlier

Question: Same dataset plus an outlier: {2, 3, 3, 5, 7, 100}. Compare mean and median.

Step 1: Mean = (2+3+3+5+7+100)/6 = 120/6 = 20.

Step 2: Median: sorted [2, 3, 3, 5, 7, 100], average of two middle = (3+5)/2 = 4.

Answer: **Mean = 20 (distorted by outlier). Median = 4 (still representative)**.

### Breadcrumb 2: Measures of Spread

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1 (mean)

Spread measures how spread out the data is.

**Range**: max - min. Simple but sensitive to outliers.

**IQR**: Q3 - Q1. Q1 = median of lower half, Q3 = median of upper half. Robust to outliers.

**Variance and Standard Deviation**: Average squared distance from the mean. s² = Σ(xᵢ - x̄)²/(n-1) for a sample.

The standard deviation is in the same units as the original data (unlike variance), making it more interpretable.

#### Chunked Example

Question: Dataset: {2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9}. Find range, IQR, sample variance, SD.

Step 1: Range = 9 - 2 = 7.

Step 2: Q1 = median of {2, 4, 4, 4} = (4+4)/2 = 4. Q3 = median of {5, 5, 7, 9} = (5+7)/2 = 6. IQR = 6 - 4 = 2.

Step 3: x̄ = (2+4+4+4+5+5+7+9)/8 = 40/8 = 5.

Deviations: -3, -1, -1, -1, 0, 0, 2, 4.
Squared: 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16. Sum = 32.

s² = 32/7 ≈ 4.571. s = √4.571 ≈ 2.138.

Answer: **Range = 7, IQR = 2, s² ≈ 4.571, s ≈ 2.138**.

### Breadcrumb 3: Visualising Distributions

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1, Breadcrumb 2

**Histogram**: Groups data into bins and shows frequency as bar heights. Shape reveals distribution type (normal, skewed, bimodal).

**Box plot**: Displays five-number summary (min, Q1, median, Q3, max) and outliers. Outliers are points beyond 1.5×IQR from Q1 or Q3.

```
Box plot structure:
  [min] -- [Q1] |========| [Q3] -- [max]
                median
  Dots beyond whiskers = outliers
```

The five-number summary (min, Q1, median, Q3, max) gives a complete picture of the distribution's range and centre in just five numbers.

#### Chunked Example — Interpreting a Box Plot

Question: A box plot shows: min=10, Q1=20, median=30, Q3=45, max=100, with a dot at 100. What does this tell you?

Step 1: The median is 30. Half the data is between 20 and 45 (IQR = 25).

Step 2: The max (100) is far from Q3 (45). The distance from Q3 to max is 55, while from Q1 to min is only 10.

Step 3: The dot at 100 indicates an outlier (beyond 1.5×IQR from Q3: upper fence = 45 + 1.5×25 = 82.5).

Answer: **Right-skewed distribution with one outlier at 100**.

### Breadcrumb 4: Skew

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1, Breadcrumb 2

Skew describes asymmetry in a distribution.

- **Symmetric**: mean ≈ median. (e.g., normal distribution)
- **Right-skewed (positive skew)**: mean > median. Long tail on the right. (e.g., income distribution)
- **Left-skewed (negative skew)**: mean < median. Long tail on the left. (e.g., age at death)

```
Right-skewed:   ▁▂▃▄▅▆▇██▇▆▅▄▃▂▁▁▁▁▁
Left-skewed:    ▁▁▁▁▁▂▃▄▅▆▇██▇▆▅▄▃▂▁
Symmetric:      ▁▁▂▃▄▅▆▇██▇▆▅▄▃▂▁▁
```

#### Chunked Example — Identifying Skew

Question: Dataset: {1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 3, 10}. Is it left-skewed, right-skewed, or symmetric?

Step 1: Mean = (1+1+2+2+2+3+3+3+3+10)/10 = 30/10 = 3.

Step 2: Median = average of 5th and 6th values = (2+3)/2 = 2.5.

Step 3: Mean (3) > Median (2.5).

Answer: **Right-skewed (long tail on the right from the value 10)**.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Salary Data Analysis

Question: A company has 10 employees. Salaries ($K): {35, 38, 40, 42, 45, 48, 52, 55, 60, 200}. Compute mean, median, IQR. What do you conclude?

Step 1: Mean = (35+38+40+42+45+48+52+55+60+200)/10 = 615/10 = 61.5 ($61.5K).

Step 2: Sorted: [35, 38, 40, 42, 45, 48, 52, 55, 60, 200]. Median = (45+48)/2 = 46.5 ($46.5K).

Step 3: Q1 = median of [35, 38, 40, 42, 45] = 40. Q3 = median of [48, 52, 55, 60, 200] = 55. IQR = 15.

Step 4: Compare mean (61.5) to median (46.5). Mean > median → right-skewed.

Answer: **Mean = $61.5K, Median = $46.5K, IQR = $15K. The CEO's salary ($200K) creates right skew. The median better represents the typical employee's salary**.

### Example 2 — Comparing Two Distributions

Question: Two algorithms are tested 10 times each on the same data. Runtime (ms):
Algorithm A: {10, 11, 10, 12, 11, 10, 11, 12, 10, 11}
Algorithm B: {5, 7, 10, 14, 18, 6, 9, 15, 12, 8}

Compute mean and SD for each. Which is faster? Which is more consistent?

Step 1 (A): Mean = (10+11+10+12+11+10+11+12+10+11)/10 = 108/10 = 10.8.
Deviations: -0.8, 0.2, -0.8, 1.2, 0.2, -0.8, 0.2, 1.2, -0.8, 0.2.
Squared: 0.64, 0.04, 0.64, 1.44, 0.04, 0.64, 0.04, 1.44, 0.64, 0.04. Sum = 5.6.
s² = 5.6/9 = 0.622. s ≈ 0.789.

Step 2 (B): Mean = (5+7+10+14+18+6+9+15+12+8)/10 = 104/10 = 10.4.
Deviations: -5.4, -3.4, -0.4, 3.6, 7.6, -4.4, -1.4, 4.6, 1.6, -2.4.
Squared: 29.16, 11.56, 0.16, 12.96, 57.76, 19.36, 1.96, 21.16, 2.56, 5.76. Sum = 162.4.
s² = 162.4/9 = 18.04. s ≈ 4.248.

Answer: **Algorithm B is slightly faster on average (10.4 vs 10.8 ms), but Algorithm A is far more consistent (SD 0.79 vs 4.25). For real-time systems, consistency matters more than raw speed**.

## Why This Works

Descriptive statistics reduce high-dimensional data to a few comprehensible numbers. The mean tells you the centre of mass; the standard deviation tells you the typical distance from that centre; the IQR tells you the range of the bulk of the data.

Using multiple measures (mean AND median, IQR AND SD) gives you robustness — the median and IQR are not distorted by outliers, while the mean and SD are more mathematically convenient.

The five-number summary (min, Q1, median, Q3, max) provides a complete picture of a distribution in just five numbers. The box plot visualises this summary, making it easy to spot outliers and skew at a glance.

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Reporting mean without checking for outliers
> If the data has outliers, the mean can be misleading. Always check with a box plot or compare mean and median.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Using sample variance formula (n-1) for population data
> If you have the entire population (not a sample), the denominator is n, not n-1. The n-1 correction (Bessel's correction) adjusts for the fact that a sample tends to underestimate variance.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Interpreting IQR as "the range of the middle 50%"
> Correct. But be careful: Q1 and Q3 may coincide with data points or may lie between them depending on the convention used.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Assuming symmetric means "normal"
> Symmetric distributions can be very different from normal (e.g., uniform is symmetric but flat, not bell-shaped).
