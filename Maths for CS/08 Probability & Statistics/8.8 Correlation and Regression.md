## Introduction

Do taller people weigh more? Do students who study more score higher? **Correlation** answers "how strong is the relationship?" and **regression** answers "what is the line that best predicts Y from X?"

From [[8.7 Descriptive Statistics]], you can summarise a single variable. Now we look at the relationship between two variables. The key insight from [[7.10 Orthogonality and Least Squares]]: the best-fit line is the one that minimises the sum of squared vertical errors — the **least squares** solution.

Why are correlation and regression essential in CS? Nearly every prediction problem is a regression problem: predicting house prices from features, estimating user engagement from content characteristics, forecasting server load from time patterns. Correlation helps you select which features matter; regression gives you the prediction equation. R² tells you how much of the outcome you can explain. Residual analysis tells you whether your model is missing something systematic.

## New Words

- **[[correlation coefficient (r)]]** — measures strength and direction of linear relationship, -1 ≤ r ≤ 1.
- **[[regression line]]** — ŷ = a + bx, the best-fit line for predicting Y from X.
- **[[least squares]]** — minimises Σ(yᵢ - ŷᵢ)².
- **[[residual]]** — error = yᵢ - ŷᵢ (vertical distance from point to line).
- **[[coefficient of determination (R²)]]** — r², the proportion of variance in Y explained by X.
- **[[outlier]]** — a point far from the pattern; can distort correlation and regression.
- **[[extrapolation]]** — predicting beyond the range of observed data (dangerous).

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Scatterplots and Correlation

**Builds on:** [[8.7 Descriptive Statistics]] (visualising data)

A scatterplot shows each data point as (x, y). The **correlation coefficient r** quantifies the strength and direction of the linear relationship:

- r = +1: perfect positive linear relationship (points on a line with positive slope)
- r = -1: perfect negative linear relationship
- r = 0: no linear relationship
- |r| near 1: strong linear relationship

```
r = 0.9:    ████████████  (strong positive)
r = 0.3:    ████          (weak positive)
r = -0.8:   ██████████    (strong negative)
r = 0.0:                   (no pattern)
```

Correlation does NOT imply causation. Ice cream sales and drowning deaths are correlated (both increase in summer), but one does not cause the other.

#### Chunked Example — Matching r Values

Question: Match each r value to the description: r = 0.95, r = -0.50, r = 0.10, r = -0.90.

Step 1: r = 0.95 → strong positive.
Step 2: r = -0.90 → strong negative.
Step 3: r = -0.50 → moderate negative.
Step 4: r = 0.10 → very weak (almost no linear relationship).

#### Chunked Example 2 — Anscombe's Quartet

Question: Anscombe's quartet shows four datasets with nearly identical r (≈0.816), but very different scatterplots. What does this teach us?

Answer: **Always visualise your data! A single number (r) cannot capture the shape of the relationship**.

### Breadcrumb 2: Least Squares Regression

**Builds on:** [[7.10 Orthogonality and Least Squares]] (minimising squared error), Breadcrumb 1

The regression line ŷ = a + bx minimises the sum of squared residuals:

```
Minimise:  Σ(yᵢ - (a + bxᵢ))²
```

Formulas for slope b and intercept a:

```
b = r × (sᵧ / sₓ)    (slope)
a = ȳ - b × x̄          (intercept)
```

where sₓ and sᵧ are the sample standard deviations of x and y, and r is the correlation coefficient.

The slope b tells you: a one-unit increase in x predicts a b-unit change in y.

#### Chunked Example — Study Hours and Exam Scores

Question: Five students: hours studied (x) = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], exam score (y) = [50, 55, 65, 70, 75]. Find the regression line.

Step 1: x̄ = 3, ȳ = 63. sₓ = √[( (1-3)²+(2-3)²+(3-3)²+(4-3)²+(5-3)² )/4] = √[(4+1+0+1+4)/4] = √(10/4) = √2.5 ≈ 1.581.

sᵧ = √[( (50-63)²+(55-63)²+(65-63)²+(70-63)²+(75-63)² )/4]
    = √[(169+64+4+49+144)/4] = √(430/4) = √107.5 ≈ 10.369.

Step 2: r = Σ((xᵢ - x̄)/sₓ)((yᵢ - ȳ)/sᵧ) / (n-1)

Deviations: x: [-2, -1, 0, 1, 2], y: [-13, -8, 2, 7, 12]

Products: (-2)(-13)=26, (-1)(-8)=8, (0)(2)=0, (1)(7)=7, (2)(12)=24. Sum = 65.

r = 65 / (4 × 1.581 × 10.369) = 65 / (4 × 16.394) = 65 / 65.576 ≈ 0.991.

Step 3: b = 0.991 × (10.369/1.581) = 0.991 × 6.558 ≈ 6.5.
a = 63 - 6.5×3 = 63 - 19.5 = 43.5.

Answer: **ŷ = 43.5 + 6.5x. Each hour studied adds about 6.5 points**.

### Breadcrumb 3: R² — How Good Is the Fit?

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 2

R² = r² is the proportion of variance in Y explained by the regression line.

- R² = 0.81: 81% of the variance in Y is explained by X. 19% is due to other factors.
- R² = 0.25: only 25% explained — weak predictive power.

R² ranges from 0 (no explanatory power) to 1 (perfect fit). It is the square of the correlation coefficient, so if r = 0.5, R² = 0.25.

#### Chunked Example — Study Hours

Question: In the study-hours example, r = 0.991. What is R²? Interpret.

Step 1: R² = 0.991² ≈ 0.982.

Answer: **R² ≈ 0.982, meaning 98.2% of the variance in exam scores is explained by hours studied. Very strong linear fit**.

#### Chunked Example 2 — Weak Correlation

Question: If r = 0.3 between advertising spend and sales, what is R²? Interpret.

Step 1: R² = 0.3² = 0.09.

Answer: **R² = 0.09. Only 9% of sales variance is explained by ad spend — most variation is due to other factors**.

### Breadcrumb 4: Residuals and Diagnostics

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 2 (what residuals are)

Residuals = yᵢ - ŷᵢ. If the model is good, residuals should:

1. Be roughly symmetric around 0 (mean residual ≈ 0)
2. Show no obvious pattern when plotted against x (no curvature or fan shape)
3. Have roughly constant spread (homoscedasticity)

A residual plot with a "funnel" shape (wider at one end) indicates heteroscedasticity. A U-shaped pattern means the relationship is not linear.

#### Chunked Example — Computing Residuals

Question: From the regression ŷ = 43.5 + 6.5x, compute the residuals for x=1 and x=5.

Step 1: x=1: ŷ = 43.5 + 6.5 = 50. y = 50. Residual = 50-50 = 0.

Step 2: x=5: ŷ = 43.5 + 32.5 = 76. y = 75. Residual = 75-76 = -1.

Answer: **Residuals are small (0 and -1), indicating a good fit**.

### Breadcrumb 5: Cautions — Outliers and Extrapolation

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1, Breadcrumb 2

**Outliers**: A single extreme point can dramatically change r and the regression line. Always check a scatterplot. An outlier in the x-direction (high leverage) can pull the regression line toward it.

**Extrapolation**: Predicting outside the range of observed x values is unreliable. The linear relationship may not hold beyond the data.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Height and Weight

Question: A dataset of 10 people gives x = height (cm), y = weight (kg). x̄ = 170, ȳ = 72, sₓ = 10, sᵧ = 12, r = 0.85. Find the regression line. Predict weight for a person 180 cm tall. Is this extrapolation?

Step 1: b = 0.85 × (12/10) = 0.85 × 1.2 = 1.02. a = 72 - 1.02×170 = 72 - 173.4 = -101.4.

Step 2: ŷ = -101.4 + 1.02x. For x=180: ŷ = -101.4 + 1.02×180 = -101.4 + 183.6 = 82.2 kg.

Step 3: x=180 is within the observed range (we have data from 150-190), so this is interpolation, not extrapolation.

Answer: **ŷ = -101.4 + 1.02x. Predicted weight at 180 cm = 82.2 kg. Not extrapolation**.

### Example 2 — The Effect of an Outlier

Question: Dataset: x = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], y = [2, 4, 5, 4, 6, 20]. Compute r with and without the outlier at x=6, y=20.

Step 1 (without outlier): x=[1,2,3,4,5], y=[2,4,5,4,6]. x̄=3, ȳ=4.2.
r ≈ 0.82 (strong positive correlation).

Step 2 (with outlier): x=[1,2,3,4,5,6], y=[2,4,5,4,6,20]. x̄=3.5, ȳ=6.83.
r ≈ 0.67 (moderate positive — weakened by the outlier).

Answer: **Without outlier: r ≈ 0.82. With outlier: r ≈ 0.67**. The outlier at (6,20) inflates the apparent relationship but makes it less linear.

### Example 3 — Interpreting R² in ML

Question: A linear regression model for house prices achieves R² = 0.72. What does this mean? What is the correlation between predicted and actual prices?

Step 1: R² = 0.72 means 72% of the variance in house prices is explained by the features in the model. 28% is due to other factors (location details, condition, market timing) not captured.

Step 2: r = √R² = √0.72 ≈ 0.85. The correlation between predicted and actual prices is 0.85.

Answer: **R² = 0.72. r ≈ 0.85. The model explains most but not all of the price variation**.

## Why This Works

Least squares regression finds the line that minimises squared errors, which has a closed-form solution thanks to the geometric insight from linear algebra: the vector of predicted values ŷ is the orthogonal projection of y onto the column space of the design matrix (from [[7.10 Orthogonality and Least Squares]]).

Correlation standardises the covariance so it is scale-free and bounded: r always lies in [-1, 1], making it comparable across different datasets.

R² measures how much better the regression line is compared to simply guessing the mean. If R² = 0, the line is no better than using ȳ for every prediction. If R² = 1, the line predicts perfectly.

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Assuming r = 0 means no relationship
> r = 0 means no LINEAR relationship. There could be a strong non-linear relationship (e.g., a perfect parabola).

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Extrapolating blindly
> The regression line is only valid within the range of observed x values. Beyond that, the relationship may change.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Confusing correlation with causation
> r close to 1 does not mean x causes y. There may be a lurking variable (e.g., temperature causing both ice cream sales and drowning).

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Not checking residual plots
> A high R² does not guarantee a good model. If the residuals show a pattern (curvature, fan shape), the model is misspecified.
