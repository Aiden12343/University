## Introduction

The **derivative** f'(x) is the instantaneous rate of change of f at x. If s(t) is position, s'(t) is velocity — how fast your position is changing at that exact moment.

The central problem that gave birth to calculus: **how do you find the slope of a curve at a single point?**

For a straight line, slope is easy: pick any two points, compute rise/run. But for a curve, the slope changes at every point. You need two points to compute a slope, but at the point of interest you only have one. This is the same 0/0 problem from [[9.1 Limits and Continuity]].

The brilliant resolution: pick a second point very close to the point of interest, compute the slope of the **secant line** (the line through both points), then take the limit as the second point approaches the first. The limit is the slope of the **tangent line** — and that is the derivative.

```
f'(x) = lim_{h→0} (f(x+h)-f(x))/h
```

From [[9.1 Limits and Continuity]], you already know how to evaluate 0/0 limits. The derivative definition is exactly that: a 0/0 limit as the two points get infinitely close.

## New Words

- **[[derivative]]** — f'(x) = lim_{h→0} (f(x+h)-f(x))/h, the instantaneous rate of change.
- **[[tangent line]]** — the line through (x, f(x)) with slope f'(x). It "just touches" the curve at that point.
- **[[differentiability]]** — f is differentiable at x if f'(x) exists (the limit exists).
- **[[difference quotient]]** — (f(x+h)-f(x))/h, the slope of the secant line through (x,f(x)) and (x+h,f(x+h)).
- **[[Leibniz notation]]** — dy/dx, df/dx, reads "derivative of y with respect to x."

> [!important] You pass this topic only when you can define all five terms without looking.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: The Definition of the Derivative — From Secant to Tangent

**Builds on:** [[9.1 Limits and Continuity]] (0/0 limits), [[2.2 Simplifying Expressions]] (expanding polynomials)

The **difference quotient** (f(x+h)-f(x))/h is the slope of the secant line. As h→0, the secant line approaches the tangent line, and the difference quotient approaches the derivative.

Understanding the notation:
- x is the point where we want the derivative.
- h is the horizontal distance between our two points (x and x+h).
- As h→0, the two points merge, and the secant becomes the tangent.

#### Chunked Example — Derivative of x² from First Principles

Question: Find f'(x) for f(x)=x² using the limit definition.

Step 1: Write the difference quotient:
```
f(x+h)-f(x) = (x+h)²-x² = x²+2xh+h²-x² = 2xh+h²
```

Step 2: Divide by h:
```
(f(x+h)-f(x))/h = (2xh+h²)/h = 2x+h
```

Step 3: Take the limit as h→0:
```
f'(x) = lim_{h→0} (2x+h) = 2x
```

Answer: **f'(x) = 2x**.

> [!example] Interpretation
> At x=3, f'(3)=6. This means the tangent line at (3,9) has slope 6. If you increase x by a tiny amount from 3, f(x) increases by about 6 times that amount.

#### Chunked Example — Derivative of x³ from First Principles

Question: Find f'(x) for f(x)=x³ using the limit definition.

Step 1: Expand (x+h)³ = x³+3x²h+3xh²+h³.

Step 2: Difference quotient: (x³+3x²h+3xh²+h³-x³)/h = 3x²+3xh+h².

Step 3: lim_{h→0} (3x²+3xh+h²) = 3x².

Answer: **f'(x) = 3x²**.

#### Chunked Example — Tangent Line Equation

Question: Find the equation of the tangent line to f(x)=x² at x=3.

Step 1: f'(x) = 2x. f'(3) = 6 (this is the slope).

Step 2: The point is (3, f(3)) = (3, 9).

Step 3: Point-slope form: y - y₀ = m(x - x₀). So y - 9 = 6(x - 3).

Step 4: Simplify: y = 6x - 18 + 9 = 6x - 9.

Answer: **y = 6x - 9**.

### Breadcrumb 2: Differentiability Implies Continuity

**Builds on:** [[9.1 Limits and Continuity]] (continuity definition), Breadcrumb 1

If f is differentiable at x (the derivative exists), then f must be continuous at x. Why? If the secant slopes approach a finite number as h→0, the function values cannot have a jump at x.

The converse is false: **continuous does NOT imply differentiable**. A continuous function can have a sharp corner where the left and right slopes differ.

#### Chunked Example

Question: Is f(x)=|x| differentiable at x=0?

Step 1: Compute the derivative from the left:
```
f'(0⁻) = lim_{h→0⁻} (|0+h|-|0|)/h = lim_{h→0⁻} |h|/h
```
For h<0, |h| = -h, so |h|/h = -1. f'(0⁻) = -1.

Step 2: Compute the derivative from the right:
```
f'(0⁺) = lim_{h→0⁺} |h|/h
```
For h>0, |h| = h, so |h|/h = 1. f'(0⁺) = 1.

Step 3: The one-sided derivatives differ (-1 ≠ 1), so f'(0) does not exist.

Answer: **No. f(x)=|x| has a sharp corner at x=0 — not differentiable**.

### Breadcrumb 3: Derivatives of Common Functions

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1

These formulas come from the limit definition but are used as shortcuts. Each should be memorised:

```
d/dx[xⁿ] = nxⁿ⁻¹           Power rule
d/dx[sin x] = cos x
d/dx[cos x] = -sin x
d/dx[eˣ] = eˣ
d/dx[ln x] = 1/x
d/dx[c] = 0                 Constant function
```

Notice the pattern: eˣ is its own derivative — the only function with this property. That is why eˣ appears everywhere in growth models.

#### Chunked Example

Question: Find f'(x) for each: (a) f(x)=x⁵, (b) f(x)=eˣ, (c) f(x)=sin x, (d) f(x)=7.

Step 1: (a) Power rule with n=5: f'(x)=5x⁴.

Step 2: (b) eˣ rule: f'(x)=eˣ.

Step 3: (c) sin rule: f'(x)=cos x.

Step 4: (d) Constant: f'(x)=0.

Answer: **(a) 5x⁴, (b) eˣ, (c) cos x, (d) 0**.

### Breadcrumb 4: Leibniz Notation and the Derivative as a Function

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1

In **Leibniz notation**, the derivative is written dy/dx, read "dee y by dee x." This emphasises that the derivative is a ratio of infinitesimal changes.

```
dy/dx = lim_{Δx→0} Δy/Δx
```

The notation is suggestive: dy and dx are thought of as "infinitesimals" — infinitely small changes. While not rigorous without further theory, this notation makes the chain rule (next topic) almost obvious: dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx).

#### Chunked Example

Question: If y = x³, write dy/dx and evaluate it at x=2.

Step 1: dy/dx = 3x² using power rule.

Step 2: At x=2: dy/dx = 3×4 = 12.

Answer: **dy/dx = 3x². At x=2, dy/dx = 12**.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Derivative of a Cubic from First Principles

Question: f(x) = x³. Find f'(x) and f'(1).

Step 1: f(x+h) = (x+h)³ = x³+3x²h+3xh²+h³.

Step 2: Difference quotient: (3x²h+3xh²+h³)/h = 3x²+3xh+h².

Step 3: Limit as h→0: f'(x) = 3x².

Step 4: f'(1) = 3×1² = 3.

Answer: **f'(x)=3x², f'(1)=3**.

### Example 2 — Tangent Line to a Cubic

Question: f(x)=x³ at x=2. Find the tangent line equation.

Step 1: f'(x)=3x². f'(2)=12 (slope).

Step 2: f(2)=8. Point: (2,8).

Step 3: y-8 = 12(x-2) → y = 12x-24+8 = 12x-16.

Answer: **y = 12x - 16**.

### Example 3 — CS Application: Marginal Cost

Question: A company's cost to produce x units is C(x) = 100 + 50x + 0.1x². Find the marginal cost C'(x). If they are currently producing 100 units, estimate the cost of producing one more unit.

Step 1: C'(x) = 50 + 0.2x.

Step 2: C'(100) = 50 + 20 = 70.

Step 3: The marginal cost at x=100 is $70. Producing the 101st unit costs approximately $70 (the exact cost increase depends on the derivative approximation).

Answer: **C'(x) = 50 + 0.2x. C'(100) = $70/unit**.

### Example 4 — Derivative of Sine from the Limit Definition

Question: Show that d/dx[sin x] = cos x using the limit definition.

Step 1: Write the difference quotient:
(sin(x+h)-sin x)/h

Step 2: Use the identity sin(A+B) = sin A cos B + cos A sin B:
(sin x cos h + cos x sin h - sin x)/h

Step 3: Group terms:
= sin x(cos h - 1)/h + cos x(sin h/h)

Step 4: Two important limits:
lim_{h→0} (cos h - 1)/h = 0
lim_{h→0} sin h/h = 1

Step 5: Therefore: f'(x) = sin x × 0 + cos x × 1 = cos x.

Answer: **d/dx[sin x] = cos x**.

## Why This Works

The difference quotient (f(x+h)-f(x))/h is the slope of the secant line. As h→0, the secant rotates to become the tangent line. The limit of these secant slopes is the derivative.

This is fundamentally a 0/0 limit — as h→0, both numerator and denominator approach 0. The genius of calculus is recognising that although the ratio is 0/0 at h=0, the **limit** of that ratio as h→0 exists and gives meaningful information.

The derivative of eˣ is itself because eˣ is defined to satisfy this property. The number e ≈ 2.71828 is the unique base for which the exponential function is its own derivative. This is why e is the natural base for calculus.

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Confusing the difference quotient with the derivative
> The difference quotient (f(x+h)-f(x))/h approximates the derivative for a specific h. The derivative is the LIMIT as h→0, not the quotient at any fixed h.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting the limit
> f'(x) is NOT (f(x+h)-f(x))/h — it is the limit of that fraction as h→0. Without the limit, you have an approximation, not the exact derivative.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Assuming differentiability from continuity
> |x| is continuous everywhere but not differentiable at 0. Continuous functions can have corners, cusps, or vertical tangents where the derivative does not exist.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting to include the denominator h in derivative definition
> f'(x) = lim_{h→0} (f(x+h)-f(x))/h, NOT lim_{h→0} (f(x+h)-f(x)). The division by h is essential — it turns the change in f into a rate of change.

### Gradient Descent — The Fundamental Optimisation Algorithm
```python
# f'(x) tells us which direction decreases f
# Move opposite to the derivative
def gradient_descent(derivative, start, lr=0.01, steps=100):
    x = start
    for _ in range(steps):
        x -= lr * derivative(x)  # x <- x - ηf'(x)
    return x
```
