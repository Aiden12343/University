## Introduction

Differentiation splits things apart — it tells you how fast something is changing at a single instant. Integration puts things back together — it adds up infinitely many tiny pieces to find a total.

If velocity v(t) is the derivative of position s(t), then position is the **integral** of velocity. If you know your speed at every moment, integrating adds up those speeds over time to find how far you travelled.

The symbol ∫ (an elongated S, for "sum") represents this summing-up process:

∫ f(x) dx

This is the **indefinite integral** (antiderivative). A **definite integral** ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx gives a number: the area under the curve from x=a to x=b.

The [[9.1 Limits and Continuity]] limit process reappears: the definite integral is the limit of a sum of rectangle areas as the rectangles become infinitely thin. This is the **Riemann sum**.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) says: ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) - F(a), where F'(x) = f(x). This is why integration and differentiation are inverses.

## New Words

- **[[indefinite integral]]** — ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C, where F'(x)=f(x). The general antiderivative.
- **[[definite integral]]** — ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx, the signed area under f(x) from a to b.
- **[[antiderivative]]** — F(x) such that F'(x)=f(x).
- **[[Fundamental Theorem of Calculus]]** — links differentiation and integration: ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b)-F(a).
- **[[Riemann sum]]** — approximates the area under a curve as a sum of rectangles.
- **[[constant of integration]]** — +C, representing the family of antiderivatives differing by a constant.
- **[[integrand]]** — the function f(x) inside the integral ∫ f(x) dx.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Antiderivatives — Reversing Differentiation

**Builds on:** [[9.3 Differentiation Rules]] (power rule, derivative formulas), [[9.2 Differentiation Basics]]

An **antiderivative** F of f satisfies F'(x) = f(x). Since the derivative of a constant is 0, antiderivatives are only unique up to a constant: ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C.

Basic antiderivative formulas (reverse of the basic derivative formulas):

| Derivative (we know) | Antiderivative (new) |
|---|---|
| d/dx[xⁿ] = nxⁿ⁻¹ | ∫ xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1) + C, n≠-1 |
| d/dx[sin x] = cos x | ∫ cos x dx = sin x + C |
| d/dx[cos x] = -sin x | ∫ sin x dx = -cos x + C |
| d/dx[eˣ] = eˣ | ∫ eˣ dx = eˣ + C |
| d/dx[ln x] = 1/x | ∫ 1/x dx = ln|x| + C |

> [!warning] The power rule fails at n=-1: ∫ x⁻¹ dx = ∫ 1/x dx = ln|x| + C. There is no formula x⁰/0 (undefined).

#### Chunked Example — Basic Antiderivatives

Question: Find each indefinite integral: (a) ∫ x³ dx, (b) ∫ 5 dx, (c) ∫ eˣ dx, (d) ∫ cos x dx.

Step 1: (a) n=3: x³⁺¹/(3+1) + C = x⁴/4 + C.

Step 2: (b) 5 = 5x⁰: 5x⁰⁺¹/(0+1) + C = 5x + C.

Step 3: (c) eˣ: ∫ eˣ dx = eˣ + C.

Step 4: (d) cos x: ∫ cos x dx = sin x + C.

Answer: **(a) x⁴/4 + C, (b) 5x + C, (c) eˣ + C, (d) sin x + C**.

#### Chunked Example — Verifying by Differentiating

Question: Verify that ∫ x² dx = x³/3 + C is correct.

Step 1: Differentiate the right side: d/dx[x³/3 + C] = (3x²)/3 + 0 = x².

Step 2: The derivative equals the integrand (x²). ✓ Verification passed!

Answer: **Confirmed**.

### Breadcrumb 2: The Riemann Sum — Approximating Area

**Builds on:** [[9.1 Limits and Continuity]] (limit concept), [[2.2 Simplifying Expressions]] (summation notation)

The area under f(x) from a to b is approximated by dividing [a,b] into n rectangles of equal width Δx = (b-a)/n. The height of each rectangle is f(xᵢ) for some xᵢ in that subinterval.

**Left Riemann sum**: ∑_{i=0}^{n-1} f(a + iΔx) · Δx
**Right Riemann sum**: ∑_{i=1}^{n} f(a + iΔx) · Δx

As n→∞ (rectangles get infinitely thin), the Riemann sum approaches the exact area — that limit is the definite integral.

#### Chunked Example — Riemann Sum for x²

Question: Approximate ∫₀² x² dx using 4 right rectangles (n=4).

Step 1: Δx = (2-0)/4 = 0.5. Right endpoints: 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0.

Step 2: Rectangle areas:
- x=0.5: f(0.5)=0.25, area=0.25×0.5=0.125
- x=1.0: f(1.0)=1.00, area=1.00×0.5=0.500
- x=1.5: f(1.5)=2.25, area=2.25×0.5=1.125
- x=2.0: f(2.0)=4.00, area=4.00×0.5=2.000

Step 3: Total: 0.125+0.500+1.125+2.000 = 3.75.

Answer: **Approximate area: 3.75. Exact value (next breadcrumb): 8/3 ≈ 2.667**.

Notice that right rectangles overestimate for an increasing function like x².

### Breadcrumb 3: The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

**Builds on:** Breadcrumbs 1 and 2, [[9.1 Limits and Continuity]]

The FTC has two parts that bridge the gap between the Riemann sum and antiderivatives:

**FTC Part 1**: d/dx[∫ₐˣ f(t) dt] = f(x). Integrating then differentiating gives back the original function.

**FTC Part 2**: ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) - F(a), where F'(x) = f(x). The definite integral equals the change in any antiderivative.

This is profound: area (the definite integral) can be computed by evaluating the antiderivative at two points, without any rectangle summing at all.

#### Chunked Example — FTC in Action

Question: Evaluate ∫₀² x² dx using FTC Part 2.

Step 1: Find an antiderivative F(x) = x³/3 (check: F'(x)=x²).

Step 2: F(2) = 8/3, F(0) = 0.

Step 3: ∫₀² x² dx = F(2)-F(0) = 8/3.

Answer: **8/3 ≈ 2.667**. This matches the Riemann sum (3.75 was an overestimate; with more rectangles the sum approaches 2.667).

#### Chunked Example — FTC Part 1

Question: d/dx[∫₀ˣ t² dt] = ?

Step 1: ∫₀ˣ t² dt = x³/3. Then d/dx[x³/3] = x².

Answer: **x²** — the original integrand.

### Breadcrumb 4: Linearity of Integration

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1

Like differentiation, integration is linear:
```
∫ [f(x) + g(x)] dx = ∫ f(x) dx + ∫ g(x) dx
∫ c·f(x) dx = c·∫ f(x) dx
∫ₐᵇ c·f(x) dx = c·∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx
∫ₐᵇ [f(x)+g(x)] dx = ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx + ∫ₐᵇ g(x) dx
```

#### Chunked Example

Question: Find ∫ (3x² + 2x + 1) dx.

Step 1: Split: ∫ 3x² dx + ∫ 2x dx + ∫ 1 dx.

Step 2: 3∫ x² dx = 3(x³/3) = x³. 2∫ x dx = 2(x²/2) = x². ∫ 1 dx = x.

Step 3: Add +C: x³ + x² + x + C.

Answer: **x³ + x² + x + C**.

### Breadcrumb 5: Definite Integrals — Signed Area

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 3

The definite integral gives **signed** area: area above the x-axis is positive, area below is negative.

- ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx > 0: more area above the axis.
- ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx < 0: more area below the axis.
- ∫ₐᵦ f(x) dx = -∫ᵦₐ f(x) dx (reversing the limits flips the sign).

#### Chunked Example — Signed Area

Question: Evaluate ∫₀²π sin x dx.

Step 1: Antiderivative: F(x) = -cos x.

Step 2: F(2π) = -cos(2π) = -1. F(0) = -cos(0) = -1.

Step 3: ∫₀²π sin x dx = F(2π)-F(0) = -1-(-1) = 0.

Answer: **0**. The positive area (0 to π) exactly cancels the negative area (π to 2π).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Definite Integral with FTC

Question: ∫₁³ (2x+1) dx.

Step 1: Antiderivative: F(x) = x² + x.

Step 2: F(3) = 9+3 = 12. F(1) = 1+1 = 2.

Step 3: ∫₁³ (2x+1) dx = 12-2 = 10.

Answer: **10**.

### Example 2 — Area Between Curve and x-axis

Question: Find the area under f(x)=4-x² from x=-2 to x=2.

Step 1: ∫₋₂² (4-x²) dx = [4x - x³/3]₋₂².

Step 2: At x=2: 8-8/3 = 16/3. At x=-2: -8-(-8/3) = -8+8/3 = -16/3.

Step 3: 16/3 - (-16/3) = 32/3.

Answer: **32/3 ≈ 10.667**.

### Example 3 — CS Application: Probability Density

Question: The PDF of a uniform distribution on [0,1] is f(x)=1 on [0,1] and 0 elsewhere. Verify that total probability = 1.

Step 1: ∫_{-∞}^{∞} f(x) dx = ∫₀¹ 1 dx.

Step 2: F(x)=x. F(1)-F(0) = 1-0 = 1.

Answer: **1**. This confirms it is a valid probability distribution.

### Example 4 — CS Application: Average Value

Question: The average value of f on [a,b] is (1/(b-a))∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx. Find the average of f(x)=x² on [0,3].

Step 1: ∫₀³ x² dx = [x³/3]₀³ = 27/3-0 = 9.

Step 2: Average = 9/(3-0) = 3.

Answer: **3**.

## Why This Works

The FTC states that differentiation and integration are inverse operations:

d/dx[∫ₐˣ f(t) dt] = f(x)   and   ∫ₐᵇ F'(x) dx = F(b)-F(a)

This is not an accident. The Riemann sum approximates area using rectangles; as the rectangles become infinitely thin, their sum is the limit that defines both the integral and, in reverse, the derivative.

For probability ([[8.4 Continuous Random Variables]]), the connection is:
- PDF f(x): probability density at x.
- CDF F(x) = ∫_{-∞}ˣ f(t) dt: cumulative probability up to x.
- f(x) = F'(x): the PDF is the derivative of the CDF.

This is why calculus is essential for probability and statistics.

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting the constant of integration +C for indefinite integrals
> ∫ x² dx = x³/3 + C, NOT just x³/3. The constant matters because infinitely many functions have derivative x².

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Confusing definite and indefinite integrals
> ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx is a NUMBER (area). ∫ f(x) dx is a FAMILY OF FUNCTIONS (antiderivatives). They are different objects.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting to subtract F(a) in FTC Part 2
> ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) - F(a), NOT F(b) + F(a) or F(b) alone.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Misapplying the power rule for n=-1
> ∫ x⁻¹ dx = ln|x| + C, NOT x⁰/0 (which is undefined).

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Riemann sum with wrong endpoint
> For right Riemann sums, start at x=a+Δx, not x=a.

### Numerical Integration — Approximating When No Antiderivative Exists
```python
# Some functions have no elementary antiderivative (e.g. e^(-x²))
# We approximate the integral numerically:
def trapezoidal(f, a, b, n=1000):
    h = (b-a)/n
    total = (f(a)+f(b))/2
    for i in range(1, n):
        total += f(a + i*h)
    return total*h
```
