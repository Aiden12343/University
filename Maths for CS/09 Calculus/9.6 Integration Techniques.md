## Introduction

From [[9.5 Integration Basics]], you know how to integrate simple functions like x², eˣ, and cos x. But what about ∫ x·eˣ dx? Or ∫ 2x·cos(x²) dx? Or ∫ 1/(x²-1) dx?

These require **techniques** — methods that reverse the differentiation rules you learned in [[9.3 Differentiation Rules]]:

| Differentiation rule | Integration technique |
|---|---|
| Chain rule | Substitution (u-sub) |
| Product rule | Integration by parts |
| Quotient rule | Partial fractions |

**Substitution** (u-sub) is the most important and most common. It works by recognising "the derivative of the inside function" multiplied by the outside. You set u = g(x), compute du = g'(x) dx, and the integral simplifies.

**Integration by parts** rearranges ∫ u dv = uv - ∫ v du. It is useful when you have a product of two unrelated functions (like x·eˣ or x·sin x).

**Partial fractions** decomposes rational functions into simpler fractions that can be integrated term by term.

## New Words

- **[[u-substitution]]** — ∫ f(g(x))g'(x) dx = ∫ f(u) du where u=g(x). Reverse chain rule.
- **[[integration by parts]]** — ∫ u dv = uv - ∫ v du. Reverse product rule.
- **[[partial fractions]]** — decomposing P(x)/Q(x) into a sum of simpler fractions A/(x-r₁) + B/(x-r₂) + ...
- **[[improper integral]]** — an integral with an infinite limit or integrand that blows up within the interval.

> [!important] You pass this topic only when you can define all four terms without looking.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: U-Substitution — Reversing the Chain Rule

**Builds on:** [[9.5 Integration Basics]] (basic integrals), [[9.3 Differentiation Rules]] (chain rule)

When you see f(g(x))·g'(x), substitution simplifies it to ∫ f(u) du.

Procedure:
1. Identify u = g(x) (the "inner function").
2. Compute du = g'(x) dx.
3. Rewrite the integral entirely in terms of u (no x should remain).
4. Integrate with respect to u.
5. Substitute back to x.

#### Chunked Example — Simple Substitution

Question: ∫ 2x·cos(x²) dx.

Step 1: Notice x² and its derivative 2x appear. Let u = x².

Step 2: du = 2x dx. The integral becomes: ∫ cos(u) du.

Step 3: ∫ cos(u) du = sin(u) + C.

Step 4: Substitute back: sin(x²) + C.

Step 5: Check: d/dx[sin(x²)+C] = cos(x²)·2x = 2x·cos(x²). ✓

Answer: **sin(x²) + C**.

#### Chunked Example — Substitution with Definite Integral

Question: ∫₀¹ 2x·e^(x²) dx.

Step 1: u = x², du = 2x dx.

Step 2: Also change the limits: when x=0, u=0. When x=1, u=1.

Step 3: ∫₀¹ e^u du = [e^u]₀¹ = e¹ - e⁰ = e - 1.

Answer: **e - 1**.

#### Chunked Example — When the Derivative Is Almost There

Question: ∫ x·e^(x²) dx.

Step 1: u = x², du = 2x dx. We have x dx, not 2x dx.

Step 2: Solve for x dx: x dx = du/2.

Step 3: ∫ x·e^(x²) dx = (1/2)∫ e^u du = (1/2)e^u + C = (1/2)e^(x²) + C.

Answer: **(1/2)e^(x²) + C**.

### Breadcrumb 2: Integration by Parts — Reversing the Product Rule

**Builds on:** [[9.3 Differentiation Rules]] (product rule), [[9.5 Integration Basics]]

The product rule reversed: ∫ u dv = uv - ∫ v du.

Procedure:
1. Choose u (differentiate to get du) and dv (integrate to get v).
2. Apply: uv - ∫ v du.
3. The goal: the new integral ∫ v du should be simpler than the original.

> [!tip] LIATE priority for choosing u: Logarithmic, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trig, Exponential. Pick u from the leftmost category present.

#### Chunked Example — Product x·eˣ

Question: ∫ x·eˣ dx.

Step 1: Choose u and dv. By LIATE: Algebraic (x) before Exponential (eˣ). Let u = x, dv = eˣ dx.

Step 2: du = dx, v = eˣ.

Step 3: ∫ x·eˣ dx = x·eˣ - ∫ eˣ dx = x·eˣ - eˣ + C.

Step 4: Check: d/dx[xeˣ - eˣ + C] = eˣ + xeˣ - eˣ = x·eˣ. ✓

Answer: **xeˣ - eˣ + C**.

#### Chunked Example — Product x·sin x

Question: ∫ x·sin x dx.

Step 1: u = x (Algebraic), dv = sin x dx.

Step 2: du = dx, v = -cos x.

Step 3: ∫ x·sin x dx = x·(-cos x) - ∫ (-cos x) dx = -x·cos x + ∫ cos x dx.

Step 4: = -x·cos x + sin x + C.

Answer: **-x·cos x + sin x + C**.

#### Chunked Example — Definite Integration by Parts

Question: ∫₀¹ x·eˣ dx.

Step 1: Antiderivative: xeˣ - eˣ.

Step 2: [xeˣ - eˣ]₀¹ = (1·e¹ - e¹) - (0·e⁰ - e⁰) = (e-e) - (0-1) = 0+1 = 1.

Answer: **1**.

### Breadcrumb 3: Partial Fractions

**Builds on:** [[2.8 Factoring]] (factorising quadratics), [[1.6 Fractions and Mixed Numbers]] (manipulating fractions)

For integrating rational functions P(x)/Q(x) where degree(P) < degree(Q), decompose into simpler fractions.

If Q(x) factors into (x-r₁)(x-r₂)...:
```
P(x)/Q(x) = A₁/(x-r₁) + A₂/(x-r₂) + ...
```
Solve for constants A₁, A₂, ... then integrate each term: ∫ A/(x-r) dx = A·ln|x-r| + C.

#### Chunked Example — Simple Partial Fractions

Question: ∫ 1/(x²-1) dx.

Step 1: Factor denominator: x²-1 = (x-1)(x+1).

Step 2: Decompose: 1/(x²-1) = A/(x-1) + B/(x+1).

Step 3: Solve for A and B. Multiply both sides by (x-1)(x+1):
1 = A(x+1) + B(x-1)
1 = (A+B)x + (A-B)

Step 4: Equate coefficients:
A+B = 0 → B = -A
A-B = 1 → A-(-A) = 2A = 1 → A = 1/2, B = -1/2.

Step 5: Integrate: ∫ (1/2)/(x-1) + (-1/2)/(x+1) dx = (1/2)ln|x-1| - (1/2)ln|x+1| + C.

Answer: **(1/2)ln|(x-1)/(x+1)| + C**.

#### Chunked Example — Repeated Linear Factor

Question: ∫ 1/(x-1)² dx.

Step 1: For repeated factors, the decomposition uses (x-1) and (x-1)².

Step 2: 1/(x-1)² = A/(x-1) + B/(x-1)².

Step 3: Multiply: 1 = A(x-1) + B = Ax + (-A+B).

Step 4: A=0, -A+B=1 → B=1.

Step 5: ∫ 0/(x-1) + 1/(x-1)² dx = ∫ (x-1)⁻² dx = -(x-1)⁻¹ + C = -1/(x-1) + C.

Answer: **-1/(x-1) + C**.

### Breadcrumb 4: Improper Integrals

**Builds on:** [[9.1 Limits and Continuity]] (limits at infinity, vertical asymptotes), [[9.5 Integration Basics]]

An **improper integral** has either:
- An infinite limit of integration (e.g. ∫₁^∞ 1/x² dx).
- A vertical asymptote within the interval (e.g. ∫₀¹ 1/x dx).

In both cases, evaluate as a limit.

#### Chunked Example — Infinite Limit

Question: ∫₁^∞ 1/x² dx.

Step 1: Replace ∞ with a variable b, then take the limit as b→∞.

Step 2: ∫₁ᵇ x⁻² dx = [-x⁻¹]₁ᵇ = -1/b - (-1/1) = 1 - 1/b.

Step 3: lim_{b→∞} (1 - 1/b) = 1.

Answer: **1** (the integral converges).

#### Chunked Example — Vertical Asymptote

Question: ∫₀¹ 1/x dx.

Step 1: The integrand blows up at x=0. Replace 0 with a, take a→0⁺.

Step 2: ∫ₐ¹ 1/x dx = [ln x]ₐ¹ = ln(1) - ln(a) = 0 - ln(a).

Step 3: lim_{a→0⁺} -ln(a) = ∞.

Answer: **Diverges to ∞**. The area is infinite.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Substitution with Definite

Question: ∫₀^{π/4} sin(2x) cos(2x) dx.

Step 1: u = sin(2x). du = 2cos(2x) dx → cos(2x) dx = du/2.

Step 2: Limits: x=0 → u=0. x=π/4 → u=sin(π/2)=1.

Step 3: ∫₀¹ u·(du/2) = (1/2)∫₀¹ u du = (1/2)[u²/2]₀¹ = (1/2)(1/2) = 1/4.

Answer: **1/4**.

### Example 2 — Integration by Parts with Logarithm

Question: ∫ ln x dx.

Step 1: There is no obvious product, but let u = ln x, dv = dx.

Step 2: du = (1/x)dx, v = x.

Step 3: ∫ ln x dx = x·ln x - ∫ x·(1/x)dx = x·ln x - ∫ 1 dx = x·ln x - x + C.

Answer: **x·ln x - x + C**.

### Example 3 — Twice-by-Parts (Cyclic)

Question: ∫ eˣ·sin x dx.

Step 1: u = sin x, dv = eˣ dx. du = cos x dx, v = eˣ.

Step 2: I = eˣ·sin x - ∫ eˣ·cos x dx.

Step 3: Parts again on ∫ eˣ·cos x dx: u=cos x, dv=eˣ dx. du=-sin x dx, v=eˣ.
∫ eˣ·cos x dx = eˣ·cos x - ∫ eˣ·(-sin x) dx = eˣ·cos x + ∫ eˣ·sin x dx.

Step 4: Substitute back:
I = eˣ·sin x - [eˣ·cos x + I] = eˣ·sin x - eˣ·cos x - I

Step 5: 2I = eˣ(sin x - cos x) → I = (1/2)eˣ(sin x - cos x) + C.

Answer: **(1/2)eˣ(sin x - cos x) + C**.

### Example 4 — CS Application: Gaussian Integral

Question: The normal distribution PDF is f(x) = (1/√(2πσ²))·e^{-(x-μ)²/(2σ²)}. The improper integral ∫_{-∞}^{∞} f(x) dx = 1 (total probability).

This requires a famous result: ∫_{-∞}^{∞} e^{-x²} dx = √π. While the proof uses a trick (squaring and converting to polar coordinates), the result is fundamental — it is why the normal distribution has √(2π) in its normalisation constant.

## Why This Works

U-substitution is precisely the chain rule reversed. If F'(u) = f(u), then d/dx[F(g(x))] = f(g(x))g'(x). Integrating both sides gives ∫ f(g(x))g'(x) dx = F(g(x)) + C = ∫ f(u) du.

Integration by parts reverses the product rule: (uv)' = u'v + uv'. Rearranging: uv' = (uv)' - u'v. Integrating: ∫ uv' dx = uv - ∫ u'v dx.

Partial fractions work because any rational function can be decomposed into a sum of terms with linear denominators — this is a theorem from algebra (partial fraction decomposition). Each term A/(x-r) integrates to A·ln|x-r|.

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Using u-sub without completely replacing dx
> All x must be eliminated from the integral. If you have u=x² but still have x in the integrand, you need to express x in terms of u.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting to change limits for definite integrals with u-sub
> When doing ∫ₐᵇ f(g(x))g'(x) dx via u-sub, you can either: (a) change limits to u-values (u(a), u(b)), or (b) substitute back to x before evaluating. Mixing methods causes errors.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Integration by parts: picking u and dv in the wrong order
> Following LIATE helps. If the new integral is harder than the original, you picked u and dv the wrong way.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Partial fractions: not checking degree
> Partial fractions requires the numerator degree to be less than the denominator degree. If not, do polynomial long division first.

### Numerical Integration — Monte Carlo Methods
```python
# When integrals are too hard for analytical techniques:
def monte_carlo(f, a, b, n=100000):
    xs = np.random.uniform(a, b, n)
    return (b-a) * np.mean(f(xs))
```
