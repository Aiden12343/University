## Introduction

A **differential equation** (DE) is an equation that involves a function and its derivatives. While an algebraic equation like x²-4=0 asks "what number x satisfies this?", a DE like y' = ky asks "what **function** y(t) satisfies this?"

Differential equations are the language of change. They describe:
- **Population growth**: dP/dt = rP (growth rate proportional to population).
- **Newton's law of cooling**: dT/dt = -k(T - T_env) (cooling rate proportional to temperature difference).
- **Radioactive decay**: dN/dt = -λN (decay rate proportional to amount remaining).
- **Learning curves**: dL/dt = k(M - L) where M is the maximum knowledge.

From [[9.4 Applications of Differentiation]], you know that y' describes instantaneous rate of change. A DE says: the rate of change of y depends on y itself. This self-referential property is what makes DEs powerful (and sometimes tricky).

The simplest type is **separable**: you can write all y terms on one side and all t terms on the other, then integrate both sides.

## New Words

- **[[differential equation]]** — an equation relating a function to its derivatives.
- **[[order of a DE]]** — the highest derivative that appears (e.g., y' is first-order, y'' is second-order).
- **[[separable DE]]** — dy/dx = f(x)·g(y). Can be separated: dy/g(y) = f(x) dx.
- **[[general solution]]** — the family of functions satisfying the DE (includes a constant C).
- **[[particular solution]]** — the specific function satisfying an initial condition.
- **[[initial value problem]]** — a DE plus an initial condition y(t₀) = y₀.
- **[[equilibrium solution]]** — constant solution y(t)=c where dy/dt=0.
- **[[Euler's method]]** — numerical approximation: y_{n+1}=y_n+h·f(tₙ,yₙ).

> [!important] You pass this topic only when you can define all eight terms without looking.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: What is a Differential Equation?

**Builds on:** [[9.2 Differentiation Basics]] (derivative notation), [[9.5 Integration Basics]] (antiderivatives)

A differential equation involves an unknown function and its derivatives. The **order** is the highest derivative present.

Examples:
- y' = 2x (first-order, the derivative equals a function of x).
- y'' + y = 0 (second-order, the function plus its second derivative equals zero).
- dy/dt = 0.1y (first-order, rate of change proportional to y itself).

The **general solution** contains an arbitrary constant C (because integration introduces C). The **particular solution** pins down C using an initial condition.

#### Chunked Example — Solving y' = 2x

Question: Solve y' = 2x. Find the general solution, then the particular solution with y(0)=5.

Step 1: y' = dy/dx = 2x. Integrate both sides: y = ∫ 2x dx = x² + C.

Step 2: General solution: y = x² + C (a family of parabolas shifted vertically).

Step 3: Use y(0)=5: 5 = 0² + C → C = 5.

Step 4: Particular solution: y = x² + 5.

Answer: **General: y = x² + C. Particular: y = x² + 5**.

### Breadcrumb 2: Separable Differential Equations

**Builds on:** [[9.6 Integration Techniques]] (u-substitution, integration methods), Breadcrumb 1

A **separable** DE can be written as:
```
dy/dx = f(x)·g(y)
```

Solution method:
1. Separate: dy/g(y) = f(x) dx.
2. Integrate both sides: ∫ 1/g(y) dy = ∫ f(x) dx.
3. Solve for y if possible.

#### Chunked Example — Exponential Growth

Question: Solve dy/dt = 0.1y with y(0)=100. Find y(t).

Step 1: Separate: dy/y = 0.1 dt.

Step 2: Integrate: ∫ dy/y = ∫ 0.1 dt → ln|y| = 0.1t + C.

Step 3: Solve for y: y = e^{0.1t + C} = e^{C}·e^{0.1t} = Ae^{0.1t}, where A = ±e^{C}.

Step 4: Use y(0)=100: 100 = A·e⁰ = A.

Step 5: Particular solution: y(t) = 100e^{0.1t}.

Answer: **y(t) = 100e^{0.1t}** (population growing at 10% per year).

#### Chunked Example — Newton's Law of Cooling

Question: A coffee at 90°C cools in a 20°C room. dT/dt = -0.05(T-20). Find T(t), given T(0)=90.

Step 1: Separate: dT/(T-20) = -0.05 dt.

Step 2: Integrate: ln|T-20| = -0.05t + C.

Step 3: T-20 = Ae^{-0.05t} → T(t) = 20 + Ae^{-0.05t}.

Step 4: T(0)=90: 90 = 20 + A → A=70.

Step 5: T(t) = 20 + 70e^{-0.05t}.

Step 6: After 30 minutes: T(30) = 20 + 70e^{-1.5} ≈ 20 + 70×0.223 ≈ 35.6°C.

Answer: **T(t) = 20 + 70e^{-0.05t}**. Coffee cools to ~35.6°C after 30 min.

#### Chunked Example — Logistic Growth

Question: dP/dt = 0.1P(1-P/1000). This is the logistic equation (growth with carrying capacity).

Step 1: Separate: dP/(P(1-P/1000)) = 0.1 dt.

Step 2: Partial fractions on the left: 1/(P(1-P/1000)) = 1/P + 1/(1000-P).

Step 3: Integrate: ln|P| - ln|1000-P| = 0.1t + C → ln|P/(1000-P)| = 0.1t + C.

Step 4: Solve: P/(1000-P) = Ae^{0.1t} → P = 1000Ae^{0.1t}/(1+Ae^{0.1t}).

Step 5: As t→∞, P→1000 (the carrying capacity).

Answer: **P(t) = 1000Ae^{0.1t}/(1+Ae^{0.1t})**. Population approaches 1000.

### Breadcrumb 3: Equilibrium Solutions

**Builds on:** [[9.4 Applications of Differentiation]] (critical points), Breadcrumb 2

An **equilibrium solution** is a constant solution y(t)=c where dy/dt=0. Setting the right-hand side of the DE to zero gives the equilibria.

For dy/dt = f(y), equilibria occur where f(y)=0.

Equilibria can be:
- **Stable**: if y is slightly perturbed, it returns to equilibrium (a sink).
- **Unstable**: if y is slightly perturbed, it moves away (a source).

#### Chunked Example — Logistic Equilibrium

Question: Identify and classify equilibria for dP/dt = 0.1P(1-P/1000).

Step 1: Set RHS=0: 0.1P(1-P/1000)=0 → P=0 or P=1000.

Step 2: P=0: If P is slightly above 0, growth is positive (P increases → moves away). **Unstable**.

Step 3: P=1000: If P is slightly below, growth is positive (returns to 1000). If slightly above, growth is negative (also returns). **Stable**.

Answer: **P=0 unstable, P=1000 stable**. The population tends toward the carrying capacity.

### Breadcrumb 4: Euler's Method — Numerical Approximation

**Builds on:** [[9.2 Differentiation Basics]] (derivative as slope), Breadcrumb 1

When a DE cannot be solved analytically, we approximate numerically. **Euler's method** uses the slope at the current point to step forward:

```
y_{n+1} = y_n + h·f(tₙ, yₙ)
```

where h is the step size and y' = f(t,y).

#### Chunked Example — Euler's Method for dy/dt = y

Question: Approximate y(1) for dy/dt = y, y(0)=1, using h=0.5 (two steps).

Step 1: y₀=1, t₀=0.
Step 2: y₁ = y₀ + h·y₀ = 1 + 0.5×1 = 1.5. t₁=0.5.
Step 3: y₂ = y₁ + h·y₁ = 1.5 + 0.5×1.5 = 2.25. t₂=1.0.

Answer: **Approximate y(1) ≈ 2.25. Exact: y(1)=e ≈ 2.718. Error ≈ 0.468**.

With smaller h, the approximation improves.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Separable DE with Complicated Integration

Question: Solve dy/dx = y²·cos x, y(0)=1.

Step 1: Separate: dy/y² = cos x dx.

Step 2: Integrate: ∫ y⁻² dy = ∫ cos x dx → -y⁻¹ = sin x + C.

Step 3: y = -1/(sin x + C).

Step 4: y(0)=1: 1 = -1/(0+C) → C = -1.

Step 5: y = -1/(sin x - 1) = 1/(1 - sin x).

Answer: **y(x) = 1/(1 - sin x)**.

### Example 2 — CS Application: Learning Curve

Question: A student's knowledge K(t) (percentage) follows dK/dt = 0.2(100-K), K(0)=0. Find K(t) and the time to reach 90%.

Step 1: Separate: dK/(100-K) = 0.2 dt.

Step 2: Integrate: -ln|100-K| = 0.2t + C → ln|100-K| = -0.2t - C.

Step 3: 100-K = Ae^{-0.2t} → K(t) = 100 - Ae^{-0.2t}.

Step 4: K(0)=0: 100 - A = 0 → A=100.

Step 5: K(t) = 100(1 - e^{-0.2t}).

Step 6: Solve K(t)=90: 100(1-e^{-0.2t}) = 90 → e^{-0.2t} = 0.1 → -0.2t = ln(0.1) → t = -ln(0.1)/0.2 ≈ 11.5 time units.

Answer: **K(t) = 100(1-e^{-0.2t}). Reaches 90% at t ≈ 11.5**.

### Example 3 — CS Application: Radioactive Decay for Carbon Dating

Question: Carbon-14 decays with half-life 5730 years. dN/dt = -kN. Find k and determine the age of a sample with 30% of original C-14 remaining.

Step 1: Solution of dN/dt = -kN: N(t) = N₀e^{-kt}.

Step 2: Half-life: N(5730) = N₀/2 → e^{-5730k} = 1/2 → k = ln(2)/5730 ≈ 0.000121.

Step 3: N(t) = 0.3N₀: N₀e^{-kt} = 0.3N₀ → e^{-kt} = 0.3 → t = -ln(0.3)/k ≈ 9953 years.

Answer: **k ≈ 0.000121. Sample is ~9953 years old**.

## Why This Works

The method of separation works because we treat dy/dx as a ratio of differentials. While not fully rigorous without further theory, the technique ∫ g(y) dy = ∫ f(x) dx produces correct results because it reverses the chain rule: if y satisfies the separated equation, then implicit differentiation of the integrated equation gives back the original DE.

Equilibrium solutions correspond to constant functions. If the system starts at equilibrium, it stays there forever (derivative is zero). Stability analysis (whether small perturbations grow or decay) connects to the derivative of the right-hand side f'(y) at the equilibrium — a preview of linearisation.

Euler's method works because the derivative is the instantaneous slope. Over a small step h, the function is approximately linear, so the new value is old value plus slope times step size.

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Separating without checking for g(y)=0 solutions
> When dividing by g(y), you may lose equilibrium solutions where g(y)=0. Always check for these separately.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting the +C from integration
> The general solution requires one constant (from one integration). Apply the initial condition at the end.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Confusing independent and dependent variables
> In dy/dt = ky, y is the dependent variable (what we solve for) and t is independent (the variable we integrate with respect to). Keep track of which is which.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Euler's method with too large a step size
> Large h causes significant error. Euler's method is O(h) — halving h roughly halves the error. Adaptive step methods exist for better accuracy.
