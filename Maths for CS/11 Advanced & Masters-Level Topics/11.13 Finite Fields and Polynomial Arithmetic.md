## Introduction

**Finite fields** (Galois fields) are algebraic structures with finitely many elements, where all four arithmetic operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) are well-defined. They are the foundation of modern cryptography, error-correcting codes, and computer arithmetic.

From [[6.3 Modular Arithmetic]], you understand arithmetic modulo a prime. From [[6.5 Permutations and Combinations]], you have basic counting tools. From [[10.7 Error Correction QR and Reed Solomon]], you have seen finite fields applied in practice.

The core facts:
- A finite field exists iff it has p^k elements for some prime p and integer k ≥ 1.
- GF(p): prime field — integers {0, 1, ..., p−1} with modulo p arithmetic.
- GF(p^k): extension field — polynomials of degree < k over GF(p), modulo an irreducible polynomial of degree k.
- The multiplicative group GF(p^k)\{0} is cyclic (generated by a primitive element).

## New Words

- **[[finite field]]** — GF(p^k): a field with p^k elements (p prime, k ≥ 1); also called a Galois field.
- **[[prime field]]** — GF(p): integers {0, 1, ..., p−1} with addition and multiplication modulo p.
- **[[extension field]]** — GF(p^k): polynomials over GF(p) modulo an irreducible polynomial of degree k.
- **[[irreducible polynomial]]** — a polynomial with no non-constant factors (analogue of a prime number in integer arithmetic).
- **[[polynomial arithmetic]]** — addition, multiplication, and division (with remainder) of polynomials over a field.
- **[[primitive element]]** — a generator of the multiplicative group of a finite field.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Prime Fields GF(p) — Arithmetic Modulo a Prime

**Builds on:** [[6.3 Modular Arithmetic]] (modular arithmetic, inverses)

GF(p) = {0, 1, ..., p−1} with arithmetic modulo p (p prime). Every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse.

#### Chunked Example — Finding Inverses in GF(7)

Question: Find 5⁻¹ in GF(7) (the inverse of 5 modulo 7).

Step 1: We need x such that 5x ≡ 1 (mod 7).

Step 2: Check: 5×1 = 5, 5×2 = 10 ≡ 3, 5×3 = 15 ≡ 1.

Step 3: 5⁻¹ = 3. Verify: 5 × 3 = 15 ≡ 1 (mod 7). ✓

Answer: **5⁻¹ = 3 in GF(7). Every non-zero element has a unique inverse in GF(p).**

#### Chunked Example — Additive and Multiplicative Groups

Question: Describe the additive and multiplicative groups of GF(7).

Step 1: Additive group: (ℤ₇, +) — cyclic of order 7, generator 1.

Step 2: Multiplicative group: (GF(7)\{0}, ×) — cyclic of order 6.

Step 3: Check generator 3: 3¹=3, 3²=2, 3³=6, 3⁴=4, 3⁵=5, 3⁶=1. All non-zero elements are powers of 3.

Answer: **Additive: ℤ₇ (order 7). Multiplicative: cyclic of order 6, generator 3.**

### Breadcrumb 2: Extension Fields GF(p^k) — Polynomials over Finite Fields

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1, [[6.5 Permutations and Combinations]] (counting argument for number of polynomials)

GF(p^k): elements are polynomials of degree < k with coefficients in GF(p), modulo an irreducible polynomial m(x) of degree k. There are p^k such polynomials — hence the notation.

Operations:
- Addition: coefficient-wise mod p (XOR for GF(2)).
- Multiplication: multiply polynomials, then reduce modulo m(x).

#### Chunked Example — Multiplication in GF(2³)

Question: GF(8) with irreducible polynomial m(x) = x³ + x + 1. Multiply a(x) = x + 1 and b(x) = x².

Step 1: a × b = x²(x+1) = x³ + x².

Step 2: Reduce: x³ ≡ x + 1 (mod x³ + x + 1). So x³ + x² ≡ (x + 1) + x² = x² + x + 1.

Answer: **(x+1) × x² = x² + x + 1 in GF(8).**

### Breadcrumb 3: Irreducible Polynomials and Primitive Elements

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 2, [[6.3 Modular Arithmetic]] (prime numbers, analogy between primes and irreducible polynomials)

An **irreducible polynomial** is the polynomial analogue of a prime number — it has no non-constant factors. Irreducible polynomials of degree k exist for every k ≥ 1 over every finite field.

A **primitive element** g generates the full multiplicative group: GF(p^k)\{0} = {g⁰, g¹, g², ..., g^{p^k−2}}.

#### Chunked Example — Checking Irreducibility in GF(2)

Question: Is x³ + x + 1 irreducible over GF(2)?

Step 1: Degree 3. If reducible, it has a linear factor (degree 1). Check if any element of GF(2) is a root.

Step 2: At x=0: 0+0+1 = 1 ≠ 0. At x=1: 1+1+1 = 1 ≠ 0.

Step 3: No linear factor. But could factor as product of irreducible quadratic × linear? No — we already checked no linear factors.

Answer: **x³ + x + 1 is irreducible over GF(2). It generates GF(8).**

## Why This Works

Finite fields satisfy all field axioms (associativity, commutativity, distributivity, additive and multiplicative inverses) within a finite set — a remarkable fact. The existence and uniqueness theorem says:

- For every prime p and integer k ≥ 1, there exists a field with p^k elements.
- Any two fields with p^k elements are isomorphic (the same up to relabelling).

The multiplicative group is cyclic: there exists a primitive element g such that every non-zero element = g^i for some i. This property is essential for cryptography (discrete log) and coding theory (Reed-Solomon codes use primitive elements as evaluation points).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Polynomial GCD Over Finite Fields

Question: Find gcd(x³ + x + 1, x² + 1) over GF(2).

Step 1: Polynomial division: x³ + x + 1 ÷ x² + 1 = x remainder 1 (since x(x²+1)+1 = x³ + x + 1).

Step 2: Next: x² + 1 ÷ 1 = x² + 1 remainder 0.

Step 3: gcd = 1 (coprime — both are irreducible over GF(2)).

Answer: **gcd(x³ + x + 1, x² + 1) = 1 over GF(2).**

### Example 2 — AES SubBytes

Question: How does AES use GF(2⁸)?

Step 1: Each byte is an element of GF(2⁸) with irreducible polynomial x⁸ + x⁴ + x³ + x + 1.

Step 2: SubBytes = multiplicative inverse in GF(2⁸) + affine transformation over GF(2).

Step 3: The inverse provides nonlinearity — critical for AES security. The affine transform breaks the algebraic structure (prevents attacks exploiting the inverse alone).

Answer: **AES SubBytes: byte → (inverse in GF(2⁸)) → affine transform. Provides confusion.**

### Example 3 — Reed-Solomon Error Correction

Question: How do Reed-Solomon codes use finite fields?

Step 1: Message = coefficients of polynomial P(x) of degree k−1 over GF(2^m).

Step 2: Codeword = evaluations P(α₁), P(α₂), ..., P(αₙ) for n distinct αᵢ ∈ GF(2^m).

Step 3: Any k evaluations suffice to recover P via Lagrange interpolation.

Step 4: With n > k evaluations, can detect and correct up to (n−k)/2 errors via Berlekamp-Welch algorithm.

Answer: **Reed-Solomon: polynomial evaluation → codeword. Interpolation → decoding. Corrects (n−k)/2 errors.**

### Example 4 — Secret Sharing (Shamir's Scheme)

Question: Share a secret among n parties such that any t can reconstruct.

Step 1: Secret s ∈ GF(p). Pick random polynomial P(x) of degree t−1 with P(0) = s.

Step 2: Share i: give party i the value P(i) for i = 1, ..., n.

Step 3: Any t parties can interpolate to recover P and compute s = P(0). Fewer than t parties learn nothing.

Answer: **Shamir secret sharing: polynomial interpolation over GF(p). t shares needed for reconstruction.**

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Using composite modulus (non-prime)
> Integers mod n where n is composite is NOT a field — some elements lack multiplicative inverses. It is a ring (ℤₙ). Fields require prime modulus.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Mixing characteristic 2 arithmetic with integers
> In GF(2^k), addition = XOR, not integer addition. a + a = 0 for all a. This is a common source of error in implementations.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting that polynomial coefficients are in GF(p)
> In GF(p^k), coefficients are elements of GF(p). For GF(2^k), coefficients are 0 or 1 only.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Assuming every irreducible polynomial is primitive
> Not all irreducible polynomials are primitive. A primitive polynomial's root generates the full multiplicative group. Some irreducible polynomials have lower-order roots.

## Where This Shows Up in CS

### Cryptography
```text
- AES: GF(2⁸) operations (SubBytes, MixColumns)
- ECC: elliptic curves over GF(p) and GF(2^k)
- Post-quantum crypto: lattice-based (still uses modular arithmetic)
- Secret sharing: Shamir's scheme over GF(p)
- Elliptic curve pairings: over extension fields
```

### Error Correction
```python
# Reed-Solomon over GF(2^8)
import reedsolo
rs = reedsolo.RSCodec(10)  # 10 error correction bytes
encoded = rs.encode(b"hello world")
decoded = rs.decode(encoded)  # corrects up to 5 errors
```

### Computer Arithmetic
```text
- GF(2) arithmetic in hardware: XOR gate
- CRC (Cyclic Redundancy Check): polynomial division over GF(2)
- Montgomery multiplication: modular arithmetic optimised for crypto
```

### Coding Theory
```text
- BCH codes: generalised Reed-Solomon
- LDPC codes: sparse parity check matrices (not field-dependent)
- Quantum error correction: GF(4) for stabilizer codes
```
