## Introduction

**Lattice-based cryptography** is the leading candidate for post-quantum public-key cryptography. Unlike RSA and ECC — both broken by Shor's algorithm — lattice problems have no known efficient quantum algorithm.

From [[11.13 Finite Fields and Polynomial Arithmetic]], you understand finite field arithmetic used in cryptographic implementations. From [[7.3 Matrix Multiplication]], you can work with vector and matrix operations. From [[10.6 Cryptography RSA and Diffie Hellman]], you know the fundamentals of public-key encryption, key exchange, and digital signatures.

Key concepts:
- **Lattice**: a regular grid of points in ℝⁿ, Λ = {∑a_i b_i | a_i ∈ ℤ}.
- **SVP** (Shortest Vector Problem): find the shortest non-zero lattice vector.
- **CVP** (Closest Vector Problem): find the lattice point closest to a given target.
- **LWE** (Learning With Errors): given noisy linear equations b = A·s + e, recover s.
- **Ring-LWE** and **Module-LWE**: structured variants for efficiency.

The NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography standardisation (2024) selected CRYSTALS-Kyber (key encapsulation) and CRYSTALS-Dilithium (signatures) — both lattice-based — as primary standards.

## New Words

- **[[lattice]]** — Λ(B) = {B·a : a ∈ ℤⁿ} where B ∈ ℝ^{m×n} is a basis with linearly independent columns.
- **[[shortest vector problem (SVP)]]** — given a lattice, find the shortest non-zero vector (in Euclidean norm).
- **[[closest vector problem (CVP)]]** — given a lattice and a target vector t, find the lattice point closest to t.
- **[[Learning With Errors (LWE)]]** — recover secret s ∈ ℤ_qⁿ from samples (a_i, b_i = a_i·s + e_i) where e_i are small errors.
- **[[ring-LWE]]** — LWE over polynomial rings R = ℤ_q[x]/(f(x)); more efficient than standard LWE due to structured matrices.
- **[[module-LWE]]** — LWE over modules (vectors of ring elements); used in Kyber and Dilithium; balances efficiency and security.

> [!important] You pass this topic only when you can define all six terms without looking.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Lattice Basics — Grids in High Dimensions

**Builds on:** [[7.3 Matrix Multiplication]] (basis vectors, linear combinations)

A **lattice** Λ(B) is the set of all integer linear combinations of basis vectors b_1, ..., b_n:

```
Λ = {a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + ... + a_n b_n | a_i ∈ ℤ}
```

The **determinant** det(Λ) = |det(B)| is the volume of the fundamental parallelepiped.

#### Chunked Example — Checking Lattice Membership

Question: Basis b₁ = (3, 1), b₂ = (2, 2). Is v = (8, 4) in the lattice?

Step 1: Solve a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ = v: 3a₁ + 2a₂ = 8, a₁ + 2a₂ = 4.

Step 2: Subtract equations: 2a₁ = 4 → a₁ = 2. Then 2 + 2a₂ = 4 → a₂ = 1.

Step 3: Both a₁, a₂ ∈ ℤ. Yes, v = 2b₁ + b₂ ∈ Λ.

Answer: **v ∈ Λ because integer coefficients exist.**

### Breadcrumb 2: Learning With Errors (LWE)

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1, [[11.13 Finite Fields and Polynomial Arithmetic]] (modular arithmetic)

The **LWE problem**: given m samples (a_i, b_i = a_i·s + e_i) where a_i ∈ ℤ_qⁿ, s ∈ ℤ_qⁿ, and e_i are "small" (drawn from a narrow distribution), recover s.

Without errors: trivial (Gaussian elimination). With errors: provably hard (reduction from worst-case lattice problems).

#### Chunked Example — LWE Without Errors

Question: q = 13, n = 2. Samples: (a₁=(2,1), b₁=5), (a₂=(3,5), b₂=7). No errors. Find s.

Step 1: Unknown s = (s₁, s₂). Equations: 2s₁ + s₂ ≡ 5, 3s₁ + 5s₂ ≡ 7 mod 13.

Step 2: From first equation: s₂ = 5 − 2s₁. Substitute into second: 3s₁ + 5(5 − 2s₁) = 25 − 7s₁ ≡ 7 mod 13.

Step 3: −7s₁ ≡ 7 − 25 = −18 ≡ 8 mod 13. s₁ = 8 × (−7⁻¹) mod 13. Since −7 ≡ 6 and 6⁻¹ ≡ 11 (6×11=66≡1): s₁ = 8 × 11 = 88 ≡ 10 mod 13. Then s₂ = 5 − 2×10 = 5 − 20 = −15 ≡ 11.

Step 4: Verify: 2×10+11 = 31 ≡ 5 ✓. 3×10+5×11 = 85 ≡ 7 ✓.

Answer: **Without errors: s = (10, 11). With errors: Gaussian elimination fails — LWE is hard.**

## Why This Works

Lattice-based cryptography relies on worst-case hardness: breaking LWE implies solving approximate SVP in any lattice of dimension n — a problem believed to be intractable even for quantum computers.

The hardness hierarchy:
```
SVP (exact) — NP-hard
    ↓
GapSVP_γ (decision version) — NP-hard for small γ
    ↓
LWE — provably as hard as GapSVP_γ (quantum reduction, Regev 2005)
    ↓
Ring-LWE / Module-LWE — as hard as LWE on structured lattices
    ↓
Practical schemes (Kyber, Dilithium, FALCON)
```

The best classical algorithms (LLL, BKZ) require exponential time in dimension n for cryptographic parameters. No quantum algorithm provides more than a polynomial speedup for these problems.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — One Step of LLL Basis Reduction

Question: Given lattice basis b₁ = (3, 1), b₂ = (2, 2), apply one step of LLL size reduction to obtain a shorter, more orthogonal basis.

Step 1: Compute Gram-Schmidt coefficient μ₂₁ = (b₂·b₁)/(b₁·b₁) = (6 + 2) / (9 + 1) = 8 / 10 = 0.8.

Step 2: Round μ₂₁ to the nearest integer: round(0.8) = 1. Subtract b₂ ← b₂ − 1·b₁.

Step 3: New b₂ = (2 − 3, 2 − 1) = (−1, 1). b₁ unchanged = (3, 1).

Step 4: Verify determinant unchanged: |det([[3, 1], [−1, 1]])| = |3 − (−1)| = 4 = original det([[3, 1], [2, 2]]) = |6 − 2| = 4.

Answer: **Reduced basis: b₁ = (3, 1), b₂ = (−1, 1). Both vectors are shorter; det(Λ) = 4 preserved.**

### Example 2 — LWE: Recovering the Secret With No Errors

Question: Given LWE samples (a₁ = 3, b₁ = 5) and (a₂ = 7, b₂ = 2) with modulus q = 17 and dimension n = 1, recover the secret s ∈ ℤ₁₇ assuming no errors. Do the samples contain errors?

Step 1: Without errors, b = a·s mod q. From the first sample: 3s ≡ 5 mod 17.

Step 2: Compute 3⁻¹ mod 17. Since 3 × 6 = 18 ≡ 1 mod 17, 3⁻¹ = 6. Then s ≡ 5 × 6 = 30 ≡ 13 mod 17.

Step 3: Verify with second sample: a₂·s = 7 × 13 = 91 ≡ 91 − 5×17 = 6 mod 17. But b₂ = 2. Since 6 ≠ 2, the equations are inconsistent — errors must be present.

Step 4: This demonstrates why LWE is hard: even a single error term makes Gaussian elimination impossible.

Answer: **Without errors, s = 13 from first sample. Second sample gives 6 ≠ 2, confirming errors. LWE is intractable because of these small errors.**

### Example 3 — Lattice Determinant and Shortest Vector

Question: Lattice basis b₁ = (2, 0), b₂ = (1, 1). Compute det(Λ) and find the shortest non-zero vector in the lattice.

Step 1: Basis matrix B = [[2, 0], [1, 1]]. det(Λ) = |det(B)| = |2 × 1 − 0 × 1| = 2.

Step 2: The lattice Λ = {a₁(2, 0) + a₂(1, 1) | a₁, a₂ ∈ ℤ} = {(2a₁ + a₂, a₂)}.

Step 3: Short non-zero candidates: (1, 1) = b₂ (length √2), (2, 0) = b₁ (length 2), (1, −1) = b₁ − b₂ (length √2), (0, 2) = −b₁ + 2b₂ (length 2), (0, −2) (length 2).

Step 4: The shortest non-zero vectors are (1, 1) and (1, −1), both with length √2 ≈ 1.414. No shorter non-zero vector exists by Minkowski's bound (√det = √2).

Answer: **det(Λ) = 2. Shortest vectors: (1, 1) and (1, −1) with length √2.**

### Example 4 — CVP via Babai's Rounding

Question: Given lattice basis b₁ = (3, 1), b₂ = (2, 2) and target t = (5, 2), use Babai's rounding approach to find the closest lattice point v and the distance ||t − v||.

Step 1: Solve for coefficients t = c₁b₁ + c₂b₂. Matrix B = [[3, 2], [1, 2]], B⁻¹ = (1/4)[[2, −2], [−1, 3]].

Step 2: Compute [c₁, c₂]ᵀ = B⁻¹tᵀ = (1/4)[[2, −2], [−1, 3]][5, 2]ᵀ = (1/4)[10−4, −5+6] = (1/4)[6, 1] = [1.5, 0.25].

Step 3: Round to nearest integers: a₁ = round(1.5) = 2, a₂ = round(0.25) = 0.

Step 4: Closest lattice point: v = 2·b₁ + 0·b₂ = 2(3, 1) = (6, 2). Distance: ||t − v|| = ||(5, 2) − (6, 2)|| = 1.

Answer: **v = (6, 2) = 2·b₁. Distance = 1.**

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Assuming LWE is the only lattice problem
> SVP, CVP, SIS (Short Integer Solution), LWE, ring-LWE, module-LWE each have different hardness and efficiency trade-offs.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Forgetting that LLL is weak in high dimensions
> LLL gives 2^{n/2} approximation to SVP. BKZ improves to cⁿ with better constant. For cryptographic parameters (n ≥ 512), the best algorithms are exponential.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Confusing ideal lattices (ring-LWE) with standard lattices
> Ring-LWE uses polynomial rings with extra structure. This enables efficient implementation but requires careful parameter selection to avoid attacks exploiting the structure.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Underestimating decryption failure probability
> LWE-based decryption can fail when noise exceeds a threshold. Parameters must ensure failure probability ≤ 2⁻¹²⁸ or better.

## Where This Shows Up in CS

### Post-Quantum Standards (NIST 2024)
```text
- CRYSTALS-Kyber: KEM (key encapsulation mechanism) — primary standard
- CRYSTALS-Dilithium: signature scheme — primary standard
- FALCON: lattice-based signature (compact, complex)
- SPHINCS+: hash-based signature (not lattice, backup)
```

### Implementation
```python
# Kyber via liboqs (Open Quantum Safe)
import oqs
kem = oqs.KeyEncapsulation("Kyber512")
public_key = kem.generate_keypair()
ciphertext, shared_secret_enc = kem.encap_secret(public_key)
shared_secret_dec = kem.decap_secret(ciphertext)
assert shared_secret_enc == shared_secret_dec
```

### Security Level Comparison
```text
| Security  | RSA     | ECC     | Kyber   |
|-----------|---------|---------|---------|
| 128-bit   | 3072    | 256     | Kyber512|
| 192-bit   | 7680    | 384     | Kyber768|
| 256-bit   | 15360   | 512     | Kyber1024|
```

### Other Applications
```text
- Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE): computation on encrypted data
- Verifiable delay functions (VDFs): lattice-based constructions
- Program obfuscation: candidate constructions using multilinear maps
- Privacy-preserving ML: encrypted inference using FHE
```
