## Introduction

**Quantum computing** exploits quantum mechanical phenomena — superposition, entanglement, and interference — to perform computations that may be infeasible for classical computers. The mathematics is entirely linear algebra in complex vector spaces.

From [[7.4 Complex Numbers]], you can work with complex amplitudes and phases. From [[7.5 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors]], you understand how operators act on states. From [[7.9 Singular Value Decomposition]], you can decompose arbitrary linear operators.

The core ideas:
- **Qubit**: a quantum bit is a unit vector in ℂ²: |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩.
- **Superposition**: a qubit can be in a linear combination of |0⟩ and |1⟩ simultaneously.
- **Unitary evolution**: quantum gates are unitary matrices (U†U = I).
- **Measurement**: Born rule — outcome k with probability |⟨k|ψ⟩|².
- **Entanglement**: multi-qubit states that cannot be factored into product states.
- **Quantum algorithms**: Shor (exponential speedup for factoring), Grover (quadratic speedup for search).

## New Words

- **[[qubit]]** — a quantum bit: state α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ ∈ ℂ² with |α|² + |β|² = 1.
- **[[superposition]]** — a linear combination of basis states; the qubit is simultaneously in both states until measured.
- **[[unitary operator]]** — a matrix U such that U†U = UU† = I; all quantum gates are unitary.
- **[[entanglement]]** — a multi-qubit state that cannot be written as a tensor product of individual qubit states; gives rise to non-classical correlations.
- **[[quantum Fourier transform]]** — the unitary transform |j⟩ → (1/√N)∑_{k=0}^{N-1} ω^{jk}|k⟩ where ω = e^{2πi/N}; used in Shor's algorithm for period finding.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: Qubits and the Bloch Sphere

**Builds on:** [[7.4 Complex Numbers]] (complex amplitudes, phases), [[7.5 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors]] (basis states)

A single qubit is a unit vector in ℂ²:

```
|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩,  |α|² + |β|² = 1
```

|0⟩ = [1, 0]ᵀ and |1⟩ = [0, 1]ᵀ form the computational basis. Any qubit state can be written as:

```
|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^{iφ} sin(θ/2)|1⟩
```

This parameterises the **Bloch sphere**: every point on the sphere surface is a pure qubit state.

#### Chunked Example — The |+⟩ State

Question: What is |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 on the Bloch sphere?

Step 1: α = 1/√2, β = 1/√2. |α|² = |β|² = 1/2.

Step 2: This corresponds to θ = π/2, φ = 0: cos(π/4) = sin(π/4) = 1/√2.

Step 3: Measurement: P(|0⟩) = 1/2, P(|1⟩) = 1/2.

Answer: **|+⟩ = equal superposition on the x-axis of the Bloch sphere. 50% chance of measuring 0 or 1.**

### Breadcrumb 2: Quantum Gates as Unitary Matrices

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 1, [[7.9 Singular Value Decomposition]] (unitary matrices)

Quantum gates are unitary matrices: U†U = I. Common single-qubit gates:

```
X = [[0,1],   Z = [[1, 0],    H = (1/√2)[[1, 1],
     [1,0]]        [0,−1]]              [1,−1]]
```

- X (NOT): |0⟩ ↔ |1⟩
- Z (phase flip): |1⟩ → −|1⟩
- H (Hadamard): |0⟩ → |+⟩, |1⟩ → |−⟩

#### Chunked Example — Applying Hadamard

Question: Apply H to |0⟩. What is the resulting state?

Step 1: H|0⟩ = (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]]·[1,0]ᵀ = (1/√2)[1,1]ᵀ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 = |+⟩.

Step 2: Measuring gives |0⟩ with probability 1/2, |1⟩ with probability 1/2.

Step 3: Apply H again: H|+⟩ = H²|0⟩ = |0⟩ (since H² = I).

Answer: **H|0⟩ = |+⟩. H² = I (Hadamard is its own inverse).**

### Breadcrumb 3: Quantum Fourier Transform

**Builds on:** Breadcrumb 2, [[7.4 Complex Numbers]] (roots of unity)

The QFT transforms basis state |j⟩ (j = 0, ..., N−1 where N = 2ⁿ):

```
QFT|j⟩ = (1/√N) ∑_{k=0}^{N-1} e^{2πijk/N} |k⟩
```

Circuit complexity: O(n²) gates. Classical FFT: O(n·2ⁿ) on the full vector.

#### Chunked Example — QFT on 2 Qubits

Question: Apply QFT to |0⟩ ⊗ |0⟩ = |00⟩.

Step 1: N = 4. QFT|00⟩ = (1/2)∑_{k=0}^{3} e^{2πi·0·k/4}|k⟩ = (1/2)(|00⟩ + |01⟩ + |10⟩ + |11⟩).

Step 2: This is the uniform superposition over all 4 basis states.

Step 3: Measuring yields each outcome with equal probability 1/4.

Answer: **QFT on |00⟩: uniform superposition of all 2ⁿ basis states.**

## Why This Works

Quantum computation derives power from three resources:

1. **Superposition**: n qubits represent all 2ⁿ basis states simultaneously — exponential parallelism.
2. **Interference**: amplitudes can cancel (destructive interference) or reinforce (constructive), analogous to wave interference.
3. **Entanglement**: correlations with no classical analogue — measuring one qubit instantly affects the state of entangled partners.

Shor's algorithm combines all three: use superposition to explore all possible periods, QFT to amplify correct period via interference, and measurement to read the result. The exponential speedup for factoring comes from the QFT's exponential advantage over classical FFT.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Deutsch-Jozsa Algorithm

Question: Determine if f: {0,1}ⁿ → {0,1} is constant or balanced with a single query.

Step 1: Start with n+1 qubits: |0⟩^⊗ⁿ|1⟩. Apply H gates to all: (1/√2ⁿ)∑|x⟩ ⊗ (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2.

Step 2: Query f in superposition: |x⟩|y⟩ → |x⟩|y ⊕ f(x)⟩. The (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2 qubit picks up factor (−1)^{f(x)}.

Step 3: State: (1/√2ⁿ)∑(−1)^{f(x)}|x⟩ ⊗ (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2.

Step 4: Apply H^⊗ⁿ to the first register, measure. If constant → probability 1 of |0⟩^⊗ⁿ. If balanced → some other outcome with nonzero prob.

Answer: **Deutsch-Jozsa: 1 query for exponential speedup. Oracle separates constant from balanced functions.**

### Example 2 — Shor's Algorithm (Factoring)

Question: Factor N = 15 using Shor's algorithm.

Step 1: Pick random a = 2 (coprime with 15). Find order r: smallest r such that aʳ ≡ 1 mod 15.

Step 2: r = 4: 2¹=2, 2²=4, 2³=8, 2⁴=16≡1 mod 15.

Step 3: Shor uses QFT to find period r efficiently. Classical step: compute p = gcd(a^{r/2}+1, N) = gcd(5, 15) = 5, q = gcd(a^{r/2}−1, N) = gcd(3, 15) = 3.

Step 4: N = 5 × 3. Factored!

Answer: **Shor: use QFT for period finding → factor N in polynomial time. Breaks RSA and ECC.**

### Example 3 — Grover's Search

Question: Find a marked element in an unsorted database of N items.

Step 1: Uniform superposition: |ψ⟩ = (1/√N)∑|x⟩.

Step 2: Oracle O marks the target state: O|x⟩ = −|x⟩ if x is target, +|x⟩ otherwise.

Step 3: Grover diffusion: D = 2|ψ⟩⟨ψ| − I (reflect about the mean).

Step 4: Each Grover iteration = O followed by D. After O(√N) iterations, target amplitude ≈ 1.

Answer: **Grover: O(√N) vs O(N) classical — quadratic speedup for unstructured search.**

### Example 4 — Quantum Phase Estimation

Question: Given unitary U with eigenvector |ψ⟩ and eigenvalue e^{2πiθ}, estimate θ.

Step 1: Use m ancillary qubits initialised to |0⟩^⊗ᵐ. Apply H gates, then controlled-U²ʲ operations.

Step 2: Inverse QFT on the ancilla register. Measure ancilla → estimate of θ to m bits of precision.

Step 3: This is the subroutine used in Shor (order finding) and quantum chemistry (Hamiltonian simulation).

Answer: **Phase estimation: estimate eigenvalue θ via controlled-U²ʲ + inverse QFT. Uses O(1/ε) gates for precision ε.**

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Thinking quantum computers are faster for everything
> Quantum speedups are known for specific problems: factoring, search, simulation. Most problems have no known significant speedup.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Confusing superposition with probabilistic mixture
> |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 and 50% |0⟩, 50% |1⟩ give the same measurement statistics but different behaviour under unitary operations. A superposition is coherent; a mixture is incoherent.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Underestimating error correction overhead
> Physical qubits are noisy (~10⁻³ error per gate). Fault-tolerant computing requires 10³−10⁶ physical qubits per logical qubit. Current devices have ~10³ physical qubits.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Assuming measurement is harmless
> Measurement collapses the quantum state irreversibly. Quantum algorithms must extract the result while preserving coherence until the final step.

## Where This Shows Up in CS

### Quantum Computing Status (2026)
```text
- ~1000-2000 physical qubits (IBM, Google, Quantinuum)
- Gate error rates: ~10⁻³ (threshold for fault-tolerance: ~10⁻⁶)
- No fully error-corrected logical qubit at scale
- NISQ (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) era
- Hybrid quantum-classical algorithms for near-term
```

```python
# Qiskit: Bell state
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit

# Result: (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2 (Bell state)
```

