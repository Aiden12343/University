## Introduction

**Research methods and mathematical writing** are the capstone skills of a mathematician or computer scientist. Research is not just solving problems — it is communicating what you solved, why it matters, and how you did it. Mathematical writing follows precise conventions: define notation before use, state theorems exactly, prove step-by-step.

From [[10.12 Research Paper Maths Reading]], you know how to extract mathematical content from published papers. From [[5.9 Direct Proof and Counterexample]] and [[5.10 Proof by Contradiction and Contrapositive]], you can construct rigorous arguments. From [[11.17 Information Theory for ML and Compression]], you have deep technical knowledge of an advanced CS topic — the kind of content you will now write about.

The skills covered:
- **Formulating research questions**: specific, measurable, scope-limited.
- **Paper structure**: IMRaD (Introduction, Method, Results, Discussion).
- **Experimental design**: baselines, ablation, statistical significance.
- **Mathematical writing**: definitions, theorem-proof environments, notation.
- **Reproducibility**: code, data, environment, seeds.
- **Reviewing**: constructive evaluation of others' work.

## New Words

- **[[research question]]** — a focused, answerable question that guides the investigation; must be specific, measurable, and scope-limited.
- **[[related work]]** — prior research providing context; identifies what is known and the gap that the current work fills.
- **[[ablation study]]** — an experiment that removes one component of a system to measure its individual contribution.
- **[[statistical significance]]** — a result that is unlikely to have occurred by chance, typically measured via p-value or confidence intervals.
- **[[reproducibility]]** — the ability of others to replicate experimental results given the same code, data, environment, and instructions.

## Breadcrumbs

### Breadcrumb 1: The Research Cycle and Question Formulation

**Builds on:** [[5.9 Direct Proof and Counterexample]] (hypothesis formulation), [[10.12 Research Paper Maths Reading]] (critical reading)

The research cycle: **Question → Literature Review → Hypothesis → Experiment/Proof → Analysis → Write → Review → Revise → Publish**.

A good research question is:
- **Specific**: not "how can we make training faster?" but "can Adam reduce training iterations vs SGD on ImageNet?"
- **Measurable**: metrics (accuracy, runtime), baselines, statistical tests.
- **Scope-limited**: constrained to a specific domain, dataset, or problem class.

#### Chunked Example — From Observation to Question

Question: You observe that neural network training is slow. Formulate a good research question.

Step 1: Bad: "How can we make training faster?" (too vague, not measurable).

Step 2: Better: "Do adaptive learning rates reduce the number of training iterations needed to reach target accuracy in image classification?"

Step 3: Good: "Does Adam require fewer SGD iterations than SGD with momentum to achieve 76% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet with ResNet-50?"

Step 4: Hypothesis: "Adam requires ≤ 70% of the iterations that SGD+momentum needs for target accuracy."

Answer: **Good question: specific comparison (Adam vs SGD), measurable (iterations to 76%), clear scope (ImageNet, ResNet-50).**

### Breadcrumb 2: Paper Structure — IMRaD

**Builds on:** [[10.12 Research Paper Maths Reading]] (reading papers, identifying structure)

The standard structure for CS conference papers:

1. **Abstract**: problem, method, result (150-250 words). Most-read part.
2. **Introduction**: motivation, problem, contributions (numbered list), outline.
3. **Related Work**: contextualise, compare, identify gap (not exhaustive).
4. **Method**: algorithm, maths, pseudocode. Must be self-contained.
5. **Experiments**: datasets, baselines, metrics, ablation, runtime.
6. **Conclusion**: summarise, limitations, future work.

#### Chunked Example — Writing a Theorem

Question: Write a theorem and proof about gradient descent convergence.

Step 1: Define terms: Let f be convex and β-smooth (||∇f(x)−∇f(y)|| ≤ β||x−y||).

Step 2: Theorem: For any x₀, gradient descent with η=1/β satisfies f(x_k)−f(x*) ≤ β||x₀−x*||²/(2k).

Step 3: Proof sketch: f(x_{t+1}) ≤ f(x_t) + ∇f(x_t)ᵀ(x_{t+1}−x_t) + (β/2)||x_{t+1}−x_t||² (smoothness). Substitute x_{t+1} = x_t − (1/β)∇f(x_t) and telescope.

Step 4: Every symbol (f, β, x*, η) must be defined before use.

Answer: **Theorem → definitions → formal statement → structured proof. Every symbol defined before first use.**

## Why This Works

Research is cumulative: each paper builds on prior work. A well-written paper:
- Gets cited (impact on the field).
- Passes peer review (acceptance at conferences).
- Enables replication (contributes to scientific progress).

Mathematical writing is precise because mathematics is precise. Ambiguity in a theorem statement can lead to incorrect applications. Unclear notation can make a proof unreadable.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Writing a Clear Theorem and Proof

Question: Write a formal theorem statement and proof for: "The sum of two even integers is even."

Step 1: Theorem: Let a and b be even integers. Then a + b is even.

Step 2: Proof: Since a is even, a = 2k for some integer k. Since b is even, b = 2ℓ for some integer ℓ. Then a + b = 2k + 2ℓ = 2(k + ℓ), which is even because k + ℓ ∈ ℤ.

Step 3: Every symbol (a, b, k, ℓ) is defined before use. The proof is self-contained, step-by-step, and ends with the conclusion restated.

Answer: **Theorem: ∀a,b ∈ ℤ, (2|a ∧ 2|b) ⇒ 2|(a+b). Proof: a=2k, b=2ℓ ⇒ a+b=2(k+ℓ).**

### Example 2 — Identifying and Fixing Missing Steps in a Proof

Question: The following proof of "If n² is even, then n is even" is incomplete. Identify the gaps and rewrite it correctly.

Given proof: "n² is even, so n² = 2k for some k. Then n = √(2k), so n is even."

Step 1: Gap 1: √(2k) may not be an integer — there is no justification that √(2k) is an integer n.

Step 2: Gap 2: Even if n is an integer, the equation n = √(2k) does not prove n is even (e.g., n² = 18 gives n = √18, not an integer; the argument confuses necessary with sufficient conditions).

Step 3: Correct proof (contrapositive): Suppose n is odd. Then n = 2m + 1 for some m ∈ ℤ. Then n² = (2m+1)² = 4m² + 4m + 1 = 2(2m² + 2m) + 1, which is odd. Therefore, if n² is even, n cannot be odd — so n is even.

Answer: **Original proof is invalid (√(2k) may be non-integer). Correct: contrapositive shows odd ⇒ odd, so even ⇒ even.**

### Example 3 — Writing a Definition, Theorem, and Proof

Question: Write a self-contained definition of "injective function", then state and prove that f(x) = 3x − 1 from ℝ to ℝ is injective.

Step 1: Definition: A function f: A → B is injective (one-to-one) if for all x₁, x₂ ∈ A, f(x₁) = f(x₂) ⇒ x₁ = x₂.

Step 2: Theorem: The function f: ℝ → ℝ defined by f(x) = 3x − 1 is injective.

Step 3: Proof: Let x₁, x₂ ∈ ℝ and suppose f(x₁) = f(x₂). Then 3x₁ − 1 = 3x₂ − 1. Add 1 to both sides: 3x₁ = 3x₂. Divide by 3: x₁ = x₂. Hence f is injective.

Answer: **Definition is stated first. Theorem is explicit. Proof uses the definition directly: f(x₁)=f(x₂) ⇒ x₁=x₂.**

### Example 4 — Rewriting a Poorly Presented Epsilon-Delta Argument

Question: The following epsilon-delta argument is poorly structured. Rewrite it with proper notation, clear steps, and complete justification.

"Limit as x→2 of x² is 4. For epsilon>0 we can find delta. |x²−4|<epsilon when |x−2|<delta. |x−2||x+2|<epsilon. If delta≤1 then |x+2|<5 so pick delta=min(1,epsilon/5)."

Step 1: Missing: no formal definition cited, unclear variable binding, "we can find" is vague, conclusion is absent.

Step 2: Rewrite: Theorem: lim_{x→2} x² = 4. Proof: Let ε > 0. Choose δ = min(1, ε/5). Assume 0 < |x − 2| < δ.

Step 3: Since δ ≤ 1, we have |x − 2| < 1, so |x + 2| = |(x − 2) + 4| ≤ |x − 2| + 4 < 1 + 4 = 5.

Step 4: Then |x² − 4| = |x − 2||x + 2| < δ · 5 ≤ (ε/5) · 5 = ε. Therefore lim_{x→2} x² = 4.

Answer: **Rewrite adds: formal theorem, explicit δ choice, bounded |x+2|, complete inequality chain, concluding statement.**

## Common Mistakes

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: "I know it, so I do not need to write it down"
> Mathematical writing is for the reader. Every non-trivial step needs justification. Every symbol needs definition. Do not assume the reader can fill gaps.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Cherry-picking experimental results
> Report ALL experiments, including failures. Ablation studies show what actually contributes. Selective reporting is misleading and unscientific.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Claiming more than proven
> "We suggest..." is honest. "We prove..." requires proof. "We show empirically..." requires experiments. Do not overclaim — reviewers will notice.

> [!failure] Mistake pattern: Ignoring baselines
> A new method must compare to the current state of the art. Fair implementation of baselines is one of the hardest and most important parts of research.

## Where This Shows Up in CS

### Publishing Pipeline
```text
1. arXiv preprint → establish priority
2. Conference submission → NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, CVPR, ACL, etc.
3. Review process → 3-4 reviewers, meta-reviewer, area chair
4. Rebuttal → respond to reviews (limited length, 1 page)
5. Camera-ready → incorporate feedback
6. Presentation → talk or poster at conference
```
