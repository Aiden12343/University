## Example 1 - Classifying Lines

Question: Identify each figure as a [[line]], [[line segment]], or [[ray]].

```text
(a) <------------------------------>
(b) X------------------------------Y
(c) P------------------------------>
(d) <------------------------------Q
```

### Step 1 - Check each figure for endpoints and direction arrows

(a) Arrows on both ends → extends infinitely in both directions → [[line]].

(b) Endpoints X and Y, no arrows → finite length with start and end → [[line segment]].

(c) Endpoint at P, arrow at the other end → starts at P, extends infinitely → [[ray]].

(d) Arrow at the left end, endpoint Q at the right → extends infinitely from the left, ends at Q → [[ray]] (pointing leftward).

### Step 2 - Write the answer

```text
(a) Line     (b) Line segment     (c) Ray     (d) Ray
```

Answer: (a) line, (b) line segment, (c) ray, (d) ray.

> [!important] Direction does not change type
> A ray pointing left is still a ray. Only the number of endpoints matters.

## Example 2 - Angle Types

Question: Classify each angle measure by type: 15°, 91°, 180°, 89°, 200°, 90°.

### Step 1 - Recall the type boundaries

```text
Acute:     0° < angle < 90°
Right:     angle = 90°
Obtuse:    90° < angle < 180°
Straight:  angle = 180°
Reflex:    180° < angle < 360°
```

### Step 2 - Compare each measure to the boundaries

```text
15°  - between 0 and 90          → acute
91°  - between 90 and 180        → obtuse
180° - exactly 180               → straight
89°  - between 0 and 90          → acute
200° - between 180 and 360       → reflex
90°  - exactly 90                → right
```

Answer: 15° acute, 91° obtuse, 180° straight, 89° acute, 200° reflex, 90° right.

## Example 3 - Parallel Line Angles

Question: Two [[parallel]] lines are cut by a [[transversal]]. One angle is 117°. Find all eight angles.

```text
              t
              |
    l --------+------------------------>
              |  117°  b
              |  d     c
    m --------+------------------------>
              |  e     f
              |  h     g
              |
```

### Step 1 - Identify the relationship of 117° to other angles

Angle 117° and angle b are adjacent on a straight line. From [[1.3 Addition and Subtraction]], they sum to 180°.

b = 180° - 117° = 63°

### Step 2 - Use corresponding angles

Corresponding angles are in the same position at each intersection.

```text
e corresponds to 117° (top-left on m).  e = 117°
f corresponds to b (top-right on m).    f = 63°
```

### Step 3 - Use alternate angles

Alternate angles form a Z shape between the parallels.

```text
c is alternate to 117°.  c = 117°
d is alternate to b.     d = 63°
```

### Step 4 - Use corresponding angles again (or vertical opposites)

```text
g corresponds to c.      g = 117°
h corresponds to d.      h = 63°
```

### Step 5 - Verify

Check co-interior pairs sum to 180°: d + e = 63° + 117° = 180°, c + f = 117° + 63° = 180°.

Answer: 117°, 63°, 117°, 63°, 117°, 63°, 117°, 63° (clockwise from the given angle).

## Example 4 - Triangle Angle Sum and Classification

Question: A triangle has angles 35° and 85°. Find the third angle, then classify the triangle by angles.

### Step 1 - Recall the [[angle sum]] of a [[triangle]]

From Concept 4: the three interior angles always sum to 180°.

### Step 2 - Add the known angles

From [[1.3 Addition and Subtraction]]:
35° + 85° = 120°

### Step 3 - Subtract from 180°

180° - 120° = 60°

The third angle is 60°.

### Step 4 - Classify by angles

```text
All three angles are less than 90°:
35° < 90°, 85° < 90°, 60° < 90°
```

Answer: The third angle is **60°**. The triangle is an **acute triangle** (all angles < 90°).

## Example 5 - Spot the Mistake

Question: A student writes:

> "A triangle has angles 70°, 50°, and 50°. I checked: 70 + 50 + 50 = 170. That is close to 180, so it is probably correct."

### Step 1 - Identify the error

The student is treating the [[angle sum]] as approximate. The rule is exact: the sum must be **exactly** 180°, not approximately 180°.

### Step 2 - Compute the correct sum

70° + 50° + 50° = 170°, not 180°.

### Step 3 - Determine what is wrong

At least one of the given angles is incorrect. The sum is 10° short of 180°.

### Step 4 - Suggest a fix

If two angles are definitely 70° and 50°, the third must be 180° - 70° - 50° = 60°, not 50°.

```text
Correct set: 70°, 50°, 60°   (sum = 180°)
```

Answer: The student mistakenly accepted 170° as close enough. The [[angle sum]] rule is exact: every triangle sums to exactly 180°. The correct third angle is 60°.

> [!failure] Common wrong answer
> Some students write 180° - 70° = 110°, then subtract 50° to get 60° but forget to check that 110° is the remaining sum after removing 70°, not the answer itself. Always do: 180 - (sum of given angles).

## Example 6 - CS Transfer: Ray-Triangle Intersection

Question: In a 3D game engine, a ray is cast from the camera position `(0, 0, 0)` in direction `(0, 0, -1)`. A triangle has vertices at `(-1, -1, -5)`, `(1, -1, -5)`, and `(0, 1, -5)`. Does the ray hit the triangle?

### Step 1 - Understand the geometry

A [[ray]] from Concept 1 starts at the camera and goes in a fixed direction. The triangle lies in a plane at z = -5 (all vertices have z = -5). The ray travels along the z-axis in the negative direction.

### Step 2 - Find the intersection point with the plane

The ray goes from (0, 0, 0) to (0, 0, -1) to (0, 0, -2) and so on. At z = -5, the ray position is (0, 0, -5).

### Step 3 - Check if the point (0, 0, -5) is inside the triangle

The triangle vertices are at z = -5. Ignoring the z-coordinate (all the same), we have a 2D triangle in the xy-plane:

```text
Vertices: (-1, -1), (1, -1), (0, 1)
```

The point (0, 0) is inside this triangle.

We can check using [[angle]] sums from Concept 2: draw lines from (0, 0) to each vertex and check the angles sum to 360° (full turn). Or use barycentric coordinates, which is what GPUs do internally.

### Step 4 - Conclude

The ray intersects the triangle at (0, 0, -5). This is how ray-triangle intersection works in every 3D renderer — the fundamental operation of [[10.4 Computer Graphics and Transformations]].

Answer: Yes, the ray hits the triangle at point (0, 0, -5).
