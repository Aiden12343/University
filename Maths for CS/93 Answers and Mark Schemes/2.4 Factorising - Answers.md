## Practice Answers

### A. Vocabulary
1. A common factor divides exactly into every term of an expression.
2. A factor pair is two expressions (or numbers) that multiply together to give a product.
3. Quadratic factorisation means writing a quadratic expression as the product of two linear brackets.
4. `a² - b² = (a + b)(a - b)`
5. Expanding the brackets confirms the factorisation matches the original expression.
6. Yes. `6x = 3 × 2x` and `9 = 3 × 3`, so 3 divides both terms.

### E. Spot the Mistake
26. Correct: **4(2x + 3)**. Divide each term by 4, do not leave the original inside.
27. Correct: **(x + 3)(x + 4)**. Check: `2 + 5 = 7` ✓, but `2 × 5 = 10 ≠ 12`. Use `3 + 4 = 7` and `3 × 4 = 12`.
28. Correct: **(x + 3)(x - 3)**. Difference of squares needs one plus and one minus bracket.
29. Correct: **3(2x + 3)**. Divide each term by 3.

### F. Mixed and CS Transfer
30. **3(x + y + z)**
31. Expand: `x² + 2x + 5x + 10 = x² + 7x + 10`. ✓ Yes.
32. **a(b + c + d)**
33. Factorised form reveals repeated subexpressions that can be computed once and reused.
34. RSA encryption relies on the difficulty of factoring large numbers into primes.
35. A good answer mentions simplification, common subexpression elimination, symbolic algebra, or cryptography.

Common wrong answers:
- `(x + 1)(x + 6)` for `x² + 5x + 6` — factor pair adds to 7, not 5
- `x² + 9 = (x + 3)(x + 3)` — wrong pattern; sum of squares does not factorise over integers
- `8x + 12 = 4(8x + 12)` — did not divide terms by the common factor
